ABSTRACT
Modeling human reactions to spatial audio requires understanding both what is
heard and where it comes from. Prior work generates motion offline from com-
plete acoustic and spatial input sequences. Humans, however, react to sounds
without knowing what they will hear next.
We present Sonic-AMG, to our
knowledge the first framework for streaming human motion generation from spa-
tial audio.
The model encodes acoustic content and body-relative source ge-
ometry as separate tokens. Conditioned on these tokens, a causal transformer
with a flow head generates continuous motion latents, from which a causal de-
coder reconstructs motion segments.
Standard teacher-forced training derives
motion histories and body-relative source locations from ground-truth motion,
whereas streaming inference relies on generated motion for both, resulting in
a training–inference mismatch.
To reduce this history discrepancy, we intro-
duce rehearsal training, which combines full-sequence teacher-forced supervision
with supervised prediction along short autoregressive self-rollouts. To keep spa-
tial conditioning consistent with generated motion, we propose spatial feedback,
which recomputes body-relative source locations from generated root poses dur-
ing both rehearsal training and inference. On SAM, Sonic-AMG achieves state-
of-the-art FID and R-precision. It further supports real-time online generation
of coherent reactions across sound events and source locations. Project page:
https://jingchengni.com/sonic-amg/.

INTRODUCTION
Sound provides information about events beyond the visual field, making it an important cue for
embodied interaction (Chen et al., 2020; 2021). Human reactions to sound depend on both its
content and the source’s location relative to the body. For example, hearing a voice from behind
may prompt a listener to turn toward the speaker before the utterance ends and adjust the movement
as new acoustic information arrives. As the listener turns, the source’s direction relative to the body
changes. Generating such reactions therefore requires responding as audio arrives and updating
spatial conditions as the body moves.
Recent work (Xu et al., 2025) synthesizes complete motion sequences within predefined temporal
windows, conditioned on fully observed acoustic and spatial inputs. Requiring the full input window
prevents it from responding causally to an incoming audio stream. Autoregressive motion models
provide a basis for streaming generation by predicting successive motion segments from preceding
movements (Shi et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2025). Extending autoregressive learning to spatial audio
requires representations that capture both acoustic content and spatial cues. Furthermore, stream-
ing generation requires each motion prediction to incorporate the acoustic and spatial information
currently available as the input evolves.

We treat acoustic content and spatial cues as distinct modalities of spatial-audio conditioning. We
hypothesize that early fusion introduces interference between them. Sonic-AMG therefore encodes
them as dedicated what and where tokens, respectively, and learns their interactions within the
causal transformer. With sound source geometry supplied separately, the acoustic branch can fo-
cus on sound content rather than a comprehensive description of the binaural signal. For instance,
simple log-mel features in our model yield better results than MOSPA’s 2,272-dimensional audio
features (Xu et al., 2025). To design the where token, we examine perceptual research to understand
how body-relative SSL influences human reactions. We develop an 11-dimensional spatial event
representation inspired by studies of nearby auditory space (Graziano et al., 1999), sound-source
motion (Warren et al., 2002), and looming (Neuhoff, 1998).
With acoustic and spatial representations in place, learning the causal generator further requires
addressing two sources of training–inference mismatch. The first concerns motion history: teacher-
forced training uses ground-truth histories, whereas autoregressive inference relies on the model’s
own predictions. To reduce this mismatch, two-forward training replaces selected ground-truth in-
puts with predictions from a preliminary teacher-forced pass (Mihaylova & Martins, 2019; Xiao
et al., 2025). However, these replacements are still predicted under ground-truth histories, where
later replacements do not depend on earlier sampled outputs. We introduce rehearsal training, which
combines teacher-forced supervision with supervised prediction on short histories generated autore-
gressively by the model. Training on self-rollouts improves robustness to accumulated prediction
errors, while teacher-forced supervision provides a stable learning signal across the full sequence.
The second mismatch arises in spatial conditioning. While acoustic content is directly supplied by
the incoming audio stream, body-relative SSL depends on the root position and orientation. MO-
SPA uses complete SSL sequences precomputed from recorded motion, which encode information
2


about future ground-truth root motion. Such conditioning is incompatible with causal streaming
generation, which requires SSL to be updated from the generated root. Training with SSL derived
from ground-truth motion therefore creates a mismatch with the spatial conditions encountered at
inference. Our experiments show that this mismatch substantially degrades generation quality. We
therefore introduce spatial feedback, which recomputes body-relative source coordinates after each
generated segment during both rehearsal training and inference. The updated coordinates condition
the next prediction, keeping spatial conditions consistent with the generated motion history.
On SAM (Xu et al., 2025), Sonic-AMG achieves state-of-the-art FID and R-precision. It further
supports causal and online motion generation from streaming spatial audio in real time. Although
trained on individual audio–motion clips, Sonic-AMG can generate coherent motion across succes-
sive sound events with varying content and source locations (Fig. 1), demonstrating robust streaming
generation. Our contributions are:
• We present Sonic-AMG, to our knowledge the first framework for causal human motion genera-
tion from streaming spatial audio, with dedicated what and where tokens for acoustic content and
spatial cues.
• We introduce rehearsal training to address the history mismatch between training and inference,
combining teacher-forced supervision with supervised prediction from histories constructed through
short autoregressive self-rollouts.
• We propose spatial feedback to address the mismatch in spatial conditioning, updating body-
relative source coordinates from generated motion during both rehearsal training and inference.

RELATED WORK
Audio-conditioned motion generation. Music-driven dance methods use autoregressive models to
align motion with musical structure, either in pose space or through learned choreographic codes (Li
et al., 2021; Siyao et al., 2022). Diffusion-based approaches support diverse choreography, motion
editing, and hierarchical synthesis of long dance sequences (Alexanderson et al., 2023; Tseng et al.,
2023; Luo et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024a). Diffusion-based visual generation and prediction benefit
from complementary conditioning modalities (Zhang et al., 2023b; Mou et al., 2024; Zeng et al.,
2026). Co-speech gesture models combine acoustic, textual, and speaker or style information to
generate gestures that match speech content and rhythm (Yoon et al., 2020; Ao et al., 2023). Dif-
fusion models and learned motion priors capture gesture diversity while coordinating body-part
movements (Zhu et al., 2023; Ao et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024b). Streaming sys-
tems extend these tasks to incoming music and speech (Saleem et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Vu
et al., 2026; Ji et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). MOSPA (Xu et al., 2025) introduces the SAM dataset
of human reactions to spatial audio and a diffusion model conditioned on binaural audio, source
coordinates, and reaction style. Its full-sequence conditioning requires future observations, preclud-
ing causal reactions to an incoming audio stream. MoSAT (Xu et al., 2026) conditions a latent
flow-matching model on text, acoustic features, and an initial body-relative sound-source direction.
Streaming generation remains future work. Sonic-AMG enables real-time causal motion generation
from incoming spatial audio, with spatial feedback updating body-relative source geometry.
Causal motion generation. Autoregressive (AR) models use next-token prediction for language
modeling (Brown et al., 2020) and image synthesis with discrete visual tokens (Esser et al., 2021).
Continuous latent representations support diffusion-based and flow-based generation (Li et al.,
2024b; Ren et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2026; Du et al., 2026). Autoregressive approaches pair a trans-
former with a conditional diffusion or flow head, including in multimodal language models (Sun
et al., 2026). In motion synthesis, discrete-token models predict quantized motion codes (Zhang
et al., 2023a; Jiang et al., 2023), whereas AR diffusion models sample successive frames or motion
primitives (Shi et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025; Tevet et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhao et al.,
2026). Streaming generation in latent space requires decoding segments without future latents. Sys-
tems combining causal decoding with transformer-conditioned generative heads support online text
control, two-character interaction, and co-speech gestures (Xiao et al., 2025; Cen et al., 2025; Li
et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). Causal decoding alone does not resolve exposure bias: teacher forcing
uses recorded histories, whereas inference relies on model predictions. Diffusion forcing exposes
models to contexts at varying noise levels (Chen et al., 2024a; Cai et al., 2026; Ji et al., 2026), while

METHOD
Given an incoming binaural audio stream A and a known world-space sound-source position, Sonic-
AMG generates motion autoregressively from an initial motion segment, without access to future
audio or motion (Fig. 2). We represent motion with continuous latents and encode acoustic con-
tent and body-relative source geometry as separate conditioning tokens (Sec. 3.1). A causal trans-
former conditions a flow head to generate the next motion latent from these tokens and the motion
history (Sec. 3.2). We train the generator using rehearsal training, which combines full-sequence
teacher-forced supervision with supervised prediction under histories constructed through short au-
toregressive self-rollouts (Sec. 3.3). We further apply spatial feedback to update body-relative SSL
from the generated root pose during both rehearsal training and inference, keeping spatial conditions
consistent with the generated motion history (Sec. 3.4).
3.1
MOTION AND CONDITIONING REPRESENTATIONS
Motion representation.
We represent motion at 30 Hz. Each frame xt ∈R300 comprises the
global 3D positions, local rotations in a 6D representation, and 3D velocities of 25 joints (Xu et al.,
2025). We adopt MotionStreamer’s causal temporal VAE (Xiao et al., 2025) and pretrain it on
SAM (Xu et al., 2025). The VAE encodes motion into a sequence of K latents zk ∈Rdz with
dz = 16 at a temporal stride of r = 4. Each latent zk corresponds to a four-frame motion block
Ik = {rk, . . . , rk + r −1}. The encoder and decoder remain frozen during generator training.
For spatial audio, we regard acoustic content and source geometry as two distinct conditioning
modalities. We hypothesize that the two modalities can act as noise for one another when fused into
a single conditioning representation. We therefore encode them as separate tokens.

Semantic branch: what.
The semantic branch in Figure 2(2) learns acoustic features for mo-
tion prediction from the incoming binaural stream. We represent each ear’s spectral content with
80-dimensional log-mel features and process them with a shared causal temporal CNN. The shared
encoder maps both ears into a common feature space, where we average the two streams to em-
phasize sound content shared across ears. A two-layer causal temporal CNN then aggregates recent
acoustic context, followed by layer normalization and a linear projection. Mean pooling over each
four-frame block produces fk at the motion latent rate, so that each acoustic token aligns with a mo-
tion block. We obtain the acoustic token as ak = Wafk +ec, where Wa is a learned linear projection
and ec is a learned embedding indexed by SAM’s annotated reaction-style label c.

3.3
REHEARSAL TRAINING
In two-forward training (Mihaylova & Martins, 2019; Xiao et al., 2025), an initial teacher-forced
pass estimates motion latents from GT histories. Selected estimates then replace GT motion inputs
for a second supervised pass. Although this exposes the model to predicted inputs, those inputs are
not generated recursively from earlier predictions, as they would be during autoregressive inference.
Rehearsal training instead constructs motion histories through short autoregressive self-rollouts.
Each sampled latent extends the history used for the next prediction. We combine next-latent su-
pervision under these recursively generated histories with full-sequence teacher-forced supervision.

EXPERIMENTS
Dataset. We evaluate on SAM (Xu et al., 2025), which pairs spatial audio with human reactions.
Autoregressive models receive four initial ground-truth motion frames and the reaction style.
Spatial conditioning inputs.
We consider two spatial-conditioning protocols: GT uses body-
relative SSL precomputed from recorded motion, whereas Online recomputes SSL from generated
root poses during inference. GT SSL relies on ground-truth root information that is unavailable
during causal streaming generation. We include a GT SSL variant to enable fair comparison with
MOSPA and other baselines under their established evaluation protocol. Online evaluation is more
challenging, as inaccuracies in generated root poses perturb the SSL used to condition subsequent
predictions, introducing an additional pathway for error propagation. All qualitative results of Sonic-
AMG shown in the experiments are generated under the Online protocol.
Following the established SAM evaluation protocol, SSL inputs are available to all methods in our
main comparisons. Such inputs can be supplied by dataset annotations, an upstream localization
module (Krause et al., 2024a;b), or virtual-scene metadata. Our modular conditioning design also
supports a No-SSL variant that derives spatial tokens directly from binaural audio, without requiring
SSL inputs at inference, detailed in Appendix D.
Evaluation metrics. Following MOSPA (Xu et al., 2025), we report Fr´echet Inception Distance
(FID), R-precision (R@1–3), and feature-space diversity. Lower FID indicates better distributional
fidelity, while higher R-precision indicates better conditioning alignment. Diversity closer to real
motion is preferred. We report 95% confidence intervals. Bold and underlined values mark the best
and second-best results within each comparison.
4.1
COMPARISON WITH EXISTING METHODS
Quantitative comparison. Sonic-AMG supports real-time streaming generation (Section 4.2) while
achieving the best distributional fidelity and conditioning alignment among the compared methods
under the GT SSL protocol (Table 2). Under the Online protocol, Sonic-AMG maintains strong
distributional fidelity and conditioning alignment while updating spatial conditions from its own
generated motion. We attribute these results to the complementary roles of our separate condi-
tioning representations and rehearsal training. Dedicated conditioning tokens allow each motion
prediction to distinguish acoustic content from body-relative source geometry, supporting reactions
that account for both cues. Rehearsal training combines sequence-wide supervision with local au-
toregressive consistency. Teacher forcing provides supervision across complete motion sequences,
supporting motion fidelity and long-range temporal structure, while short self-rollouts expose the
model to the temporal consequences of its own predictions. The joint objective directly targets
the recursive prediction process required for streaming generation, which is absent from MOSPA’s
full-sequence training. The ablations in Sections 4.3 and 4.5 support the contributions of the repre-
sentation and training.

under the Online protocol (Fig. 3). It covers the ears in response to crowd noise and combines

STREAMING EFFICIENCY AND LATENCY
Real-time computation. Sonic-AMG sustains real-time Online generation on both RTX A6000
and TITAN RTX GPUs (Table 3). Each autoregressive step produces four motion frames at 30 Hz,
allowing 133.3 ms of computation per block. The 95th-percentile step time remains below this
budget on both GPUs, including motion decoding and spatial feedback. MOSPA achieves an RTF
below one on RTX A6000, but requires the complete audio window before generation and therefore
does not support causal streaming. On TITAN RTX, its RTF exceeds one, while Sonic-AMG retains
real-time throughput.

Role of teacher-forced supervision. Self-rollouts are most effective when combined with full-
sequence teacher-forced supervision. Removing this supervision degrades fidelity and alignment.
Restoring the motion and conditioning prefix in Rollout Only with History (Appendix F) improves
performance but does not recover rehearsal’s fidelity or top-rank retrieval under either protocol.
Additional context therefore cannot fully replace supervision over complete sequences.
4.4
EFFECT OF SPATIAL FEEDBACK
Adapting to generated motion histories alone is insufficient for Online generation. Table 5 com-
pares two rehearsal-trained models. For the GT-trained model, switching evaluation from GT to
Online SSL with fixed generation settings substantially degrades motion fidelity and conditioning
alignment. During training, it encounters prediction errors in its motion history but not the result-
ing changes in spatial conditions. Training with spatial feedback substantially improves fidelity and
alignment under the same Online protocol. The comparison supports updating body-relative SSL
during rehearsal, so that motion histories and spatial conditions reflect the same generated trajectory.
4.5
ACOUSTIC AND SPATIAL REPRESENTATION
Separate spatial tokens. All representation ablations use Online SSL. Fused linearly projects con-
catenated acoustic and spatial embeddings into a single conditioning token. Separate tokens improve
fidelity and alignment (Table 6). Preserving distinct inputs allows the transformer to attend to acous-
tic and spatial cues separately.
Learning reaction-relevant acoustic features. MOSPA aggregates diverse audio descriptors into
a 2,272-dimensional feature vector. Our learned log-mel features achieve better fidelity and con-
ditioning alignment (Table 6). This improvement is consistent with our hypothesis that redundant
or task-irrelevant acoustic variation can interfere with condition modeling. Separating what from
where helps the model associate acoustic events with spatially appropriate responses.
5
CONCLUSION
We presented Sonic-AMG for real-time autoregressive motion generation from streaming spatial
audio, with separate acoustic and spatial representations. Rehearsal training combines full-sequence
teacher-forced supervision with short autoregressive self-rollouts, while spatial feedback keeps
source coordinates consistent with generated motion. Sonic-AMG achieves state-of-the-art FID and
R-precision on SAM and generates coherent reactions across sound events and source locations.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Our main experiments assume known world-space source positions during inference. Our No-SSL
variant (Appendix D) provides an initial demonstration that spatial conditioning can be derived
from binaural audio. A systematic evaluation of alternative spatial inference methods and their
effects on motion generation remains future work. Our evaluation is limited to SAM, so general-
ization to broader acoustic environments and human reactions remains to be assessed. Evidence for
long-horizon coherence and event transitions is primarily qualitative. Future work should establish
quantitative benchmarks for evaluating motion quality over longer audio streams with more diverse
event transitions.

STREAMING TIMING PROTOCOLS
This appendix details the computation measurements and initial response estimates reported in Sec-
tion 4.2 and Table 3.
Timing protocol.
We measure one 700-step run per GPU and spatial protocol, discarding the first
20 steps as warm-up. Generation starts from a zero motion latent. The run measures computation
over 93.3 s of additional motion. The KV cache retains the full history within a configured context of
4,096 token positions. We use FP32 tensors with TF32 enabled where supported and synchronize the
GPU around each wall-clock measurement. The GT and Online runs use the GT-SSL and feedback-
trained rehearsal models, respectively.
Measured operations.
At batch size one, each step includes both guidance branches, ten Euler
sampling steps, and the persistent KV-cache update. GT spatial tokens are precomputed. Online
timing additionally includes decoding the generated latent prefix and computing spatial tokens from
its last two poses; quality evaluation uses four-frame pooled coordinates (Section 3.4). We time the
motion-generation stage from precomputed acoustic tokens, excluding audio preprocessing and en-
coding, initialization, rendering, and output delivery. The acoustic frontend and feature-availability
delay are specified in Appendix E.
Per-step computation.
Online feedback increases mean step time from 41.2 to 50.2 ms on RTX
A6000 and from 102.5 to 118.0 ms on TITAN RTX. The corresponding online real-time factors are
0.376 and 0.885, with RTF = tstep/(4/30 s), where tstep denotes mean per-step computation time.
MOSPA timing protocol.
MOSPA jointly denoises a complete 240-frame sequence using the
full audio window (Xu et al., 2025), so its generation protocol does not support causal blockwise
streaming. We time its released sampler on the same two GPUs: batch size one, the published 1,000-
step denoising schedule, and five timed blocks after one warm-up block, with the GPU synchronized
around each measurement. Because a block is emitted in a single call, MOSPA has no per-step time
to report, and the per-step columns of Table 3 are left blank. The real-time factor is defined for both
methods as computation time divided by the duration of motion produced, so that column is directly
comparable.
Throughput calculation.
One 240-frame block spans 8.0 s of motion and takes 2,857 ms on the
RTX A6000 and 11,415 ms on the TITAN RTX, giving real-time factors of 0.357 and 1.427. Our
online configuration reaches 0.376 and 0.885 on the same hardware. RTF measures computational
throughput, independently of the input waiting time required before generation.
The gap widens with weaker hardware. Moving from the RTX A6000 to the TITAN RTX multiplies
MOSPA’s block time by 3.99 and our online step time by 2.35. Per second of generated motion,
MOSPA evaluates its network 125 times over the full 240-token sequence, whereas our model takes
7.5 autoregressive steps, each extending the KV cache by three tokens and invoking the rectified-
flow head for ten Euler steps.
Initial response latency calculation.
With the initial motion segment supplied, the acoustic token
for the first new block becomes available 142.7 ms after audio onset (Appendix E). Adding the
measured mean online generation time of 50.2 ms gives an estimated response latency of 192.9 ms
on the RTX A6000. For MOSPA’s 8.0 s input window, the corresponding estimate is 10,857 ms,
including 2,857 ms of generation. Both estimates combine input waiting with mean generation time,
19


using steady-state computation measurements. Acoustic preprocessing and encoding, initialization,
rendering, and output delivery are excluded.

IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
Dataset.
SAM (Xu et al., 2025) contains binaural recordings paired with human reactions across
27 sound scenarios and three reaction styles.
Architecture and optimization.
The generator uses a 12-layer, 12-head transformer of width
d = 768, with rotary positional embeddings and query–key normalization. Motion latents have di-
mension dz = 16. The conditional flow head contains nine residual MLP blocks of width 1792 with
adaptive layer normalization. We optimize the generator and conditioning modules with AdamW,
using batch size 64, learning rate 10−5, weight decay 10−6, and momentum coefficients (0.9, 0.95).
Each supervised transition uses four independently sampled noise–time pairs. Padded prediction
positions are excluded from all losses.
Training hardware and time.
For the H = 16 configuration, we train the online rehearsal model
from scratch for 150,000 updates on a single NVIDIA L40S GPU, taking approximately 63 hours.
The 130,000-update checkpoint is reached after approximately 54 hours. These wall-clock times
include periodic validation and checkpointing, but exclude motion-autoencoder pretraining.
Causal motion autoencoder.
The temporal autoencoder is pretrained using a Gaussian recon-
struction objective, KL regularization, and an additional root-position reconstruction term. The en-
coder and decoder are then fixed during generator training. Motion channels are standardized using
training-set statistics; decoded channels are converted back to physical coordinates before source
geometry is computed. The initial GT latent encodes four motion frames.
Acoustic encoding and feature availability.
Audio is sampled at 48 kHz. Each channel is con-
verted to 80-dimensional log-mel features using a 2048-sample Hann window, hop 1600, and non-
centered windows. The shared per-ear causal convolutional stack has 64 channels and dilation rates
(1, 2, 4); the subsequent two-layer causal CNN has 128 channels and dilations (1, 2). Both use ker-
nel size three and left padding. A layer-normalized projection, four-frame mean pooling, and a final
linear projection produce acoustic tokens of width 768.
