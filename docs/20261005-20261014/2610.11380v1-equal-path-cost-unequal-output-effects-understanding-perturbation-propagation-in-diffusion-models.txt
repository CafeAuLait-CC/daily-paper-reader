Diffusion models have achieved remarkable success in generative modeling, with their sampling procedures routinely modified to control generation and improve efficiency. These modifications introduce perturbations along the sampling trajectory, raising a central question: how do such perturbations affect generated output? To address this question, we develop a theoretical framework to investigate perturbation propagation, combining dynamical analysis of the sampling process with an information-theoretic characterization of output responses. Within this framework, we quantify perturbation strength using the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between perturbed and reference trajectory distributions, termed as path cost , which is shown to bound, but do not determine, changes in the output distribution. Building on this analysis, we derive a response identity that connects the propagation and accumulation of local perturbations with the information captured by a selected feature mean, explaining why changes in the output distribution can remain undetected by its first-order response. We test our theoretical analysis through controlled interventions at equal path cost in pretrained diffusion models, revealing distinct patterns of output sensitivity across sampling stages and spatial frequencies. To assess whether our framework can diagnose perturbations arising from practical approximations, we apply it to cache-based acceleration and show that our propagation analysis reliably identifies sampling intervals where caching causes larger image errors. 

# 1 Introduction 

Diffusion models generate samples by simulating learned reverse-time dynamics from a noise distribution to the data distribution (Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2020). During inference, these dynamics are perturbed by errors in learned score estimates or denoising predictions (Song et al., 2021; Li and van der Schaar, 2024), by deliberate guidance updates (Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021), and by approximations introduced by fast solvers, quantization, and feature caching (Lu et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024). Because a perturbation changes the input to subsequent denoising steps, its effect on the generated sample depends on the downstream sampling dynamics, not only on its magnitude at the timestep where it occurs (Li and van der Schaar, 2024; Liu et al., 2026). This creates a mismatch between the trajectory-level quantities used to characterize a perturbation and the output-level quantities used to evaluate its consequences: the former describe the change where it enters the sampling process, whereas the latter concern the endpoint distribution, the response of a selected feature, or the displacement of a generated sample. This mismatch raises a central question: How do perturbations along the sampling trajectory translate into changes in generated outputs, and to what extent can trajectory-level measures characterize those changes? 

Existing work has addressed different aspects of this question. Analyses of score estimation and discretization use aggregated errors along the sampling trajectory to bound deviations in the generated distribution (Song 1arXiv:2610.11380v1 [cs.AI] 8 Oct 2026 et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022; Benton et al., 2024). Studies of exposure bias and error propagation instead examine how prediction errors accumulate across denoising steps (Ning et al., 2023, 2024; Li and van der Schaar, 2024). More recently, in the setting of recursively trained diffusion models, Khelifa et al. (2026) derive terminal-divergence lower bounds parameterized by an endpoint-observability coefficient, which measures how much pathwise score-error energy remains detectable in the terminal distribution. These results show that inaccuracies along the sampling trajectory can accumulate into the global generation error and the pathwise error may be only partially observable in the terminal distribution. However, they do not fully characterize how the downstream sampling dynamics mediate the relationship between the magnitude of a perturbation along the trajectory and its effect on the final generated output. To address this gap, we develop a theoretic framework combining information-theoretic analysis with a dynamical characterization of output response, which explains why perturbations of equal strength can produce unequal output effects and derives how local perturbations propagate and accumulate to determine output responses. We measure perturbation strength by the KL divergence between perturbed and reference trajectory distributions, which we call path cost . The analysis shows that a perturbation’s effects on generated outputs depend jointly on its path cost, how it propagates and interacts with perturbations at other steps, and how the resulting changes are measured. Our contributions are: • A theoretical framework explaining how perturbations affect generated outputs. We derive an exact decomposition of path cost that distinguishes changes in the output distribution from changes in trajectory distributions conditional on the output. We derive a first-order propagation law that quantifies how subsequent sampling dynamics transform local perturbations and how their contributions accumulate to determine the final output response. We further establish bounds relating feature mean responses to local output-distribution changes, explaining why a selected feature mean can fail to detect such changes. These results explain why equal path costs can produce unequal output effects. • Experimental support for the theoretical analysis. We test the framework through controlled perturbations at equal path cost, varying the sampling stages at which they are applied and their spatial directions. The resulting output responses differ across sampling stages and directions. The experiments show that first-order propagation can capture the temporal patterns of responses to finite perturbations, although predicted and observed magnitudes can differ substantially. They also show that smaller absolute feature mean responses can correspond to larger changes relative to baseline fluctuations, consistent with our theoretical analysis. • Applying the framework to diagnose output errors in cache-based acceleration. We apply the framework to cache-based acceleration for diffusion generation, where earlier model predictions are reused to avoid fresh evaluations. By tracing how the resulting approximation errors propagate and accumulate, we assess their impact on the final image and identify sampling intervals where caching causes larger output deviations. This propagation-based diagnostic identifies such intervals more reliably than local error magnitude and path-cost measures, which remains informative even when predicted and observed error magnitudes differ substantially. 

# 2 Related work 

Error propagation in diffusion sampling. Path-space control relates diffusion modeling to KL minimization (Berner et al., 2024), while score-error analyses provide likelihood and sampling-error bounds (Song et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022; Benton et al., 2024). Li and van der Schaar (2024) relate local and cumulative errors through a propagation equation. Khelifa et al. (2026) quantify the endpoint visibility of pathwise score errors to derive terminal-divergence lower bounds, with applications to recursive training. We study perturbations of a fixed sampler, connecting output visibility to downstream propagation and the information captured by a selected feature mean. 

Conditional information and observable response. Conditional-score projections characterize Fisher informa-tion retained by an observation (Pollard, 2013), while fluctuation–response bounds relate mean sensitivity to variance and path information (Dupuis et al., 2016; Dechant and Sasa, 2020). De Bortoli et al. (2025) bound diffusion sampling errors using smooth test functions. We build on these information and response identities 2to characterize how much path information reaches the output and how much a feature mean captures, with propagation determining its response. 

Spectral generation dynamics. Benita et al. (2025) derive Gaussian spectral transfer dynamics and use data spectra to design schedules. We compare temporal and directional responses at matched path cost, and distinguish absolute response from response relative to baseline feature fluctuations. 

Caching and sensitivity-based acceleration. DeepCache reuses high-level features (Ma et al., 2024); SenCache uses denoiser sensitivity to latent and timestep changes (Haghighi and Alahi, 2026). ERTACache analyzes and corrects feature-shift and step-amplification errors (Peng et al., 2026), while EpaCache calibrates reuse thresholds from terminal deviations under single-step caching interventions (Liu et al., 2026). We use reference-trajectory propagation to diagnose image errors from multi-step output caching and test whether it identifies sampling windows with larger errors more reliably than local-defect and path-cost measures. 

# 3 A Theoretical Framework for Perturbation Propagation 

In this section, we derive a theoretical characterization of how local perturbations propagate through diffusion sampling and affect measured output responses. We first quantify trajectory changes by path cost, separate output changes from conditional trajectory changes, and derive perturbation propagation. Our main theorem expresses the measured response through the propagated local perturbations and identifies the fractions of path information retained in the output distribution and captured by the chosen feature mean.

# 4 Controlled Perturbations Propagation with Equal Path Cost 

In this section, we test the theoretical framework by using controlled perturbations at equal path cost, which provides a common measure of perturbation strength for comparison. 

4.1 Experimental setup 

We study controlled perturbations in class-conditional ImageNet-256 generation using pixel-space JiT-B/L (Li and He, 2026) and ADM (Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021), and latent-space DiT (Peebles and Xie, 2023), without additional guidance. We group the 128 stochastic sampling steps into eight consecutive windows of 16 steps, numbered 1–8 in generation order from noise toward the output. In each experiment, we perturb the transition means within one window. To vary the spatial direction, we use unit-norm discrete cosine transform (DCT) templates, which represent spatial cosine patterns on the pixel or latent grid: DC is constant, and DCT-k has higher frequency as k increases. 4.3 Local predictions and finite perturbation responses 

The responses above are measured at finite perturbation strength, whereas the propagation analysis in Section 3.3 describes the output response to infinitesimal perturbations. We use DiT to test how well this local prediction captures the magnitude and temporal pattern of responses to finite perturbations. On the same 32 sampling trajectories, we compare the mean per-sample RMS of the output derivative V , computed through the sampler and decoder using Jacobian–vector products (JVPs), with that of the symmetric finite difference (Y +ε − Y −ε)/(2 ε) at ε = 0 .8.As shown in Figure 3, the local and finite responses follow similar trends across sampling windows and peak at windows 3, 4, and 5 for DC, DCT-4, and DCT-31, respectively. However, the finite-response peaks are only 36.8%, 47.7%, and 87.9% of the corresponding local predictions, as shown in the right panel. Thus, in this DiT example, local propagation identifies the most responsive sampling windows, while overestimating the peak response magnitudes at the tested finite amplitude. 

4.4 Feature responses relative to baseline fluctuations 

Having examined propagation through the decoded-image response in DiT, we now return to the feature mean responses in JiT and ADM to compare them on their baseline fluctuation scales. Section 3.4 relates a feature’s mean response to output information after normalization by its baseline variance. In ADM, the absolute response decreases more sharply than the baseline fluctuations, so DCT-16 also has a smaller normalized response. These results show that a smaller mean response can represent a larger change relative to a feature’s baseline fluctuations, consistent with the analysis in Section 3.4.
