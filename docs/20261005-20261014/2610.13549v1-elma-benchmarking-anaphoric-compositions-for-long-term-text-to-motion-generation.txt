Abstract
Text-guided long-term human motion generation is essential for applications such
as continuously operating digital avatars. Despite recent progress on short clips,
existing models struggle with multi-segment instructions that involve anaphora,
where constraints established in earlier segments must be preserved over time. In
this paper, we introduce a novel task of long-term motion generation from such
anaphoric compositions. To mitigate this, we present ELMA, an episodic long-term
motion dataset with dense segment-level annotations that preserve cross-segment
dependencies. Built via a fully automated collection and filtering pipeline, ELMA
extracts high-quality, continuous 30-second clips from in-the-wild videos, capturing
the episodic narratives necessary for long-range state modeling. Leveraging this
dataset, we fine-tune state-of-the-art motion generation models to incorporate long-
range context. Extensive experiments reveal fundamental shortcomings in current
models when handling context-dependent instructions. We demonstrate that ELMA
enables significant improvements in both global physical realism and semantic
alignment, setting a robust new baseline for long-term motion synthesis.

synthesizing arbitrary-length sequences in a single pass is challenging due to computational and

Fig. 1 illustrates three composition types for multi-segment instructions: atomic, concurrent, and
anaphoric. In this work, we focus on anaphoric composition, where later segments refer to entities
or constraints introduced earlier (e.g., “maintaining a salute while walking”). This setting requires
explicit state persistence across segments, yet most existing long-term datasets and benchmarks
annotate and evaluate segments largely in isolation, and therefore do not directly measure this
capability. As shown in Fig. 2, existing models struggle with long-term motion generation when later
segment instructions depend on earlier context.
We therefore propose to treat anaphoric composition as a distinct task setting in long-term motion
generation. To enable this task setting, we identify two key bottlenecks: (i) the lack of episodic
long-term datasets with dense, dependency-rich segment captions, and (ii) the inherent limitation of
current segment-based generation frameworks, which are typically trained on independent segments
and lack the capacity for explicit state propagation.
To address the first issue, we introduce ELMA (Episodic Long-term Motion dataset with multi-
segment Annotations), a 3D human motion dataset paired with multi-segment text instructions. This
dataset comprises training and evaluation sets collected from online RGB video sources using an
automated collection pipeline. Combined with a dependency-aware captioning pipeline, it provides
a foundation for long-term motion generation from diverse text instructions. The online video
clips preserve episodic coherence and naturally occurring long-range dependencies, which are
essential for modeling long-term motions with cross-segment constraints. To address the second
issue and understand the limitations of current architectures in this new setting, we conducted a
comprehensive evaluation of representative baseline methods. [24, 5, 14] Our analysis reveals that
while existing methods perform adequately under standard, independent text-to-motion settings, they
lack the capacity to explicitly propagate states. Consequently, they suffer from severe temporal
inconsistencies and performance drops when subjected to anaphoric constraints. To overcome this,
we fine-tuned these models using ELMA to handle anaphoric compositions. ELMA ensures that
dependency-induced constraints are accurately preserved and seamlessly integrated into subsequent
motion segments, bridging the gap between independent segment generation and coherent long-term
synthesis.

• Task: We formulate anaphoric text-to-motion as long-term motion generation from multi-

Related Work
Dataset for Long-term Human Motion. Dataset for Long-term Human Motion. Long-term
motion generation requires datasets with long motion sequences and segment-level captions. BABEL-
TEACH [21, 2] provides segment-level annotations, but its action-based labels often lack fine-grained
details. Several datasets have enriched textual descriptions by re-annotating AMASS motions, such
as FineMotion [28] and FrankenMotion [15]. More recent efforts scale long-term motion data from
in-the-wild videos, including HuMo100M [7] and OpenT2M [6]. However, these datasets either
rely on pose-centric descriptions or annotate each segment independently, and thus do not explicitly
capture episodic narratives with cross-segment dependencies. This leaves a critical gap for learning
long-term motion generation under anaphoric constraints.
Long-term Motion Generation Models.
Recent long-term motion generation models extend
text-to-motion synthesis to multi-segment instructions through latent blending [24], timeline-based
control [5, 20], autoregressive motion priors [34], and hierarchical control [14]. Despite their
significant strides in producing visually smooth transitions across sequential instructions, these models
fundamentally struggle with anaphoric compositions. This critical limitation primarily arises from the
absence of suitable training datasets featuring episodic, dependency-rich multi-segment annotations.
Without large-scale data explicitly demonstrating how to preserve and propagate established states
across long temporal horizons, current state-of-the-art models cannot learn to maintain temporal
consistency under anaphoric constraints. This unresolved data gap directly motivates the necessity of
our proposed ELMA dataset and the accompanying fine-tuning paradigm.

Anaphoric Text to Motion
We formulate anaphoric motion generation as a long-term motion generation task from multi-segment
text instructions with cross-segment dependencies. Given a sequence of T segment-level instructions
x1:T = (x(1), . . . , x(T )) and their segment lengths ℓ1:T = (ℓ1, . . . , ℓT ), where PT
t=1 ℓt = N, the
goal is to generate a motion sequence m1:N ∈RN×D, where N is the total number of frames
and D is the motion feature dimension. Unlike standard segment-wise text-to-motion settings, the
interpretation of a target instruction x(t) may depend on the preceding context x1:t−1. Thus, the
generated motion must be locally consistent with each segment description and duration, while also
resolving cross-segment dependencies and preserving previously established actions, object states,
and body-part constraints throughout the sequence.
Formally, let pdata(m1:N | x1:T , ℓ1:T ) denote the conditional distribution of valid long-term motions
given the multi-segment instruction sequence and segment lengths. The task is to learn a generative
model pθ that approximates this distribution, generating motions as ˆm1:N ∼pθ(m1:N | x1:T , ℓ1:T ).
We instantiate this task in ELMABench.

4.2
Data collection
First, we collect online videos using action-related queries (spanning daily activities, sports, and
interactions; details in Appendix A.1.1) and segment them into scene-level clips with PySceneDetect1.
This process yields more than 5 million scene candidates; however, to ensure that the selected clips
sufficiently preserve long-term motion context, we retain only scenes longer than 30 seconds, from
which we later extract a 30-second window where a target person remains continuously visible.
However, even scenes retrieved using action-related queries do not always contain human motions
suitable for motion extraction. To address this issue, we combine keypoint detection with YOLO-
Pose [18] and bounding box tracking with SAM 2 [23] to track persons within each scene and extract
30-second clips in which the full body remains continuously visible. In particular, keypoint-based
methods are more effective than conventional BBox-based methods at detecting occlusion, enabling
strict full-body visibility assessment based on confidence scores. Details of the tracking and filtering
pipeline are provided in Appendix A.1.3.
Finally, we apply GVHMR [25] to the filtered clips to extract SMPL-X parameters. GVHMR
is a model that enables human mesh recovery aligned with the world coordinate system, and it
serves as the foundation of the motion representation used in this work. Through this collection and
reconstruction pipeline, we ultimately obtain more than 20k motion clips.
4.3
Postprocessing
Motion sequences reconstructed from videos through the motion collection pipeline often contain
artifacts caused by occlusion, abrupt body motion, and estimation errors. This challenge is amplified
in long-duration RGB videos, where even short tracking failures or pose estimation errors can
accumulate over time and corrupt an otherwise usable sequence. To ensure the reliability of long-term
motion clips, we therefore apply a two-stage filtering procedure using AMASS as a clean reference
distribution, aiming to remove clearly corrupted clips while preserving valid motion diversity.
PoseFiltering (Stage1) removes clips with unnatural poses using a VPoser [19]-based pose natural-
ness score aggregated at the clip level. MotionFiltering+ (Stage2) removes clips with abnormal
temporal dynamics based on jerk and frame-to-frame joint rotation changes, and additionally discards
near-static failure cases. Full definitions, thresholds, and implementation details are provided in
the Appendix A.2. Fig. 5 illustrates the effect of our two-stage postprocessing. Fig. 5a shows
representative pose examples removed by PoseFiltering. The rejected examples include structurally
implausible poses that often arise in challenging in-the-wild videos, such as unstable balancing poses
during climbing or grasping motions, as well as inverted poses. Fig. 5b analyzes the change in jerk

4.4
Episodic Text Annotation
In multi-segment instruction settings, users typically provide the next motion instruction incremen-
tally while taking into account the preceding motion context. To achieve annotations that better
reflect this natural human input style than existing long-form datasets, which annotate each segment
independently, we introduce Episodic Text Annotation. We use 5-second segments as a practical
compromise between fine-grained controllability and reliable captioning in unconstrained videos.
Specifically, we first divide each 30-second video into six 5-second segments and generate an in-
dependent annotation for each segment using Gemini-2.5-Pro [10]. We then rewrite each segment
caption at time t with the same model, conditioning on the preceding captions up to time t −1, to
encourage natural episodic continuity and explicit anaphoric references (e.g., maintaining previously
established poses or constraints). Details are provided in the Appendix C.
4.5
Anaphora Judgment
We define a segment as anaphoric if its motion constraints become more specific when conditioned
on preceding segments (e.g., a body-part constraint, held object state, or pose that must persist across
segments). Based on this definition, we used GPT-4o [1] to determine whether each segment in the
constructed sequences has a dependency and to identify its dependency source segment(s). For each
target segment, the annotations of preceding segments were provided, and we prompted GPT-4o
to make a binary judgment on whether the motion described in the target segment becomes more
specific when the preceding context is available, while also predicting the corresponding dependency
source segment. This procedure was repeated three times, and a segment was regarded as anaphoric
only when both the binary judgment and the identified dependency source segment were consistent
across all three trials.
4.6
Data splits and dataset contents
To support reproducible benchmarking, we provide an official split of ELMA into 10.2k training
clips and 3k test clips. Each clip includes (i) a 30-second 3D motion sequence represented in
a HumanML3D-compatible format, (ii) six 5-second segment captions that consider preceding
context, and (iii) segment-level dependency metadata indicating anaphoric target segments and their

antecedents. These components enable training and evaluation under both context-independent and
context-dependent (anaphoric) multi-segment instruction formats.

ELMABench
5.1
Benchmark overview
ELMABench evaluates long-term text-to-motion generation under anaphoric multi-segment instruc-
tions, where later segments depend on earlier context. ELMABench is constructed by randomly
sampling 1k clips from the test split of the ELMA dataset (Sec. 4.6). Each benchmark instance
provides (i) a sequence of six segment-level instructions with fixed durations and (ii) ground-truth
motion for the full 30-second clip. Crucially, evaluation is performed only on segments annotated as
anaphoric targets, so that scores directly reflect a model’s ability to preserve and propagate previously
established constraints.
5.2
Task setting
Each test case in ELMABench corresponds to one 30-second motion clip, uniformly partitioned into
six 5-second segments. The input is a sequence of segment instructions x1:6 together with segment
lengths ℓ1:6, and the model outputs a single continuous motion sequence ˆm1:N.
We adopt the 263D motion representation of HumanML3D [12] for compatibility with standard
evaluators. Let T denote the set of target segment indices that are labeled as anaphoric (Sec. 4.5).
All metrics are computed by slicing the generated and ground-truth motions to the target segments
{( ˆm(t), m(t))}t∈T and aggregating scores over t ∈T . This prevents performance on non-anaphoric
(easier) segments from diluting the benchmark signal.
5.3
Evaluation settings: independent vs. anaphoric instructions
To systematically analyze how models handle cross-segment dependencies, we consider two instruc-
tion formats:
T2M (independent Text-to-Motion): The standard setting where text instructions are strictly
independent. Each segment description is self-contained without referencing prior states.
A2M (Anaphoric Text-to-Motion): Our proposed continuous setting where instructions exhibit
anaphoric compositions. The model must resolve cross-dependencies and maintain constraints
established in preceding segments.
Using these formats, we report three configurations to isolate the effect of ELMA supervision: (1)
T2M Train →T2M Inference serves as the standard baseline, evaluating the model’s original
generative capacity without complex dependencies, (2) T2M Train →A2M Inference evaluates the
zero-shot generalization of standard models when subjected to anaphoric instructions, exposing their
inherent limitations in state propagation, and (3) A2M Train →A2M Inference evaluates models
after fine-tuning on our ELMA training set, demonstrating their acquired capacity to explicitly learn
and preserve anaphoric constraints.
5.4
Metrics
To evaluate motion quality, we report four widely used metrics. FID measures the Fréchet dis-
tance between the distribution of generated motions and that of the ground-truth (GT) motions.
R-Precision@3 indicates how highly the generated motions are ranked with respect to the correspond-
ing GT descriptions through retrieval within a mini-batch. In addition, Matching Score computes the
distance between text and motion embedding vectors to evaluate their alignment. Finally, Diversity
measures the variance of the generated motion features across all sequences, reflecting the model’s
ability to produce varied movements.

Experiments
6.1
Baseline Methods
We compare three representative models [24, 5, 14] in long-term motion generation as our baselines.
To adapt them to our specific task setting, we apply minimal necessary modifications. For a fair
comparison, all models utilize CLIP as their text encoder. Under the Anaphoric Text-to-Motion
(A2M) setting, the text condition for each baseline is modified to take the weighted sum of the current
segment’s text embedding and all preceding segments’ text embeddings. This modification explicitly
injects historical context into the current generation step, enabling the models to maintain awareness
of previously established constraints without requiring complex architectural redesigns. All baselines
are carefully retrained to accommodate this contextual input setting.
6.2
Effectiveness of ELMA for Anaphoric Text-to-Motion
Tab. 2 evaluates whether ELMA provides effective supervision for resolving cross-segment depen-
dencies. For each baseline, we compare three settings: standard T2M training and inference, T2M
training with A2M inference, and A2M training with A2M inference.
For DoubleTake [24] and FlowMDM [5], A2M training with A2M inference achieves the best
FID and R-Precision@3 within each model family, outperforming the corresponding T2M setting.
This suggests that ELMA contains useful supervision for learning cross-segment dependencies,
and that models can exploit this information when trained and evaluated with the A2M format. In
contrast, applying A2M inference to T2M-trained models degrades performance, indicating that
simply providing additional context at inference time is insufficient.
UniMotion is an exception to this trend. We attribute this to its PCA-based text embedding com-
pression, which may limit its ability to retain rich multi-segment context. Overall, ELMA provides
effective training signals for Anaphoric Text-to-Motion, but the benefit also depends on whether the
model architecture can preserve and utilize long-range textual context.

Across all baselines, ELMA training substantially improves FID and R-Precision@3 over the zero-
shot setting. This shows that the ELMA training set is not only useful for learning anaphoric
dependencies, but also provides effective in-domain supervision for text-conditioned motion genera-
tion. Although Matching Score does not improve uniformly, especially for FlowMDM, we treat it
as an auxiliary metric in this benchmark. Since many evaluation samples are originally constructed
as multi-segment episodes, isolated segment-level text matching may not fully capture whether the
generated motion satisfies the intended context-dependent behavior.
6.4
Qualitative Results on Anaphoric Text-to-Motion
Fig. 6 shows qualitative results on a test case. In the input shown in Fig. 6e, the phrase “lifting a sword
across his chest” in the preceding segment provides the context needed to interpret the following
segment as lifting the sword from the chest. The zero-shot and T2M training/T2M inference settings
fail to resolve this cross-segment dependency. In contrast, FlowMDM under the A2M training/A2M
inference setting uses the context established in the preceding segment and generates motion that
better follows the instruction.
7
Conclusion and Limitations
This paper introduced anaphoric text-to-motion, a long-term motion generation setting in which later
instructions depend on entities, states, or constraints established in earlier segments. To support this
setting, we presented the ELMA dataset and ELMABench for evaluating whether models can resolve
anaphoric dependencies and preserve dependency-induced states over time.
While our framework demonstrates the potential of anaphoric long-term motion generation, several
challenges remain. One limitation lies in the accuracy of VLM-based automatic annotation. Although
recent VLMs can describe human motions in videos with increasing detail, they can still produce
errors in fine-grained motion descriptions, such as confusing left and right hands or missing subtle
object interactions. However, we believe these limitations will be alleviated by future advances
in video understanding models, leading to more reliable and semantically consistent annotations.
Another important future direction is the development of scalable and cost-effective annotation
pipelines for cross-segment dependencies. Efficiently generating high-quality annotations that
9


naturally preserve inter-segment dependencies would further expand ELMA and facilitate larger-scale
studies on context-aware motion generation.
Despite these limitations, we believe this work is an important first step toward addressing the
challenging problem of maintaining temporal consistency in long-term text-to-motion generation.
We hope ELMA and ELMABench will serve as foundations for future research in this direction.

Data collection Pipeline Details
A.1
Data collection
A.1.1
Online video Search
The search queries for video retrieval were constructed based on the action categories from Kinetics-
700 [26] and the raw labels from BABEL [21]. We translated each label into Japanese, Chinese,
and English, and further expanded the queries by using GPT-4o [1] to generate five synonymous
expressions for each language, aiming to broaden the source-video distribution and capture a diverse
set of human motions.
A.1.2
Scene Segmentation
Raw videos often undergo frequent scene cuts, which can undermine the consistency of motion data.
We leverage PySceneDetect2 to segment the footage, retaining only video segments without detected
scene cuts for at least 30 seconds. We choose 30 seconds as a practical trade-off between semantic
transitions and data availability.
A.1.3
Hierarchical Multi-Person Detection and Tracking
This stage addresses the technical challenges of scaling motion extraction to long-term sequences.
First, error accumulation poses a significant risk in long-term mesh estimation. Continuous mesh
estimation over extended sequences is prone to drift, especially when the subject undergoes occlusion
or exits the frame. To mitigate this, it is crucial to filter out low-quality input segments in which
the tracked subject is partially occluded or not fully visible at any point in the sequence. Second,
such high-quality segments are relatively scarce in online videos. Consequently, conventional single-
subject filtering yields low data density, making large-scale collection inefficient. To address these
challenges, we adopt a hierarchical strategy: a coarse keypoint-based tracker for high-recall candidate
mining, followed by a fine pixel-level mask propagation for high-precision cropping.
Coarse stage: Candidate Selection and Filtering with Keypoint-based tracking
We first perform a computationally rough screening to identify suitable video segments using YOLO-
Pose [18] and ByteTrack [33]. YOLO-Pose provides per-frame person detection together with 2D
body keypoints, and ByteTrack associates these detections across frames to form consistent person
tracks. To maximize data yield, we extract all eligible subjects within a frame rather than limiting
detection to a single primary actor. We then sample frames at regular intervals, run pose estimation
on the sampled frames, and filter the resulting tracks based on two criteria:
• Keypoint Reliability. To ensure robust pose estimation, we require that a significant
proportion of body joints predicted by YOLO-Pose exhibit high per-joint confidence scores.
This step effectively filters out subjects with partial visibility or severe occlusion.
• Relative Movement Score. To exclude near-static subjects, we compute a relative movement
score for each track within a scene. Let ct denote the bounding-box center at frame t, and
let at denote the bounding-box area. We define the score as

steps remove noisy motions without collapsing ELMA into an AMASS-like text domain, thereby
preserving semantic diversity with object- and scene-specific terms.

C.3
Annotation Quality
To assess the reliability of our automatic annotations, we conduct two user studies and report both
annotation accuracy and inter-annotator agreement measured by Gwet’s AC1. In each study, we
randomly sample 50 motion segments and ask three annotators to provide binary judgments. Fig. 9
shows the user study instructions and the app interface used for this evaluation. In the first study, the
annotators evaluate whether the automatically assigned anaphora labels are correct. In the second
study, they evaluate whether the generated text annotations are consistent with the corresponding
motion segments. For each segment, we compute the average binary score across annotators as the
annotation accuracy, and we use Gwet’s AC1 to quantify inter-annotator agreement. As a result, the
automatically assigned anaphora labels achieve an accuracy of 75% with Gwet’s AC1 = 0.58, while
the generated text annotations achieve an accuracy of 81% with Gwet’s AC1 = 0.64.
D
Training Details
D.1
Implementation Details.
To ensure accurate assessment in our new setting, we fine-tuned the evaluation network using the
ELMA training set. Specifically, we initialized the evaluator with the Decomp_v3 AutoEncoder
pretrained on HumanML3D, and fine-tuned it for text-motion matching with a batch size of 1024 and
a learning rate of 1 × 10−4 for 37 epochs. To align with the word length distribution of our training
data, we also expanded the max_text_len parameter to 24.
For the generation evaluation, we strictly use the identical test split for all models. Each model gener-
ates a 30-second continuous motion sequence guided by the multi-segment instructions. Importantly,
the evaluation metrics are computed exclusively on the target segments that are pre-identified as
anaphoric, preventing score influence from easier segments.
During the training phase for both A2M and T2M settings, each baseline model is initialized with its
respective HumanML3D-pretrained checkpoint and fine-tuned for an additional 50,000 steps. All
experiments were conducted using a single NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada Generation GPU. Following
standard protocols, each evaluation is performed 10 times, and we report the mean values along with
their 95% confidence intervals.

E
Broader Impact
E.1
Positive Societal Impact
ELMA and ELMABench target the setting of long-term, context-dependent text-to-motion generation,
which underlies a wide range of beneficial applications. Reliable long-term motion generation
can reduce the cost of motion-capture pipelines for indie game developers and animators, enable
continuously controllable digital avatars for virtual and augmented reality, support previsualization
in film production, and provide motion priors for humanoid robotics imitation learning. By making
episodic dependency-rich data publicly available under a research license, ELMA also lowers the
barrier to entry for academic groups studying long-horizon human motion modeling, an area where
dataset access has historically been a bottleneck.
E.2
Negative Societal Impact and Mitigations
Risk of misuse for impersonation.
Realistic human motion synthesis can, in principle, contribute
to deepfake-style content. We mitigate this in two ways. First, ELMA distributes only SMPL-
19


X body-pose parameters; identity-revealing body-shape parameters (betas) are stripped from all
released sequences, and original videos are not redistributed. Second, ELMA is released under a
non-commercial research-only license (see Sec. F), explicitly disallowing commercial deployment
such as unauthorized avatar impersonation services.
Privacy of in-the-wild video subjects.
Source clips are sampled from publicly accessible online
platforms. We do not redistribute the original videos themselves, nor do we release source video
URLs. Instead, we release only derivative motion parameters without body-shape parameters (betas)
and text annotations. This design reduces the risk of directly identifying or redistributing the original
source content.
Demographic and cultural bias.
Online videos do not reflect a uniform demographic distribution.
Action categories popular in particular geographic regions, age groups, or fitness communities are
likely overrepresented, while underrepresented populations may be correspondingly underrepresented.
Models trained on ELMA may inherit these biases and produce more reliable motions for over-
represented activity types. We encourage users to report observed biases and to combine ELMA with
demographically curated datasets for downstream applications where fairness is critical.
Surveillance and tracking concerns.
Although ELMA is intended for generative modeling, motion
data, and the accompanying collection pipeline (keypoint tracking, mask propagation, mesh recovery)
could, in principle, be repurposed for surveillance. The non-commercial research-only license,
the gated access mechanism described in Sec. F, and the explicit removal of body-shape identity
parameters together discourage such repurposing.
Annotator welfare.
For the user studies in Sec. C (Annotation Quality), annotators were recruited
internally from the authors’ institution. They were compensated at a rate of 1,230 JPY per hour, which
meets or exceeds the local minimum wage. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the
authors’ institutional ethics review board prior to data collection. All annotators were provided with
task instructions, an estimated completion time, and information about the study before participation.
