Abstract
Translating language into humanoid motion requires coordinating body parts, determining mo-
tion duration, and producing references suitable for robot execution. We introduce PartiGen, a part-
aware latent diffusion framework that incorporates anatomical structure into a shared whole-body
motion representation. Part-specific attention processes anatomically masked frame features, and its
outputs are fused to combine local limb information with whole-body context. Conditioned on text
and motion history, the model generates motion primitives autoregressively. Forward-kinematics
supervision encourages kinematic consistency, while a learned end-of-sequence predictor selects
a frame-level endpoint after generation. On HumanML3D retargeted to the Unitree G1, PartiGen
achieves a segment-level FID of 3.165, a 16.8% reduction over TextOp, and improves R@1 by 1.4
percentage points. In the duration study with 16-frame primitives, endpoint prediction reduces mean
absolute duration error from 12.471 to 3.080 frames compared with a fixed-length cutoff. We further
evaluate robot execution using a pretrained, frozen GRIT tracker: 409 of 500 generated references
(81.8%) pass the posture-based stability screen in MuJoCo. Following simulation screening and
manual selection, 26 of 27 attempted motions are judged successful on the real G1.
Keywords: Motion Generation, Embodied AI, Humanoid Robotics, Structured Representations

Introduction
Natural language offers an intuitive interface for specifying whole-body humanoid behaviors. Text-to-
motion generation [Guo et al., 2022a, Tevet et al., 2022, Guo et al., 2024] supports their synthesis, but
visually plausible motion may violate robot joint limits, dynamics, actuator capabilities, or contact con-
straints. We study a system in which a language-conditioned generator specifies a whole-body reference
and its temporal extent, while a tracking controller uses robot-state feedback to produce joint-level con-
trol targets. The challenge is to generate references that express the requested behavior while accounting
for the robot’s motion constraints and the task’s temporal organization.
An instruction such as “walk while waving” illustrates the challenge: the arm gesture and locomotion
must coexist within a single robot trajectory, with consistent root motion, joint configurations, and timing.
A reference generator therefore needs to preserve the distinct roles of body parts while representing their
relationships within a shared whole-body context. Part-aware modeling [Zhong et al., 2023] and physics-
guided generation [Yuan et al., 2023] provide relevant foundations for addressing these requirements.
We investigate how an anatomical prior can guide local feature processing within a shared robot-motion
representation, together with kinematic supervision of the resulting trajectory. Its temporal extent must
also reflect the instruction, with an endpoint that need not coincide with the boundary of a fixed-length
motion primitive. These considerations connect body-part structure, kinematic consistency, and endpoint
selection within the same robot reference-generation problem.
We propose PartiGen, integrating language-conditioned humanoid motion generation and execu-
tion. Part-aware attention builds a shared whole-body representation from anatomically masked inputs.
Text- and history-conditioned latent diffusion generates motion primitives under forward-kinematics su-
pervision, followed by frame-level endpoint selection on the complete candidate trajectory. A pretrained,
frozen GRIT controller [mrzuang, 2026] tracks the references using robot-state feedback in MuJoCo and
on the Unitree G1.
Our contributions are:
1. An anatomical input-grouping mechanism for robot motion representation learning. Fixed
masks select joint-angle channels before independent Q/K/V projections, while shared motion
features and a single whole-body latent retain access to global context.
2. A language-conditioned motion generation framework with frame-level endpoint prediction.
Conditioned on text and motion history, the framework autoregressively generates motion primi-
tives in the shared latent space under forward-kinematics supervision. A learned end-of-sequence
predictor then selects an endpoint within the complete candidate trajectory.
3. Simulation and real-world validation.
Integrated with a frozen GRIT controller, PartiGen
achieves an 81.8% posture-screen pass rate over 500 simulated references. Following simulation
screening and manual selection, 26 of 27 motions attempted on a physical Unitree G1 are judged
successful by the authors.

Related Work
Text-to-motion generation.
Large-scale motion datasets [Guo et al., 2022a, Mahmood et al., 2019,
Punnakkal et al., 2021] provide a foundation for data-driven generation. Common modeling paradigms
include variational motion models [Guo et al., 2020, Petrovich et al., 2021, 2022], diffusion-based gener-
ation [Tevet et al., 2022, Zhang et al., 2022, 2023], and discrete motion tokenization [Guo et al., 2022b,
Jiang et al., 2023]. Motion representation learning [Zhu et al., 2023] and controllable diffusion pri-
ors [Karunratanakul et al., 2023, Shafir et al., 2023] offer complementary approaches to encoding and
manipulating motion. DART and TextOp [Zhao et al., 2025, Xie et al., 2026] further address autore-
gressive generation and interactive control. Building on autoregressive primitive generation, PartiGen
incorporates anatomical input masks and EOS-based endpoint prediction.
2


Structure-aware modeling.
Part-aware attention [Zhong et al., 2023] and skeleton-aware latent dif-
fusion [Hong et al., 2025] incorporate anatomical structure into motion representations. Hierarchical
models address fine-grained semantic control [Jin et al., 2023] and human-object interaction [Pi et al.,
2023], while cross-skeleton models accommodate varying skeletal structures [Khani et al., 2025, Xu
et al., 2026, Gat et al., 2025]. Skeleton conditioning has also been explored for human image genera-
tion [Ju et al., 2023]. PartiGen places its anatomical prior at the attention input through fixed masks on
raw frame features before part-specific Q/K/V projections. Each head receives its selected joint angles
together with shared root/contact features and all joint-angle increments. Head outputs are fused into a
shared stream, retaining a single motion latent rather than independent part latents.
Physical plausibility and robot execution.
PhysDiff and InterDiff incorporate physical constraints
into motion or interaction generation [Yuan et al., 2023, Xu et al., 2023]. Language-conditioned hu-
manoid generation and control [Jiang et al., 2024, Li et al., 2026], motion retargeting [Araujo et al.,
2025], and whole-body teleoperation [Ze et al., 2025] connect motion modeling to robot behavior. A2CF
supports humanoid policy learning through state-dependent assistive forces that gradually diminish as
proficiency improves [Cao et al., 2025]. HiWET uses hierarchical reinforcement learning for world-
frame end-effector tracking in long-horizon humanoid loco-manipulation [Cao et al., 2026]. PartiGen
combines FK-supervised motion generation with an execution component based on the pretrained, frozen
GRIT controller [mrzuang, 2026]. Kinematic supervision regularizes references during generator train-
ing; execution evaluation measures their tracking under this adopted controller.
Temporal modeling.
Sequence continuation and termination are distinct design choices. Related tem-
poral generation frameworks include latent audio diffusion [Liu et al., 2023], neural-codec speech model-
ing [Wang et al., 2023], and autoregressive video diffusion [Henschel et al., 2025]. For motion, PartiGen
selects an endpoint at frame resolution. It generates a complete candidate sequence before applying an
EOS probability threshold to select its endpoint; this selection does not stop generation online.

3.1
Problem Formulation
Given a natural-language instruction ctext and a motion history H = {x1, . . . , xnH}, our goal is to
generate a future whole-body robot reference F = {xnH+1, . . . , xnH+nF } and determine its length nF .
The reference should express the requested behavior through kinematically consistent motion with an
appropriate duration. It is then converted into controller-compatible reference states and tracked by the
system’s controller using robot-state feedback.

foot-contact states, and joint configurations and their inter-frame changes:

3.3
Body-Part-Aware Motion VAE (BP-MVAE)
A robot motion representation must capture local joint patterns together with their whole-body con-
text. BP-MVAE uses part-aware attention to process anatomically masked frame features, then fuses
the resulting features into a shared latent for whole-body reconstruction. The motion encoder injects
anatomical structure before Q/K/V projection: fixed masks select each head’s joint-angle channels from
the raw 57-dimensional frame features, while root/contact features and all joint-angle increments re-
main shared. Each head applies independent Q/K/V projections and temporal attention, and the outputs
are fused into the shared Transformer stream, combining part-specific inputs with whole-body context.
Grouping, masks, and implementation details are provided in Appendix S2.
Shared 128-Dimensional Motion Latent. Each motion primitive is encoded as a single latent vector
z ∈R128 that jointly represents root and joint motion. Anatomical structure guides feature processing,
while all 128 latent coordinates are shared across the whole body. The decoder reconstructs the complete

motion primitive from this code and motion history.

3.4
Duration-Adaptive Latent Motion Diffusion (DA-LDM)
Conditioned on text and motion history, DA-LDM uses latent diffusion [Rombach et al., 2022] to gen-
erate successive motion codes in the shared space Z = R128 learned by BP-MVAE. These codes are
decoded into a candidate whole-body reference trajectory, whose endpoint is selected at frame resolution
by EOS after generation. Shared-Latent Denoiser Architecture. We implement the denoiser fθ using a
Transformer backbone with direct clean-latent (x0) prediction. At diffusion timestep τ, its motion input
is the noisy shared code zτ ∈R128. A common projection maps this code into the denoiser’s hidden
space:
uτ = Wzzτ + bz + Etime(τ),
(3)
where Wz ∈R512×128 and bz ∈R512 map the shared latent to the 512-dimensional hidden space, and
Etime(τ) is the diffusion timestep embedding. The denoiser operates on one shared motion code, without
separate projections or latent branches for the root, arms, torso, or legs. Multi-Modal Conditioning. The
denoiser is conditioned on motion history H via cross-attention, CLIP text features ctext [Radford et al.,

3.5
Adaptive Motion Duration via EOS Prediction
A requested behavior need not end at a fixed-length primitive boundary. Each primitive has a shared code
z[k] ∈R128 and generates ℓ∈{8, 16} new frames; EOS therefore selects an endpoint at frame resolution
after candidate generation. The motion head predicts the clean latent, while the EOS head is an MLP
whose last hidden layer has width 128. EOS supervision uses positive-weighted binary cross-entropy,
LEOS = meanvalid [−αy log ˆs −(1 −y) log(1 −ˆs)] ,
α = 0.3,
(5)
where each frame has a binary yes/no target y and ˆs is its predicted EOS probability; the mean is over
valid frame targets. The positive-class coefficient is distinct from the overall EOS loss weight λEOS =
0.5. The overall training objective is Ltotal = Lmotion + λEOSLEOS.
Post-Generation EOS Selection. All experiments generate a 320-frame candidate, matching the
dataset maximum: 40 primitives for ℓ= 8 or 20 for ℓ= 16. The final motion reference retains frames
up to and including the first frame whose EOS probability exceeds γ = 0.9; if none does, all 320 frames
are retained. Its length need not be a multiple of ℓ. Selection occurs after complete candidate generation
and does not stop generation early or save diffusion computation.

Experiments
We evaluate how well PartiGen generates robot references, including under multi-part instructions (Ta-
bles 1 and 2), how its design choices affect reference quality and duration (Tables 4 and 5), and how a
frozen controller executes the resulting motions (Section 4.4 and Table 3). Reference metrics are com-
puted before tracking; “Ours” and “Full” denote the generator in these evaluations.
4.1
Experimental Setup
Motion Domains and Retargeting.
TextOp provides a same-domain comparison:
both it and
PartiGen are trained on G1 motions and directly generate G1 references. DART [Zhao et al., 2025]
and MDM [Tevet et al., 2022] provide generation-and-retargeting comparisons: their human-domain
outputs are converted to SMPL as needed and retargeted to G1 using General Motion Retargeting
(GMR) [Araujo et al., 2025], also used to construct HumanML3D-G1. All methods are evaluated as
G1 references under the same metric protocol. DART+GMR and MDM+GMR comparisons therefore
reflect differences in generation, training domain, and conversion path.
Source-motion fitting and
retargeting details are given in Appendix S3.1.
Text-Motion Evaluator. Due to the kinematic discrepancy between the G1 humanoid (23-DOF) and
the SMPL human model, evaluators trained on human skeletons are not directly applicable. Following
the HumanML3D evaluation protocol [Guo et al., 2022a], we train a G1-specific motion-text alignment
model. A GRU-based motion encoder and a pre-trained CLIP text encoder [Radford et al., 2021] are used
to map both modalities into a 512-dimensional shared latent space using contrastive learning. The gener-
ator and evaluator share a motion-disjoint split of approximately 25,000 training motions and 2,576 test
motions; test motions are excluded from training. All methods use the same frozen evaluator checkpoint
and identical text/motion preprocessing. The HumanML3D-G1 reference evaluation uses the complete
test set for FID and Diversity. Implementation Details. BP-MVAE and DA-LDM are trained for 200k
and 300k steps, respectively, each on a single RTX 4090 with batch size 512; evaluation uses batch size
128. Table 1 and the ablations use 8-frame primitives; the duration study uses 16-frame primitives for all
variants.

Conclusion
PartiGen integrates language-conditioned humanoid motion generation and closed-loop execution, us-

S2
Detailed Architecture
We provide the internal configurations of the motion-generator networks below. These configurations
describe BP-MVAE and DA-LDM, not the adopted GRIT policy.
S2.1
BP-MVAE Encoder and Decoder
The BP-MVAE encoder and decoder use Transformer stacks with 9 layers each. The shared hidden
dimension is 512, with 4 attention heads, GELU activations, and Layer Normalization. The part-aware
modification concerns the construction of Q/K/V from masked motion features; the shared frame embed-
ding, residual connections, and feedforward blocks retain their standard form. The decoder reconstructs
future motion from the shared latent and history. In both encoder and decoder, the nine-layer stack re-
peats two masked-feature attention layers followed by one standard attention layer three times. Each
masked Q/K/V branch reads the 57-dimensional frame features; this input dimension is distinct from the
512-dimensional hidden residual stream.
S2.2
Hidden Layer Dimensions and Block Architecture
Following the generator’s YAML specifications, we define its core dimensional parameters as follows:
Model Dimensions: We employ a consistent hidden dimension of hdim = 512 and a feedforward
network size of ffsize = 1024.
• Hidden Dimension (hdim = 512): This serves as the uniform width for all Transformer layers,
including the total dimension for Query, Key, and Value matrices, as well as the dimensionality for
Layer Normalization and residual connections.
• Feedforward Size (ffsize = 1024): This represents the intermediate dimension of the Feedforward
Network (FFN). The FFN follows a linear 512 →1024 projection, GELU, dropout, and a linear
1024 →512 projection, maintaining a 2× expansion ratio.
Structure of the Transformer Block: The shared backbone retains standard attention, normaliza-
tion, residual connections, and feedforward sublayers. In the part-aware module, the residual stream
is 512-dimensional, while the Q/K/V branch reads the original 57-dimensional frame features through
fixed part masks. The concatenated head outputs pass through the output projection and return to this
shared stream. No additional modification of the residual or feedforward blocks is introduced.

Shared 128-Dimensional Latent Representation
The PartiGen generator uses a single shared motion latent z ∈R128 for each encoded motion primitive.
Body-part information enters feature processing, while the latent represents root and articulated-body
motion jointly.
Concept of the Unified Latent Space: All body regions share the same 128 coordinates. There
are no dedicated body-part subvectors and no separate global, arm, torso, or leg latent branches. The
128-dimensional latent size is distinct from the 128-dimensional attention head width; these are separate
architectural quantities.
Design Rationale: The shared latent provides a compact common representation for the following
aspects of motion:
15


• Whole-Body Context: Locomotion and gestures involve dependencies across limbs and the torso.
Their features are represented jointly in the shared code.
• Root and Articulation: Global trajectory information and local joint motion use the same latent
representation without separate global and local code branches.
• Shared Capacity: No subset of coordinates is reserved for a particular body region, so the full
128-dimensional representation is available for the encoded motion.
Role of Body-Part Attention in a Shared Space: Body-part attention is used during feature pro-
cessing:
• In the Encoder: Attention-derived motion features are fused into the shared latent vector z.
• In the Decoder: Whole-body motion is reconstructed from the shared code together with motion
history. Individual body parts do not receive independent latent codes.
Input Feature Representation and Dimensionality
Each motion frame xt contains nfeats = 57 features describing root orientation, foot contacts, local root
displacement, root height, joint angles, and joint-angle increments.

Training Configuration and Loss Decomposition
We provide the detailed training schedules and the granular decomposition of the loss functions used for
the BP-MVAE stage.
Optimization and Learning Rate Schedule: Both stages are trained on a single NVIDIA RTX
4090 GPU with batch size 512 and optimized using AdamW with an initial learning rate of 5.0 × 10−5.
Evaluation uses batch size 128. The BP-MVAE uses cosine learning-rate annealing, whereas DA-LDM
uses linear learning-rate annealing. The gradient norm is clipped at 1.0. BP-MVAE uses β = 10−4 and
λkin = 0.8; DA-LDM uses an overall EOS loss weight of λEOS = 0.5.
Multi-Stage Training Schedule: The BP-MVAE is trained for 200,000 steps, divided into four
strategic stages:
• Stage 0 (0–30k): Initial feature and joint position reconstruction.
• Stage 1 (30k–80k): Introduction of latent space regularization (LKL).
• Stage 2 (80k–140k): Emphasis on temporal continuity and root trajectory stability.
• Stage 3 (140k–200k): Final fine-tuning of kinematic smoothness and foot contact.

S2.5
Duration-Adaptive Latent Diffusion Model (DA-LDM) Specifications
The second stage, DA-LDM, performs conditional motion synthesis in the shared latent space Z =
R128. The Transformer denoiser projects the shared noisy code into its 512-dimensional hidden space,
conditioned on text, motion history, and diffusion timestep. It does not use separate body-part latent
branches.
Denoiser Architecture: The core of the DA-LDM is a DenoiserTransformer consisting of 8 trans-
former layers. Notably, this is one layer shallower than the BP-MVAE (9 layers), optimizing the balance
17


between generative capacity and inference speed. The internal dimensions (hdim = 512, ffsize = 1024)
are kept consistent with the VAE to ensure seamless feature transition.
Multi-Modal Input and Conditioning: The denoiser processes three distinct input streams to gen-
erate kinematically grounded motion:
• Noisy Latent (zτ): A single latent token per primitive, with batched shape [B, 1, 128], representing
the noised clean motion code at diffusion timestep τ.
• Motion History (H): A temporal window of 2 historical frames ([2, 57]), providing the local kine-
matic context for transition stability.
• Textual Conditioning: Global semantic intent extracted from a pre-trained CLIP (ViT-B/32) model
with a fixed embedding dimension of 512.

Dataset and Evaluation Details
In this section, we provide further details regarding our data processing pipeline for humanoid retargeting
and the construction of our custom evaluation protocol.
S3.1
Unified Retargeting Protocol Details
As introduced in Section 4.1, PartiGen and TextOp are trained in the G1 motion domain. DART, MDM,
and AttT2M instead generate human-domain motions, which are converted to SMPL as needed and re-
targeted to G1 using the same GMR method used for the training data. The resulting DART+GMR,
MDM+GMR, and AttT2M+GMR pipelines are evaluated on G1 references under the same metric proto-
col as the native-domain generators. This comparison assesses the complete pipelines; it does not isolate
architecture from training-domain and conversion effects. TextOp provides the same-domain baseline.
The GMR joint definitions and ordering are identical for the retargeted training data used by PartiGen
and TextOp and for the retargeted human-motion outputs of the comparison baselines.
Step 1: SMPL-X to SMPL via SMPLify Optimization.
Since the General Motion Retargeting (GMR) framework expects standard SMPL body models, we first
project the SMPL-X motions into the SMPL parameter space. We utilize an optimization-based fitting
procedure inspired by SMPLify. For each frame, we optimize the SMPL pose parameters θ and shape
parameters β to minimize the distance between the extracted 3D joint positions of the source SMPL-X
motion (Jsrc) and the generated SMPL joints (Jsmpl(θ, β)). The objective function is defined as:
Lfit = Ljoints + λposeLpose + λsmoothLsmooth

Step 2: SMPL to Unitree G1 via GMR.
Once the motion is accurately represented in the SMPL space, we feed the parameter sequences into the
GMR pipeline, which maps the human kinematic chain to the specific 23-DOF topology of the Unitree
G1 robot. This mapping rigorously enforces the physical joint limits of the hardware.
S3.2
Evaluator and Statistical Protocol
The generator and G1-specific text–motion evaluator share the same motion-disjoint dataset split: ap-
proximately 25,000 training motions and 2,576 test motions. No test motion is used in training. All
methods are scored with the same frozen evaluator checkpoint and identical text and motion preprocess-
ing. The evaluator uses the GRU motion encoder, CLIP text encoder, and 512-dimensional alignment
space described in the main paper. R-Precision and text–motion matching distance use the same evalu-
ation implementation across methods; the HumanML3D-G1 reference-quality evaluation computes FID
and Diversity from the designated evaluator features over the full 2,576-motion test set. The separate
compositional stress test uses its 100-instruction evaluation set.
For learned methods, entries reported as mean ± standard deviation summarize three runs trained
with different random seeds. The evaluator checkpoint remains fixed across these runs. These error
bars describe variation across training seeds, not repeated evaluation of one trained checkpoint or con-
fidence intervals. Tracking-table mean/median entries and stability counts retain their separately stated
definitions and are not converted to this reporting format. The Random-mask row is an exception to the
fixed-architecture training-seed convention: it aggregates three different random partitions and training
runs using mean ± sample SD. Table S2 gives the individual point estimates. Its SD measures variation
across partition/run combinations, not fixed-partition training-seed variation. For dataset-reference rows,
mean ± standard deviation instead summarizes three evaluation runs on the fixed reference dataset using
the same frozen evaluator. These standard deviations describe variation across evaluation runs, rather
than across independently trained generators, and are not confidence intervals.

S6
GRIT Execution Component and Deployment on Unitree G1
S6.1
Component Interface and Deployment Pipeline
The execution component adopts the pretrained GRIT tracking policy through its public deployment
repository [mrzuang, 2026]. GRIT is existing software, not a controller proposed or trained in this
work. Its weights remain frozen, and robot-state feedback is used by the tracker rather than the motion
22


generator. The generator supplies reference trajectories; GRIT produces joint-level control targets; the
deployment stack handles state feedback, policy inference, and command transmission.
To assess execution, we use the protocol trajectory preprocessing →closed-loop MuJoCo evaluation
→candidate selection →real-world execution. Simulation screening and candidate selection define the
hardware evaluation protocol, rather than additional learned components.
Trajectory preprocessing. For generated motions, the deployment pipeline receives reconstructed
reference trajectories at 30 Hz, with root position, root orientation quaternion, and 23 joint angles per
frame. This exported trajectory format differs from the model’s 57-dimensional feature layout in Ta-
ble S3. All original frames are retained, and trajectory dimensionality, numerical validity, and quater-
nion legality are verified. An interpolated transition from the robot’s current posture to the first reference
frame precedes motion playback. This transition addresses the initial posture mismatch; it does not re-
move discontinuities between frames within the generated reference. Root quaternions are converted
from xyzw to wxyz order; root positions and joint angles are resampled by linear interpolation and root
rotations by spherical linear interpolation (SLERP), yielding 50 Hz references at the original playback
speed. No source frames are trimmed (trimmed frames=0). The 23-DoF trajectory is mapped onto
the 29-DoF model by joint name, with the six missing wrist joints set to zero. Finally, reference states
of all body links are obtained via forward kinematics and velocity computation, and stored in the NPZ
format readable by GRIT.
Closed-loop evaluation in MuJoCo. Preprocessed trajectories are executed by the GRIT policy
in MuJoCo in closed loop, rather than by kinematic replay through direct joint-position setting. Each
trajectory is initialized independently, and the test procedure covers, in sequence: the default posture,
a smooth transition to the first motion frame, full motion execution, return to the default posture, and a
post-motion stability observation. Evaluation metrics include minimum pelvis height, maximum body
inclination, joint reference tracking error, peak joint velocity, and the actuator torque saturation ratio. A
trial is classified as simulation-stable if the minimum pelvis height is no less than 0.60 m and the maxi-
mum inclination does not exceed 35◦; as suspected fall if the height drops below 0.45 m or the inclination
exceeds 60◦; and as marginally stable otherwise. Trials with abnormal termination, incomplete testing,
or communication failures are logged separately and are not counted as passes.
Candidate selection. Passing the simulation stability check does not directly imply suitability for
real-world execution. We therefore apply a conservative selection stage with stricter thresholds on pos-
ture, reference tracking error, joint velocity, and torque saturation, and grade the candidate motions ac-
cording to lower-limb motion amplitude and root displacement. Motions that pass the automatic screen-
ing are further reviewed by manual replay. We emphasize that this screening is a risk-control step prior
to hardware trials and does not constitute a safety guarantee for the robot.
Real-world deployment. The real-robot setup reuses the selected reference trajectories and the
same frozen GRIT tracking policy as simulation. A Python controller receives robot states and computes
control targets at 50 Hz, communicating with a C++ bridge program over local UDP; the bridge connects
to the G1 via the Unitree SDK/DDS and publishes low-level control commands at 500 Hz, reusing the
most recent policy output for the high-frequency loop. The operating procedure decouples standing-up
from motion initiation: after pressing START on the gamepad, the robot transitions from its currently
measured joint configuration to the default posture via a 5-second minimum-jerk interpolation; once the
posture stabilizes, pressing the A button triggers another smooth reference transition into the first motion
frame and starts playback. The SELECT button triggers a latching damping stop that blocks subsequent
policy commands. Note that the damping stop does not guarantee that the robot remains standing and is
not a substitute for independent physical safety measures.

Scope of the simulation pass rate.
Full has a lower observed pass rate under the prescribed
height/inclination screen (81.8% vs. 92.8%). This result does not support a claim that FK supervision
improves that rate. However, the screen measures compliance with posture thresholds, not instruction
completion or overall execution quality; its pass-rate ordering alone cannot establish that Full is inferior
to w/o FK on those broader criteria.
Removing FK-based supervision may permit kinematically inconsistent references, including foot
sliding and body–body or body–ground interpenetration. Such artifacts can severely impair execution
and cause the controller to fail to reproduce the intended motion even while the robot remains upright.
The height/inclination pass criterion contains no checks for these artifacts, reference-tracking accuracy,
or semantic completion. Consequently, even complete failure to track or perform the requested motion
need not reduce the pass rate if the robot stays within the posture thresholds. Degeneration to stationary
standing can even yield a higher pass rate than attempting a demanding motion, despite failing the task.
Conversely, a valid low-posture motion such as squatting can violate the 0.60 m pelvis-height threshold.
These examples explain the metric’s limitations; the incidence of these failures and their contribution to
the observed w/o FK pass-rate increase have not been quantified.

S7
Extended Related Work
This section expands the literature review in the main paper, covering motion generation, anatomical
structure, physical plausibility, and temporal modeling.
S7.1
Text-to-Motion Generation
Text-driven human motion generation has advanced with the emergence of large-scale datasets
such as HumanML3D [Guo et al., 2022a], AMASS [Mahmood et al., 2019], and BABEL [Pun-
nakkal et al., 2021], together with progress in deep generative modeling.
Variational approaches
model action-conditioned motion distributions [Guo et al., 2020, Petrovich et al., 2021], while
TEMOS [Petrovich et al., 2022] addresses diverse motion generation from text. Diffusion-based meth-
ods, including MDM [Tevet et al., 2022], MotionDiffuse [Zhang et al., 2022], and ReMoDiffuse [Zhang
et al., 2023], provide another route to text-conditioned synthesis.
Parallel to continuous diffusion,
discrete token-based formulations such as TM2T and MotionGPT [Guo et al., 2022b, Jiang et al., 2023]
explore motion as a language-like representation.
Complementary efforts investigate unified motion representation learning [Zhu et al., 2023] and con-
trollable diffusion priors [Karunratanakul et al., 2023, Shafir et al., 2023]. DART and TextOp further
explore autoregressive generation and interactive motion control [Zhao et al., 2025, Xie et al., 2026].
Language-conditioned humanoid control has also been studied through motion latent guidance without
retargeting [Li et al., 2026]. Other robot-oriented work addresses general motion retargeting [Araujo
et al., 2025] and whole-body teleoperation and imitation [Ze et al., 2025]. These approaches concern
different stages of the generation-to-execution pipeline; retargeting and tracking methods should be dis-
tinguished from text-conditioned motion generators.
These developments provide complementary choices of representation, conditioning, and temporal
generation. Our focus is how body-part information enters feature processing within a shared motion
representation. A whole-body representation does not itself imply the absence of anatomical structure,
nor does its use alone establish interference between body parts.
S7.2
Structure-Aware and Physically Plausible Motion Generation
Structure-aware motion modeling incorporates anatomical information into representations and feature
processing. AttT2M [Zhong et al., 2023] explores part-aware attention, while SALAD [Hong et al., 2025]
introduces skeleton-aware latent diffusion for motion generation and editing. Hierarchical approaches
address fine-grained control through semantic graphs [Jin et al., 2023] and multi-stage generation of
human-object interactions [Pi et al., 2023].
Recent efforts also address generation across heterogeneous skeletons, including UniMoGen [Khani
et al., 2025], NECromancer [Xu et al., 2026], and AnyTop [Gat et al., 2025]. These works broaden the
range of skeletal structures supported by motion models. Related use of skeletal conditioning extends
beyond motion: HumanSD [Ju et al., 2023] studies skeleton-guided human image generation. It therefore
provides an example of structural conditioning in a different output domain.
Another direction focuses on physical and kinematic plausibility. PhysDiff [Yuan et al., 2023] in-
corporates physics guidance into human motion diffusion, while InterDiff [Xu et al., 2023] addresses
27


human-object interaction with physics-informed diffusion. Harmon [Jiang et al., 2024] studies whole-
body humanoid motion generation from language descriptions. Such work motivates examining both the
quality of generated reference motions and their behavior under downstream control.
PartiGen introduces anatomical structure through fixed masks on raw frame features before indepen-
dent part-specific Q/K/V projections. Joint-angle channels are selected by body part, while root/contact
features and all joint-angle increments remain shared. Head outputs are fused into a shared stream and
represented by a single motion latent. FK-based supervision is applied during generator training without
a physical simulator or controller gradients. The system adopts pretrained GRIT [mrzuang, 2026] as its
frozen execution component; reference-motion quality and closed-loop execution are evaluated at their
respective stages.
S7.3
Temporal Modeling in Motion Generation
Motion duration is relevant to instructions involving concurrent or sequential actions. Motion diffusion
systems commonly specify a target horizon or length as part of generation [Tevet et al., 2022, Shafir
et al., 2023]. However, a single instruction can admit multiple valid durations, so temporal modeling
must accommodate variation rather than assume that semantics uniquely determines sequence length.
Temporal generation is also studied in other modalities. AudioLDM [Liu et al., 2023] uses la-
tent diffusion for text-to-audio synthesis, neural codec language models support text-to-speech gener-
ation [Wang et al., 2023], and StreamingT2V [Henschel et al., 2025] explores autoregressive extension
of video sequences. These examples illustrate different representations and generation strategies for
temporal data; they do not directly establish a motion-specific termination mechanism.
PartiGen generates motion primitives and selects the endpoint at frame resolution. A complete can-
didate sequence is generated before an EOS threshold is applied; the cutoff may lie within a primitive.
This is a post-generation selection stage and does not stop autoregressive generation online.
Discussion.
The PartiGen generator combines part-aware feature learning, a shared 128-
dimensional latent representation, FK-based regularization, and EOS-based duration prediction.
Body-part grouping guides feature processing without imposing independent part-specific latent
variables.
The shared representation provides a common space for modeling local movements and
their whole-body context. The system pairs this generator with the adopted frozen GRIT controller for
execution; its methodological contributions concern generation.
