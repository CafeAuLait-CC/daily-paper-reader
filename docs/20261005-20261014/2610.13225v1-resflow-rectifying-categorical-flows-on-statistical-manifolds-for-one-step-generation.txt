## Abstract 

Generative modeling for discrete data has been significantly advanced by learning continuous vector fields on the statistical manifolds to transport simple priors to target discrete distributions. However, because these continuous-time flows are not constrained to follow geodesics on the statistical manifold, thereby requiring multi-step numerical integration for simulation and leading to substantial computational costs during inference. In this work, drawing inspiration from the “straight and fast” properties of Rectified Flow in continuous Euclidean space, we propose the Rectified Statistical Flow (ReSFlow) model, which enables one-step categorical generation on statistical manifolds by explicitly learning geodesic trajectories that adhere to the intrinsic Riemannian geometry. By rectifying the flow on the statistical manifold, we theoretically guarantee that trajectories between prior and target distributions approximate geodesics. This rectification significantly reduces the transport cost by aligning the learned trajectories directly along geodesics on the statistical manifold, allowing the model to map pure noise to target discrete data in a single step. Through extensive experiments on diverse real-world generative tasks, ranging from image and text to biological domains, we demonstrate ReSFlow’s superior one-step generation quality, which truly embodies geodesically optimal and rapid categorical generation. Our code is available at https://github.com/ xiaohuanjia/ReSFlow .

## 1 Introduction 

Generative modeling has advanced rapidly, with diffusion and flow-based methods achieving state-of-the-art performance across continuous domains such as image synthesis [1, 2], molecule and protein design [3, 4, 5], and sequence generation [6, 7]. A key idea is to learn continuous-time dynamics, especially through flow matching, which formulates ODEs with continuous vector fields and provides a simulation-free, theoretically grounded training framework [8]. However, extending these methods to discrete data—common in NLP, drug discovery, and protein engineering—remains difficult, since discrete variables do not have the differentiable structure required by continuous flows. Most existing flow-based generative models for discrete data act directly on the native discrete space by optimizing the categorical probabilities of tokens [9, 10, 11, 12]. Rather than operating strictly within this discrete realm, a promising direction maps discrete variables to statistical manifolds, treating each categorical distribution as a point on the probability simplex with Riemannian geometry [13]. This allows smooth

vector fields and continuous-time flows to transport priors to target discrete distributions, bridging continuous generative modeling and discrete data synthesis. However, a critical limitation persists: The learned ODE trajectories deviate from geodesics because the conditional vector fields arising from independent couplings of prior and target samples yield a mixture of intersecting geodesics that, once marginalized, produce highly curved aggregate flows not aligned with any single geodesic path [14]. Simulating these tortuous trajectories demands multi-step numerical integration with fine temporal discretization, incurring substantial computational overhead during inference. This stands in stark contrast to the desiderata of modern generative systems, where rapid, ideally single-step, sampling is essential for scalable deployment. The fundamental tension between the geometric complexity of learned flows and the practical need for efficient generation thus constitutes a major bottleneck for categorical generative models. In the continuous Euclidean setting, an elegant solution to this problem has emerged through the concept of Rectified Flow [15]. Rectified Flow enforces straight-line trajectories between paired noise and data samples, yielding flows that are both geometrically simple and computationally efficient to simulate. The resulting “straight and fast” property enables high-quality generation with very few or even a single integration step, dramatically accelerating inference. Naturally, one might ask: can we bring the same benefits of trajectory rectification to categorical generation on statistical manifolds? 

The answer, however, is far from straightforward. The probability simplex, when equipped with the Fisher-Rao metric [16], forms a Riemannian manifold whose geometry is fundamentally non-Euclidean. Straight paths in ambient Euclidean space do not respect the intrinsic curvature of this manifold—they may leave the simplex, violate probability constraints, or deviate substantially from the true shortest paths. In this curved geometric setting, the natural analogue of a “straight path” is a geodesic: the length-minimizing curve that faithfully adheres to the manifold’s intrinsic Riemannian structure. Consequently, achieving efficient one-step categorical generation requires not Euclidean straightening, but rather a fundamentally geometric approach that aligns learned trajectories with the geodesics of the underlying statistical manifold. In this work, we propose Rectified Statistical Flow (ReSFlow), a framework that enables one-step discrete data generation by rectifying flows on statistical manifolds. The core idea is to map data from the probability simplex onto the positive orthant of the hypersphere via their natural diffeomorphism, and then perform flow rectification in this spherical space where geodesics admit closed-form great-circle expressions. By iteratively recoupling the learned ODE marginals and retraining the vector field on the hypersphere, ReSFlow progressively straightens transport paths toward geodesics, reducing both transport cost and path curvature. Extensive evaluations across vision, NLP, and bioinformatics benchmarks demonstrate that ReSFlow, requiring only a single sampling step, matches or outperforms state-of-the-art methods that rely on hundreds of steps. 

## 2 Preliminaries 

Notations. Let X = {1, . . . , K } be a finite categorical state space and P(X ) the probability simplex over X , i.e. the set of all categorical distributions. We use μ, ν ∈ P (X ) to denote individual categorical distributions, and p, q ∈ P (P(X )) to denote distributions over categorical distributions. We let p0, q ∈ P (P(X )) denote the source (prior) and target (data) distributions, respectively. We write ∆K−1 for the (K−1) -simplex and SK−1+ for the positive orthant of the unit hypersphere in RK .The square-root map π : ∆ K−1 → SK−1+ , defined by π(μ)i = √μi, is a diffeomorphism that renders the Fisher–Rao metric on ∆K−1 isometric to the round metric on SK−1+ ; we overload notation and write π(p) for the pushforward of a distribution p ∈ P (P(X )) onto SK−1+ .

## 3 Method 

As discussed above, the extension of rectified flow to statistical manifolds poses additional challenges, as the model needs to be fully aware of the intrinsic geometry induced by the Fisher information metric to properly define and straighten the ODE trajectories. In particular, the velocity field estimation and the evolution of marginal distributions—both central to the rectified flow framework—must be reformulated to respect the curvature and geometric structure of the statistical manifold. To address these challenges, we propose the Rectified Categorical Flow model, which reflows and straightens the ODE trajectories along geodesics on a mapped hyperspherical manifold.

## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental setup 

Our proposed ReSFlow models arbitrary distributions over categorical probability measures and enables one-step sampling via geodesic rectification on the statistical manifold. We first validate this property on a synthetic Swiss-roll distribution on the simplex, and then evaluate ReSFlow on real-world discrete generation tasks, including binarized MNIST image generation, character-level Text8 modeling, and conditional promoter DNA sequence design. Our primary comparison is with SFM [13], which achieves state-of-the-art performance on these benchmarks. The additional baselines reported in that work rely on multi-step sampling and do not outperform SFM; we therefore do not repeat them here. 4.2 Toy Example: Swiss Roll on the Simplex

[13], we normalize a two-dimensional Swiss roll and embed it into the simplex by setting μ3 = 1 − μ1 − μ2. All methods use the same time-dependent MLP and training budget. LinearFM and R-LFM use Euclidean interpolation on the simplex; SFM and ReSFlow use the square-root map with spherical geodesic interpolation on S2+. This controlled comparison separates the effect of geometry from the effect of rectification. As shown in Table 1, ReSFlow achieves an NLL of −2.25 , improving over LinearFM ( −1.88 ) and R-LFM ( −1.87 ), and remains competitive with SFM ( −2.38 ). It also attains the fastest ODE sampling time among all methods, reducing the runtime from 0.0679 s for SFM to 0.0361 s. This indicates that ReSFlow preserves the geometric advantage of Fisher–Rao geodesics and enabling efficient ODE generation. Non-rectified LinearFM and SFM fail to recover the Swiss-roll structure in the one-step setting, because their marginal vector fields average over many crossing conditional paths, producing curved trajecto-ries that are poorly approximated by a single update. Euclidean reflow improves one-step behavior relative to LinearFM, but it still rectifies paths under the wrong geometry, leading to samples that only partially capture the target structure. ReSFlow instead performs reflow directly on the hypersphere, aligning the learned trajectories with Fisher–Rao geodesics and producing the most faithful one-step samples. 4.3 Binarized MNIST 

We next evaluate on binarized MNIST to test whether the geometric and rectification effects observed in the Swiss-roll experiment extend to high-dimensional discrete generation. Each image is modeled as a collection of Bernoulli categorical variables, so good samples require both local binary consistency and global digit structure. This benchmark therefore allows us to examine two questions in a realistic setting: whether Fisher–Rao geometry improves categorical transport, and whether reflow makes the learned dynamics accurate under NFE = 1 sampling. Following the evaluation protocol above, we compare adaptive ODE sampling with Euler one-step generation. Table 2 reports mean FID and wall-clock sampling time over three random seeds. Under adaptive ODE sampling, SFM achieves the best FID, confirming that spherical geodesic interpolation provides a stronger reference geometry than Euclidean interpolation for binary categorical data. ReSFlow is slightly worse than SFM in this full-ODE regime, but remains competitive and is the fastest ODE-based method. Under one-step sampling, non-rectified LinearFM and SFM degrade substantially, indicating that their learned trajectories are too curved to be approximated by a single update. Rectification alleviates this issue, and ReSFlow achieves the best one-step FID by performing reflow under the hyperspherical Fisher–Rao geometry. Compared with SFM under accurate ODE sampling, one-step ReSFlow achieves an orders-of-magnitude improvement in sampling speed ( 0.452 vs. 112 .81 

seconds per 1,000 samples), while incurring only a mild and acceptable degradation in FID ( 5.973 vs.

ReSFlow achieves a mean FID of 5.973 ± 0.442 with a single function evaluation, while SFM requires 34 evaluations to obtain a comparable result ( 5.922 ± 0.262 ). This reduction in sampling steps shows that rectification makes the learned dynamics more suitable for one-step generation. 

4.4 Text8 

We further evaluate ReSFlow on character-level language modeling with Text8 [19], where each token is a categorical variable and the sequence distribution is substantially more structured than the toy and image settings. This experiment is designed to test whether the proposed manifold-aware rectification remains effective for high-entropy discrete sequences with a larger alphabet and stronger contextual dependencies. Since text generation is typically evaluated by predictive coding efficiency, we report bits per character (BPC), which naturally reflects whether the learned categorical transport is efficient and sufficiently well aligned with the data distribution: a lower BPC indicates that the model assigns higher probability to the observed character sequences and induces less distorted 8transport over categorical states. Since BPC is evaluated from the continuous-time likelihood, it requires ODE integration; we therefore report only ODE-based BPC in Table 3. Table 3 shows that the choice of geometry has a pronounced effect on categorical sequence modeling. SFM substantially improves over LinearFM, reducing BPC from 2.154 to 1.830 , which supports the importance of modeling categorical data under the Fisher–Rao geometry rather than using Euclidean interpolation on the simplex. Moreover, R-LFM performs worse than LinearFM, increasing BPC to 3.267 . By performing rectification under the correct manifold geometry, ReSFlow achieves the best overall BPC, confirming the effectiveness of our approach. Compared with binarized MNIST, Text8 has a larger categorical alphabet and richer sequential dependencies, so accumulated geometric errors are more likely to affect the final likelihood. By combining hyperspherical statistical-manifold modeling with reflow, ReSFlow preserves the intrinsic geometric advantage of SFM while further aligning the learned trajectories with geodesic transport on the manifold. These results support our design choice: the manifold formulation captures the proper geometry of discrete data, and rectification should be performed within that geometry to obtain efficient and reliable categorical generation. ## 5 Conclusion 

We proposed Rectified Statistical Flow (ReSFlow), a principled generative framework for discrete data that performs flow matching directly on the probability simplex under its natural spherical geometry. By proposing a reflow procedure in the spherical space via the square-root parameterization, ReSFlow respects the intrinsic Riemannian structure of categorical distributions while admitting closed-form conditional velocity fields. We further established a theoretical guarantee on the straightening rate of iterative spherical rectification, and demonstrated the effectiveness of ReSFlow over existing baselines across diverse discrete generation tasks. As future work, the straightening property of ReSFlow naturally lends itself to single-step generation on other spherical data, such as unit-norm embeddings, directional statistics, and rotation-valued representations.

## B Related Work 

Flow matching was originally proposed for continuous domains as an alternative to diffusion, learning transport along a prescribed probability path [21, 8, 15]. Extending this idea to discrete spaces is more challenging, since the dynamics evolve over categorical distributions rather than Euclidean variables. Related work has modeled discrete denoising in continuous time using continuous-time Markov chains (CTMCs), bridging diffusion-style methods and discrete data [22]. Building on this perspective, Discrete Flow Matching [9] extends flow matching to discrete generation, while 13 subsequent methods further refine the geometry and path design, including Fisher Flow Matching [23] and Dirichlet Flow Matching [12]. Compared with discrete diffusion, which typically learns reverse transitions, these approaches emphasize direct probability transport. Among them, Statistical Flow Matching (SFM) [13] is particularly appealing, as it formulates categorical generation on the simplex as a statistical manifold, better respecting the geometry of categorical distributions while avoiding explicit CTMC jump-rate parameterization. This provides a cleaner and often more stable framework for discrete generation, and thus serves as the foundation of our model. Flow-based models can generate high-quality discrete samples, but inference is often costly, typically requiring tens to hundreds of function evaluations. Moreover, most one- or few-step generative methods focus on continuous data [15, 24, 14, 25]; in discrete spaces, fast sampling has been explored mainly within diffusion-based frameworks through distillation or specialized acceleration schemes [26, 27, 10, 28]. Flow matching is often considered more suitable for low-step generation because it learns transport dynamics rather than local denoising and allows the design of straighter probability paths, e.g., rectified or OT-inspired flows. Its deterministic ODE formulation may therefore be easier to compress into a few updates, although standard flow matching is not inherently one-step. Recently, ReDi [11] extended rectified flow to discrete spaces via iterative reflow. However, this approach—like many continuous relaxation methods for discrete spaces—heavily requires pre-trained token embeddings to function, restricting its applicability when training purely from scratch. Moreover, the extension is performed entirely in Euclidean space, ignoring the intrinsic Riemannian geometry of the probability simplex. As a result, linear interpolation paths are not true geodesics on the statistical manifold, leading to suboptimal transport costs and trajectories that are inherently harder to straighten through reflow. Consequently, ReDi’s practical advantages over standard discrete flow matching remain less clear-cut than those of rectified flow in continuous settings.

## E Experimental Setup 

In this appendix, we provide dataset-specific implementation details for all benchmarks. Since the network architectures and training protocols differ across datasets, we describe the data processing, model architecture, and optimization setup separately for each benchmark. Unless otherwise specified, all rectified variants use a single reflow iteration, and all reflow models are initialized from the previous checkpoint and fine-tuned rather than trained from scratch. We summarize sampling procedures and evaluation metrics at the end of this section. 20 E.1 Swiss Roll on the Simplex Dataset and preprocessing. We follow the Swiss-roll-on-simplex construction used in prior cate-gorical flow-matching work. The data lie on the 2-simplex with K = 3 categories, i.e., each sample is a probability vector μ ∈ ∆2. We generate 1000 samples and use the resulting fixed empirical distribution as the target distribution q for this toy experiment. Since this benchmark is used as a geometric sanity check, we do not use a separate validation or test split. Reported likelihoods are evaluated on the same fixed empirical distribution and should be interpreted as relative comparisons among methods rather than as generalization metrics. The construction maps a two-dimensional Swiss roll to the simplex as follows. Let x and y denote the two Swiss-roll coordinates. We first shift each coordinate by subtracting its batchwise minimum and adding a padding constant pad = 1 .0. We then define 

M = max  

> i

(xi + yi) + pad , zi = M − (xi + yi),

and obtain the simplex point by normalizing 

μi = (xi, y i, z i)

M .

This construction ensures that all coordinates are nonnegative and sum to one. There is no additional dataset-level ϵ-smoothing parameter; numerical stabilization inside the flow model uses ϵ = 10 −4

where needed. The prior distribution p0 is sampled on the simplex by drawing independent exponential random variables, normalizing them to sum to one, applying a small clamp for numerical stability, and renormalizing. The target distribution q is the empirical distribution over the 1000 generated Swiss-roll samples. Unless otherwise stated, we use random seed 42 for data and model initialization, and seed 0 for generating reflow pairs. 

Model architecture. For this benchmark, all methods use the same MLP vector-field backbone. The input has per-position dimension 3 and tensor shape (B, 1, 3) , and the network outputs a vector field with the same per-position dimension. The hidden dimension is set to 128 .The encoder consists of an input branch, a time branch, and a fusion head. The input branch maps the current state through two linear layers with ReLU activation. The time branch applies two linear layers to a sinusoidal time-step embedding. The time embedding dimension is 128 . The resulting state and time features are concatenated and passed through a fusion MLP with three linear layers and ReLU activations to produce the final vector-field prediction. We do not use dropout or normalization layers in this toy architecture. All compared methods use this same MLP backbone in the Swiss-roll experiment. The methods differ only in their geometry and flow construction. LinearFM and R-LFM use Euclidean interpolation and projection on the simplex, whereas SFM and ReSFlow use the square-root map and spherical geometry on SK−1+ . Tangent-space projection is applied as described in Appendix C.1. 

Training protocol. For the initial flow-matching model, we train with Adam using learning rate 

10 −3 and no weight decay. Since the dataset contains only 1000 points, we use full-batch training with batch size 1000 . Each model is trained for 2000 epochs, corresponding to one parameter update per epoch. We do not use learning-rate scheduling, warmup, gradient clipping, or exponential moving average for this toy experiment. For the reflow variants, we use one reflow iteration. In both R-LFM and ReSFlow, the reflow model is initialized from the previous checkpoint and then fine-tuned, rather than trained from scratch. At the beginning of the reflow stage, we generate 10 ,000 recoupled endpoint pairs in batches of 256 

using ODE sampling from t = 0 to t = 1 with solver tolerances atol = 10 −5 and rtol = 10 −5.The resulting model is trained for 2000 epochs using mini-batches of 256 recoupled pairs. The two methods differ only in the geometry used for reflow: R-LFM performs Euclidean/straight-line reflow in the simplex geometry, whereas ReSFlow uses spherical tangent reflow under the square-root map. 21 E.2 Binarized MNIST Dataset and preprocessing. We use the static binarized MNIST dataset with the standard 

50 ,000 /10 ,000 /10 ,000 train/validation/test split. Each image has spatial resolution 28 × 28 . The original data are represented as 784 binary variables, and we treat each pixel as a categorical variable with K = 2 categories. Concretely, each binary pixel value x ∈ { 0, 1} is represented by the two-dimensional simplex coordinate (x, 1 − x). Thus, the model input is arranged as a tensor of shape

In the convolutional vector-field network, the simplex-valued input is internally rescaled from [0 , 1] to [−1, 1] before the first convolutional layer. 

Model architecture. For binarized MNIST, we use a convolutional vector-field predictor. The input and output tensors both have shape (B, 28 , 28 , 2) , where the last dimension corresponds to the two categorical states of each pixel. Internally, the categorical dimension is treated as the channel dimension for convolutional processing. All methods compared on binarized MNIST use the same convolutional backbone unless otherwise stated. The differences between methods are in the geometric parameterization and the reflow procedure. LinearFM and R-LFM use Euclidean/simplex geometry, whereas SFM and ReSFlow use the square-root map and spherical geometry on SK−1+ . The shared backbone uses four residual stages, four top-down refinement blocks, ELU activations, InstanceNorm++ normalization, and additive sinusoidal time embeddings with embedding dimension 128 .

Training protocol. For the base model, we train with Adam using learning rate 3 × 10 −4, weight decay 0, and batch size 256 . Models are trained for 100 ,000 optimization steps. We use a reduce-on-plateau learning-rate schedule with decay factor 0.5, patience 10 , and minimum learning rate 10 −4.No warmup is used. We clip the global gradient norm to 100 . No exponential moving average is used. Unless otherwise specified, the random seed is set to 42 .For rectified models, we generate recoupled endpoint pairs from the previously trained model by ODE sampling from t = 0 to t = 1 with solver tolerances atol = 10 −5 and rtol = 10 −5. E.3 Text8 Dataset and preprocessing. We use the Text8 character-level language modeling benchmark. We follow the standard character-level Text8 split: the first 90 M characters are used for training, the next

Model architecture. For Text8, all methods using the DiT backbone share the same architecture unless otherwise specified. The reflow model is initialized from the previous checkpoint and fine-tuned rather than trained from scratch. We generate 100 ,000 

recoupled pairs in batches of 256 and train the reflow model for 40 ,000 optimization steps with batch size 256 and learning rate 4 × 10 −5. E.5 Evaluation Metrics, NFE, and Sampling Protocols 

We describe the evaluation metrics and sampling protocols used in the main experiments. Since our model represents categorical variables as points on the probability simplex and performs flow matching after the square-root embedding, we distinguish likelihood-based evaluation, sample-quality evaluation, and task-specific downstream evaluation. 

Negative log-likelihood. For the Swiss-roll experiment, we report negative log-likelihood (NLL) using the continuous-time change-of-variables formula associated with the learned ODE. This evaluation treats the data as samples from a continuous density on the simplex, or equivalently on the positive orthant of the sphere after the square-root map. Therefore, unlike discrete cross-entropy, the resulting NLL is a differential likelihood quantity and is not constrained to be non-negative. Accurate NLL estimation requires numerical integration of the learned dynamics, and we therefore evaluate NLL only with adaptive ODE integration rather than with one-step sampling. 

Fréchet Inception Distance. For binarized MNIST, we report Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) as a sample-quality metric. Generated binary images are compared with the reference data distribution using the standard FID protocol; lower values indicate that the generated samples better match the real data distribution. We report FID both under adaptive ODE sampling and under one-step sampling. This allows us to separate the quality of the learned continuous-time flow from the quality of the compressed one-step sampler. 

Bits per character. For Text8, we report bits per character (BPC). Since ReSFlow and the compared flow-matching baselines are not autoregressive language models, we do not evaluate BPC by multi-plying token-wise autoregressive conditionals. ## G Limitations and Broader Impact 

While our manifold-based formulation successfully captures the intrinsic geometry of discrete data, it relies on model assumptions that may be violated in complex real-world scenarios. For example, the Fisher–Rao geometry inherently assumes semantic equidistance—treating all categorical states symmetrically without prior semantic bias. In reality, discrete tokens (e.g., words or subwords) possess rich, hierarchical semantic proximity (e.g., “cat” is conceptually closer to “dog” than to “apple”). While our method prevents the geometric distortion seen in continuous embedding methods (like ReDi), it does not currently incorporate pre-existing semantic priors. The assumption that pure statistical distances are sufficient may limit performance on tasks where capturing pre-trained semantic correlations is crucial. ReSFlow is a foundational methodological contribution to generative modeling on the probability simplex, and is not tied to any particular deployment. On the positive side, it offers a geometry-aware and sampling-efficient framework that can benefit discrete generative tasks in language, biology (e.g., DNA/protein sequences), and other scientific domains where categorical data are central, while its straightening property reduces inference cost and the associated energy footprint. On the negative side, ReSFlow shares the generic risks of all improved generative models: higher-quality and faster discrete generation could in principle be misused for disinformation or other malicious content. 30 NeurIPS Paper Checklist
