# ABSTRACT 

Scaling a learned flow-matching velocity field vθ by a gain γ(t) was recently shown to greatly improve generation quality. Prior work argued that velocity fields trained with mean-squared error (MSE) systematically underestimate velocity magnitude and that scaling corrects this error. We show that MSE training does not create a velocity-magnitude deficit. We find instead that velocity scaling reduces population time lag : sampled states at model time t resemble training states from an earlier time. Velocity scaling and moving model time back are two ways to address this population time lag. Across architectures and model sizes, measuring population time lag and using it to select a gain greatly improves generation quality, reducing FID from 28.0 to 12.2 1 on ImageNet-256 at NFE 25 without guidance. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Flow matching trains a time-dependent velocity field that transports a source distribution to the data distribution (Lipman et al., 2023). Samples are generated by numerically integrating this velocity field, so generation depends not only on the learned field but also on how it is integrated. Recently, Li et al. (2026b) showed that multiplying a learned velocity field vθ by a gain γ(t) during sampling improves generation quality: for unguided SiT-XL/2 (Ma et al., 2024) at NFE 50, 2 Fr´ echet Inception Distance (FID; lower is better) (Heusel et al., 2017) fell from 13.7 to 7.6. Li et al. (2026b) attribute this improvement to a systematic velocity deficit caused by MSE training. We show that MSE itself causes no such deficit: the velocity field that minimizes MSE transports the source distribution to the data distribution exactly. A separate possibility is that finite training leaves the learned field systematically underscaled. For pretrained SiT, however, the scalar multiplier that best matches predicted velocities to target velocities stays near one at every time and averages 0.999 (Section 4). That leaves the central question: why does velocity scaling improve sampled images? We find that sampled states exhibit population time lag : states generated at model time t resemble genuine training states from an earlier time. This lag can be measured directly without a generation-quality metric and increases as NFE decreases (Section 5). Related methods address this state–time mismatch by training the model on states closer to those encountered during sampling (Li et al., 2026a), or by changing the time supplied to the model (Li et al., 2024). Moving model time backward adapts the time input to a lagging state; velocity scaling advances the state toward the time supplied to the next model call. We show that, to first order, velocity scaling advances a lagging state, and empirically find that it eliminates most of the measured lag (Section 5). Velocity scaling makes objects develop earlier along coarse sampling trajectories, closer to their timing under finer sampling (Figure 1). Using the measured lag to choose the gain also sharply improves image quality: at NFE 25, the resulting lag-based gain gives an estimated FID of 12.2 1,down from 28.0 (Section 5.4). A gain sweep lowers FID further to 8.7 at gain 1.15, the lowest-FID gain among those tested. To test whether the two gains follow a simple relation across NFEs, we fit a multiplicative factor κ per model using their values at NFE 25. Reusing this κ at NFE 50, 100, and 250 shows that one fixed factor per model describes the additional gain beyond lag correction reasonably well (Section 7).

# 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 FLOW MATCHING FOR IMAGE GENERATION 

Generative flows learn a velocity field that transports a source distribution to the data distribution. Likelihood-based continuous normalizing flows require numerically integrating the differential equation during training (Chen et al., 2018). Flow Matching avoids this simulation by regressing the velocity field against target velocities from prescribed paths (Lipman et al., 2023). DiT introduced a scalable transformer backbone for diffusion models (Peebles & Xie, 2023). SiT kept the same architecture but placed it in the stochastic-interpolant framework, which supports flow and diffusion objectives and deterministic or stochastic sampling (Ma et al., 2024). REPA improved SiT training by aligning its hidden representations with features from pretrained visual encoders (Yu et al., 2025). We study how inference-time velocity scaling changes the sampling dynamics of these models. 2.2 STATE –TIME MISMATCH 

State–time mismatch occurs when a sampled state differs from the genuine states associated with its model time. Prior work has addressed this mismatch through changes to training or sampling. 

Training corrections. Input Perturbation assumes that Gaussian perturbations of diffusion training states can simulate errors accumulated earlier in sampling. It trains the denoiser on these perturbed states so that it learns to recover from accumulated sampling errors (Ning et al., 2023). MixFlow observes that generated states lag behind their scheduled time, and trains the model at time t on states from earlier times (Li et al., 2026a). DEFAR simulates one inference step during training to expose the model to a state affected by its own sampling error. It trains the model to predict a velocity that moves this state toward the target image (Huang et al., 2026). These methods change the training distribution and thereby bias the learned field toward a chosen model of sampling error. Because the mismatch changes with NFE, solver, and guidance, a single training-time correction must cover several sampling settings or require further training. 2Sampling corrections. Epsilon Scaling is a close diffusion analogue: it divides predicted noise by a factor above one. This scaling is motivated by larger noise-prediction norms on generated than genuine states at the same time. The factor is selected through an FID search (Ning et al., 2024). Time-Shift Sampler uses the variance across pixel and channel values within each generated image to infer its effective diffusion time, then supplies that time to the next model call (Li et al., 2024). 

Removing noise-level conditioning. Another approach removes noise-level conditioning altogether, requiring the denoiser to infer corruption strength from the sampled state. Sun et al. (2025) show that most diffusion, flow-matching, and consistency models remain functional without noise conditioning. Kadkhodaie et al. (2026) study the same blind formulation and connect it directly to state–time mismatch: the denoiser infers the current noise level from the state and follows an implicit schedule, adapting when residual corruption does not match the scheduled level. Equivariant blind denoisers can even generalize to noise levels never seen during training without receiving the test noise level: Mohan et al. (2020) demonstrate this with bias-free, scale-equivariant CNNs, and Saied & Fleuret (2026) extend the same robustness to arbitrary architectures. Velocity scaling addresses the same state–time mismatch by advancing a lagging state toward its scheduled time (Section 5). The lag fit selects a separate correction for each model and sampling setting without retraining, online time estimation, or an image metric. Beyond correcting population time lag, we find that a well-chosen gain can improve sample quality in ways that lag correction alone does not explain (Section 7). 

# 3 SETUP 

Flow matching. For t ∈ [0 , 1] , let X0 be source noise, X1 be data, and U = X1 − X0. The linear training path runs from noise at t = 0 to data at t = 1 :

Xt = (1 − t)X0 + tX 1. (1) The population minimizer of the flow-matching loss is 

v∗(x, t ) = E[U | Xt = x]. (2) We distinguish this population field from the learned velocity field vθ used during sampling. 

Velocity scaling. During sampling, we multiply the model’s velocity prediction by a scalar gain 

γ(t) wherever that prediction enters the sampler: 

vγθ (x, t ) = γ(t)vθ (x, t ). (3) Velocity scaling does not multiply the state or stochastic noise terms. With γ(t) = 1 , the velocity is unchanged; we call this unscaled sampling . Constant gain means γ(t) = c for all t. Li et al. (2026b) use γ(t) = 1 .1 − 0.1t in their Scale Schedule Corrector (SSC). In their SiT-XL/2 NFE-50 ablation, constant gain 1.05 gave FID 7.8 versus 7.6 for SSC, while several schedules with similar mean gain gave FID between 7.5 and 7.8. These results suggest that schedule shape has little effect on FID. We therefore use constant gain unless stated otherwise. 

Experimental scope. Under deterministic and stochastic sampling, we test velocity scaling across SiT model sizes, REPA-SiT, an ADM U-Net, and a decoder-free NCSN++ model trained on CIFAR-10. Appendix C gives the experimental setup. 

# 4 NO VELOCITY DEFICIT 

The population field has no velocity deficit. Whenever target velocity U has nonzero variance given state Xt, Jensen’s inequality implies that its conditional mean has a smaller squared norm than the target itself: ∥E[U | Xt]∥2 < E[∥U ∥2 | Xt]. Li et al. (2026b) interpret this gap as velocity magnitude lost to MSE training. The standard variance identity reveals what this gap actually represents: 

E[∥U ∥2 | Xt] = ∥v∗(Xt, t )∥2 + E[∥U − v∗(Xt, t )∥2 | Xt]. (4) 3The second term measures conditional target variation. It reflects valid training trajectories that cross at state Xt, not missing velocity. The conditional mean v∗ remains the exact population velocity field that transports the source to the data distribution (Lipman et al., 2023). A Gaussian example makes this distinction concrete: conditional target variance is positive, yet gain one gives exact transport, while any other gain changes the endpoint covariance (Appendix B.2). 

The trained field is not systematically underscaled. Although the population MSE solution is exact, finite training could leave the learned field systematically underscaled. We tested this possibility at 20 values of t by fitting the scalar multiplier that minimizes held-out flow-matching error on 16,000 ImageNet image–noise pairs: 

bγFM (t) = E[⟨vθ (Xt, t ), U ⟩ | t]

E[∥vθ (Xt, t )∥2 | t] . (5) At fixed t, the expectations in Equation 5 are over independent draws of X0 from the source distribution and X1 from the data distribution. Under this joint distribution, v∗(Xt, t ) = E[U | Xt],so replacing U with v∗(Xt, t ) leaves the numerator unchanged. Thus bγFM (t) is also the least-squares scalar that makes bγFM (t)vθ best match the population field on genuine training-path states. Its mean is 0.999, and the fitted curve remains near one at every time (Appendix Figure 7). The tested SiT model is therefore not systematically underscaled on genuine training-path states. Together, the population and finite-model results leave open why velocity scaling improves generation quality. One possibility is that sampled states differ from genuine training-path states and that velocity scaling reduces this mismatch. The next section examines this possibility. 

# 5 POPULATION TIME LAG 

We measure population time lag and compare two ways to address it: moving the model’s time input backward and advancing sampled states through velocity scaling. We show that choosing gain from the measured lag improves generation quality. 5.1 MEASURING POPULATION TIME LAG 

Let y denote the image class label. At model time t, let pt(x, y ) be the joint distribution of genuine path states and class labels, and qut (x, y ) the corresponding distribution under unscaled sampling. Kadkhodaie et al. (2026) use a learned noise-level estimator to measure schedule mismatch in diffusion sampling. Similarly, we train a probe bsϕ(x, y ) on genuine path states to estimate path time from the state x and class label y. We define the population time lag measured by probe ϕ as 

bδϕ(t) = E(x,y )∼pt [bsϕ(x, y )] − E(x,y )∼qut [bsϕ(x, y )] . (6) Subtracting the baseline expectation on genuine states ensures that intrinsic probe bias is not counted as sampling lag (Appendix D.2). A positive value means that generated states resemble earlier points along the path than the current solver time t. We use catch-up to mean reducing this population time lag. 

Measuring systematic lag. Even at a fixed true time, probe readings have high variance across genuine states (Appendix D.2). This makes individual lag estimates unreliable, so we average across samples to detect systematic population time lag. 

Validating the probe. We test whether the probe measures movement along a genuine path or simply responds to a change in the state. Replacing each held-out genuine state with the state 0.05 time units earlier on the same path lowers the SiT probe’s mean estimated time by 0.0499, almost exactly the known time change. Moving the original states the same distance in a direction perpendicular to the path lowers the mean estimate by only 0.00519. The probe therefore tracks the known change in path time and responds much less to perpendicular displacements (Appendix D.2). 

Population time lag across models. We measure population time lag across the ImageNet models, but it is much smaller in the CIFAR-10 model (Appendix Table 8). Figure 2 shows how gain changes population time lag along sampled trajectories. 45.2 MODEL -T IME CORRECTION 

Model-time correction replaces the time t passed to the network with t−bδϕ(t). It leaves the integration clock unchanged and does not multiply the velocity. 

Model-time correction improves FID. This model-time correction improves FID without scaling the velocity (at NFE 50, FID falls from 13.8 to 8.8; Appendix D.4). 

Model-time correction transfers to an ADM U-Net. The corresponding model-time correction lowers FID from 22.7 to 16.7 at NFE 50. Constant gain 1.075 reaches FID 13.2, so measured timing affects image quality beyond SiT without accounting for the full scaling benefit (Appendix Table 7). 5.3 VELOCITY SCALING AND POPULATION TIME LAG 

Velocity scaling instead moves the state farther in the current model-velocity direction before the next model call. Consider an Euler step of length h from state xt, with vt = vθ (xt, t ). The solver advances to time t + h, but suppose the updated state resembles a genuine path state xτ at an earlier effective time t < τ < t + h:

xt + hv t ≈ xτ . (7) Under a local first-order approximation of the path, scaling the velocity gives 

xt + γhv t ≈ xt+γ(τ −t) ≈ xt+h when γ(τ − t) ≈ h. (8) The next model call then receives a state that better matches its time input t + h.5.4 SELECTING THE LAG -B ASED GAIN 

We select the lag-based gain , γlag , by searching for the constant gain that minimizes mean squared population time lag along the sampled trajectories. We estimate this gain separately at each NFE for the four SiT sizes, the ADM U-Net, and CIFAR-10 NCSN++. The objective uses neither image decoding nor an image-quality metric. We can calibrate the gain at 0.01 resolution using 256 trajectories per candidate. Appendix E gives the search procedure, cost, and stability checks. When a state lags, the model is called with a time ahead of the state’s effective time. Velocity scaling moves the state forward, reducing this mismatch before subsequent model calls. Frozen-field control. From the same initial state, the frozen-field control follows the velocity predictions recorded along the unscaled trajectory, multiplied by the gain used for full velocity scaling. The model never evaluates the moved control states, excluding the changed-state response (Appendix G.1). Across five SiT checkpoints under stochastic sampling, this control achieves only 22.4% to 24.9% of the FID reduction from full velocity scaling, so most of the benefit requires re-evaluating the model at the moved states. Under deterministic sampling, the control achieves a larger share, reaching 53.9% for SiT and 59.0% for an independently trained ADM U-Net (Dao et al., 2023) (Appendix Table 22). 

Radial control. Re-evaluating the model at moved states does not by itself guarantee better generation quality. Let x be the control’s current state and v = vθ (x, t ) its predicted velocity. The radial control replaces the extra velocity (γ − 1) v with (γ − 1) ∥v∥x/ ∥x∥, while still evaluating the model at the moved states. This control worsens FID, whereas velocity scaling improves it (Appendix G.5). The direction of the extra push therefore matters even when the model is re-evaluated throughout sampling. Velocity scaling advances lagging states toward their scheduled time along the learned velocity (Section 5). The frozen-field control shows that much of the improvement under stochastic sampling requires the model to predict new velocities at these advanced states. The radial control shows that re-evaluation after a different displacement can instead worsen quality. These findings support an explanation in which scaling improves generation by advancing lagging states and then using the learned field at states that better match its time input.

7.1 NUMERICAL ACCURACY AND GAIN 

More accurate integration reduces the gain favored by FID. Across all ImageNet models, the lag-based gain approaches one as NFE increases (Appendix Table 8). The lowest-FID gain also moves toward one in all ImageNet models, while staying at one in the CIFAR-10 model (Figure 3a,d). For SiT-XL/2 at fixed NFE, stochastic Heun reduces both population time lag and the lowest-FID gain relative to Euler (Figure 3b). Although better integration needs less gain, velocity scaling is not simply a correction for integration error; it changes the sampling path (Section 6; Appendix Table 18).

7.3 SCOPE OF GAIN SELECTION 

Guidance. Classifier-free guidance combines predictions made with and without the class label to control how strongly the label affects sampling (Ho & Salimans, 2021). As guidance strength 

ω increases, the lowest-FID gain decreases and crosses below one (Figure 3c). Stronger guidance also lowers the lag-based gain, but the lowest-FID gain falls faster (Appendix I.2). Guidance also increases velocity magnitude, but the measured increase is too small to explain the full decrease in the lowest-FID gain (Appendix I.2). 

Image metrics. Even within one sampler, different image metrics can favor different gains. For SiT-XL/2 at NFE 25, FID favors a higher gain than sFID. That higher gain also raises precision and lowers recall (Appendix Table 25). 

Dataset. The decoder-free NCSN++ model trained on CIFAR-10 provides a setting where velocity scaling is unnecessary (Liu et al., 2023). Across deterministic Euler, adaptive RK45, and pixel-space Euler–Maruyama curves, the lowest measured FID occurs at gain one. The lag-based gain stays close to one at all four NFEs (Appendix E.1). This result matches the catch-up account: population time lag is small and gain above one provides no benefit (Figure 3d). 

# 8 CONCLUSION 

Mean-squared-error training does not create a velocity deficit: the population minimizer is exact, and the learned field shows no systematic norm bias. In coarse samplers, sampled states fall behind their model time, exhibiting population time lag. Velocity scaling advances these lagging states and changes subsequent velocity predictions. Under stochastic sampling, this changed-state response produces most of the FID reduction. Across the tested NFEs, the additional gain favored by FID remains roughly proportional to the lag-based gain. The lowest-FID gain changes with solver, guidance, and image metric, reflecting the sampling and evaluation setting rather than the learned field alone. 9REPRODUCIBILITY STATEMENT

B.1 RESIDUAL TARGET VARIATION AND EXACT TRANSPORT 

Flow matching assigns a target velocity U to each sampled training path. At a fixed state Xt and time 

t, U can still vary. Different training paths can reach the same state. The population MSE field is the mean target velocity at that state and time: 

v∗(Xt, t ) = E[U | Xt, t ]. (11) Define the residual target variation by 

εt = U − v∗(Xt, t ), E[εt | Xt, t ] = 0 . (12) The residual εt is not noise added to the model. It represents the variation in U that Xt and t do not determine. Using U = v∗(Xt, t ) + εt and expanding the squared norm gives Equation 4. The cross term has conditional mean zero. The residual variance therefore equals the gap between the average squared target norm and the squared norm of v∗.

E[∥εt∥2] = E[∥U ∥2] − E[∥v∗(Xt, t )∥2]. (13) It is target variation, not a missing velocity component. The continuity equation shows why this residual is not a transport error. For any smooth test function 

φ with bounded support, 

ddt E[φ(Xt)] = E[∇φ(Xt)⊤U ] (14) 

= E[∇φ(Xt)⊤{v∗(Xt, t ) + εt}] (15) 

= E[∇φ(Xt)⊤v∗(Xt, t )] . (16) The residual contributes zero because its conditional mean is zero. When the continuity equation has a unique solution, v∗ transports the training distribution exactly at every time (Lipman et al., 2023). This residual variation is not a missing velocity component. # D POPULATION TIME LAG AND MODEL -TIME CORRECTION 

This section shows that sampled states lag behind genuine states at the same model time. Velocity scaling reduces this population time lag, and correcting the lag improves image quality. Velocity scaling gives a larger FID reduction than the tested model-time correction. D.1 PROBE ARCHITECTURE AND TRAINING 

Kadkhodaie et al. (2026) train a noise-level estimator to measure schedule mismatch in diffusion sampling. We similarly train a probe on genuine path states to estimate path time: 

xt = (1 − t)x0 + tx 1, (xt, y ) 7 −→ bsϕ(xt, y ) ≈ t. 

The input is a genuine path state xt and its class label y; the output is estimated path time. For unconditional CIFAR-10, we omit y.

Population variation. At a fixed path time, genuine states vary with the data example, source noise, and class label when present. This variation matters because a single probe reading mixes time with ordinary differences between states. We therefore compare average readings across many paths rather than correcting samples one by one. 

Probe architecture. Each probe uses three 3 × 3 stride-2 convolutions with 16, 32, and 64 channels and SiLU activations, followed by global average pooling and a linear head. The head predicts 20 equal-width time bins on [0 , 1] ; its posterior mean over the bin centers gives a time estimate in [0 .025 , 0.975] . ImageNet probes standardize latent channels and concatenate the pooled 64-dimensional feature with a 16-dimensional class embedding before the head. The scale-normalized SiT probe divides each state by its global RMS before channel standardization. The ADM U-Net probe uses the model’s latent space and sampling-time convention. The CIFAR-10 probe uses standardized centered RGB pixels without a class embedding. 

Probe training. We train each probe only on genuine states, using batch size 256 and AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) with learning rate 10 −3 and weight decay 10 −4. We use the final fixed epoch without validation selection. After training, a separate set of genuine states selects one scalar softmax temperature by minimizing cross-entropy on a 401-point log-spaced grid from 0.25 to 4.0. For the ImageNet SiT analysis, training and saved-state evaluation of the probes took about 30 seconds on 16 CPU threads once the states had been prepared.

D.4 MODEL -TIME CORRECTION AND SCALE CONTROL 

Model-time correction improves image quality. At each model call, we replaced t with t − bδϕ(t)

using the population time lag measured by the raw-state probe. We left the integration clock and velocity scale unchanged. At NFE 50, this correction reduced FID from 13.8 to 8.8 under the unguided public hybrid SDE sampler. Both settings used 50,000 images with the same class labels, initial latents, and Brownian paths. 

A separate probe tests the role of global scale. We trained this probe on states divided by their own global RMS and used the same normalization at evaluation. It otherwise uses the same architecture and genuine-state training examples as the raw-state probe. The population time lag remained. On 1,024 unscaled paths from uniform stochastic Euler sampling at NFE 25, the scale-normalized population time lag peaked at 0.0453 and ended at 0.0152 on the unit time scale. At NFE 50, the scale-normalized time correction reduced FID from 13.8 to 8.6 on 50,000 exactly paired images. E.4 CALIBRATION COST 

At SiT-XL/2 NFE 50, direct scalar calibration at gain resolution 0.01 used six candidate gains with 256 paired paths each. This totals 1,536 trajectories and 76,800 model evaluations. All three probe-seed fits reuse these candidate trajectories. Separate validation evaluated identity and the selected gain on 1,280 new paired paths per gain; that work is not part of calibration cost. Replaying 0.01-resolution bisection on the saved lag curves takes 4–9 candidate evaluations across the 24 model/NFE settings, with a median of six. At 256 paths per candidate, this corresponds to 1,024–2,304 trajectories within the already explored gain brackets. These replay counts exclude discovering a bracket. The broader exploration used up to 25 evaluations of 1,280 paths each; that exploration budget is not required by the coarse search. By comparison, one 50,000-image NFE-50 setting used 2.5 million model evaluations and a median 4.7 RTX-4090 hours. The full seven-gain sweep used 33 GPU-hours before image-metric extraction. Scalar calibration requires neither image decoding nor image-feature extraction. Preparing each 16,000-state genuine calibration and held-out set took about 147 seconds on two RTX 4090 GPUs. State preparation and probe training are one-time costs reused across model sizes and NFEs. 27 E.5 CALIBRATION STABILITY

# J SCOPE AND PRACTICAL USE 

For the tested unguided ImageNet models, scalar calibration provides a useful starting gain when decoding or image-metric evaluation is costly. It selects gain from population time lag, and an image-metric search can improve FID further. The changed-state response transfers from SiT to the ADM U-Net under deterministic sampling; model-time correction transfers under stochastic sampling. The CIFAR-10 experiments measure lag and gain curves but do not repeat these causal interventions. The SiT ODE–SDE comparison changes the complete integration rule, so it does not isolate the effect of stochastic noise. 41
