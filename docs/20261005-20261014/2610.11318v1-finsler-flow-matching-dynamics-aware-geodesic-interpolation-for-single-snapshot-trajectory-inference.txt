# Finsler Flow Matching: Dynamics-Aware Geodesic Interpolation for Single-Snapshot Trajectory Inference 

## Niklas Canova Jonas Simon Fleck 

Institute of Human Biology (IHB), Roche, Basel, Switzerland 

niklas.canova@roche.com 

Abstract 

Single-cell snapshot data can resolve a continuum of cellular states but do not uniquely determine the dynamics governing transitions between them. However, additional dynamical information can often be encoded in a cell-cell Markov transition kernel. Existing generative approaches for single cell trajectory inference either infer transport only from population marginals, impose a symmetric geometry on the state space, or incorporate directionality through a single velocity vector at each observed state. We introduce Finsler Flow Matching (FFM), a framework for learning continuous stochastic dynamics from discrete Markov transition graphs. We use the first and second local moments to construct a Finsler structure motivated by the Freidlin–Wentzell action, where the second moment determines anisotropic accessibility and the first moment introduces a preferred direction of motion. We learn neural approximations of the resulting directed geodesics, use their Finsler cost to construct source-target couplings, and define geometry-aware stochastic conditional paths that can be distilled into a continuous generative process through simulation-free score and flow matching. Across synthetic and single-cell trajectory inference benchmarks, FFM improves recovery of withheld intermediate populations, particularly when the transition dynamics are strongly directional or anisotropic. Our results provide a principled route from discrete transition probabilities to continuous generative dynamics while retaining both directional and diffusive structure. ## 1 Introduction 

Understanding how cells change their state in response to intrinsic programs and extrinsic perturbations is a central challenge in biology. Such transitions underlie development and differentiation, but also cellular reprogramming, disease progression, and responses to therapeutic intervention. Single-cell genomics methods have made it possible to probe these processes at high resolution by resolving a continuum of cellular states in a single cross-sectional experiment [44]. However, because single-cell sequencing methods are destructive, individual cells cannot be followed through time and transitions between observed states are not directly measured. As a result, the continuous dynamics underlying cell state changes can generally not be determined from these measurements alone [48]. Several experimental and computational strategies can provide additional information about developmental directionality and dynamics. RNA velocity infers the direction of local transcriptional change [7, 25], lineage tracing tracks ancestry 

Code: https://github.com/mlbelab/FinslerFlowMatching 

1arXiv:2610.11318v1 [cs.AI] 8 Oct 2026 [31, 46, 49] and metabolic or fluorescent labelling can introduce an absolute timescale [6, 11, 17]. Measurements collected across multiple experimental time points also carry temporal information, although the correspondence between cells at successive time points is lost upon sampling and must itself be inferred [38]. Despite their different origins, these sources of dynamical information can often be translated into a common representation, a cell-cell Markov transition kernel, whose entries quantify the likelihood of transitions between observed cellular states [26, 47]. Such kernels have proven highly effective for inferring terminal states, lineage relationships, and fate probabilities [14, 27]. However, the resulting dynamics remain defined on the finite graph of observed cells. A Markov kernel encodes transition preferences between observed states, but this does not specify how the system evolves continuously through state space, particularly between or beyond the observed cells. Generative modeling of population dynamics provides a complementary approach [32]. Given samples at multiple time points, optimal transport formulations recover a coupling between the observed marginals [13, 38], while flow and Schr¨ odinger Bridge matching provide simulation-free frameworks for learning continuous dynamics [29, 37, 42, 43]. Some variants additionally incorporate the geometry of the state or distribution space [12, 18]. Metric Flow Matching replaces Euclidean interpolation with geodesics under a learned Riemannian metric [24], capturing the anisotropic geometry of the data manifold but not a preferred direction of motion. Curly Flow Matching incorporates RNA velocity through a nonzero-drift reference process and can therefore capture directed and non-gradient dynamics [35], but takes as input a single drift vector at each state, and therefore cannot exploit the second-order structure of a local transition distribution. In this paper, we use a discrete Markov transition graph to define a continuous dynamical prior for endpoint-constrained generative transport. We introduce Finsler Flow Matching (FFM), which converts the directional and diffusive structure of a Markov transition graph into a smooth Finsler geometry and uses this geometry to learn continuous stochastic transport through score and flow matching. Under the additional assumption that the Markov chain discretizes an Itˆ o diffusion, the graph-derived local moments recover its drift and diffusion. Freidlin–Wentzell [15, 20] theory then motivates a Randers metric [4], in which local diffusion determines anisotropic accessibility and drift introduces directional preference. We then learn a neural path interpolant to approximate directed Randers geodesics and use the resulting path cost to construct source–target couplings consistent with the transition dynamics. Finally, we use these couplings and geodesic interpolants to construct tractable conditional probability paths and learn an endpoint-constrained stochastic generative process through simulation-free score and flow matching [43], whose local noise geometry is shaped by the graph-derived second moment. To summarize, our main contributions are: • A principled construction of Finsler geometry from Markov graphs. Under a short-time Itˆ o diffusion model, we show that the first and second local moments of a cell-cell Markov kernel recover the drift and diffusion of an underlying stochastic process. We use these estimates to derive a Randers geometry from the Freidlin-Wentzell minimum-action principle [15], jointly encoding anisotropic accessibility and directional dynamics. • A framework for learning continuous stochastic dynamics from discrete Markov graphs. We introduce Finsler Flow Matching, which learns a neural path interpolant under the induced Finsler geometry, uses directed geodesic costs to construct source-target couplings, and defines stochastic conditional paths whose local noise structure is shaped by the graph-derived diffusion. These paths are then distilled into a continuous generative process through simulation-free score and flow matching. • Flexible inference of single-cell trajectory dynamics. By using cell-cell Markov graphs as a common representation of transition dynamics, FFM can incorporate information derived from various experimental and computational sources, including RNA velocity, lineage tracing, labeling experiments, or temporally sampled populations within a unified generative framework. We show that our model outperforms previous methods in intermediate state prediction on various benchmarks.

33 Related Work 

Single-cell trajectory inference. OT methods infer developmental trajectories from populations observed at multiple time points by coupling successive snapshot distributions [9, 38, 41, 50]. Relatedly, Huguet et al. [23] combine manifold-aware OT with neural differential equations to learn continuous cellular dynamics from snapshot data. Complementary approaches such as CellRank construct cell-cell Markov transition kernels from RNA velocity, lineage information, and other dynamical measurements to infer fate probabilities and developmental relationships [26, 47]. FFM builds on these representations by using local transition statistics to construct a smooth directed geometry and continuous generative dynamics beyond the observed cells. 

Stochastic generative transport. Simulation-free Schr¨ odinger bridge methods combine score and flow matching to learn stochastic dynamics between endpoint distributions [43]. Related work studies closed-form Gaussian Schr¨ odinger bridges and smooth Schr¨ odinger bridges for trajectory inference [10, 22]. FFM builds on this framework by using the transition-derived geometry to determine both the interpolating paths and the local anisotropic noise structure. 

Geometric and directional flow matching. Kapusniak et al. [24] replace Euclidean conditional paths with neural approxi-mations of geodesics under a data-dependent Riemannian metric. FFM extends this idea to a directed Randers geometry derived from local transition statistics. Relatedly, Bamberger et al. [3] uses local Markov kernels to construct anisotropic covariance for geometry-aware flow matching, whereas FFM additionally incorporates first-moment directionality through a Randers geometry and geodesic coupling.Petrovi´ c et al. [35] uses a nonzero-drift reference process to model directed and non-gradient dynamics from local velocity fields. FFM instead derives directionality from the first moment of a transition kernel and uses its second moment to capture anisotropic transition structure. 

Lineage-informed Finsler geometry. Zweig et al. [51] recently use Finsler geometry to incorporate directed lineage-tree priors into trajectory inference, penalizing motion inconsistent with a prescribed adjacency between cell types. FFM instead derives its Finsler geometry directly from cell-cell transition probabilities, using the probabilistic transition graph to encode both directional preference and local transition accessibility rather than relying on a categorical lineage prior.

FFM recovers directed and anisotropic transition structure: To test whether FFM can recover intermediate dynamics when both directionality matters and when the data satisfy the manifold hypothesis, we construct the synthetic Sheet benchmark, a saddle-shaped manifold. We score against a narrow corridor, which represents a subset of the manifold corresponding to high probability transition regions. We define a directed transition kernel that drives motion towards that middle corridor before crossing and away from it afterward. Curly-FM receives the first transition moment as its local velocity field, whereas FFM additionally uses the second moment to capture anisotropic accessibility. FFM substantially improves midpoint recovery, achieving W2 = 0 .1588 and 0.1449 for deterministic and stochastic FFM, respectively, compared with 

0.5221 for Curly-FM Fig. 2. We further performed and ablation of the Finsler metric, which demonstrates that both the directional and anisotropic components contribute to this improvement Fig. 4b. On a complementary synthetic bifurcation benchmark, FFM also preserves cell-level fate assignments across both branches Fig. 8. 

FFM improves recovery of pancreatic differentiation in high-dimensional representations: To test whether FFM’s graph-derived geometry improves trajectory reconstruction on real RNA-velocity data, we evaluate FFM on 3,696 pancreatic cells [5] partitioned into latent-time tertiles and withhold the intermediate population, which is enriched for Ngn3-high pre-endocrine cells. FFM receives an RNA-velocity-derived transition kernel computed using Lange et al. [26], whereas Curly-FM receives per-cell velocities. We found that relative performance depended strongly on representation dimensionality. ## 6 Discussion 

We present Finsler Flow Matching (FFM), a framework for transforming discrete Markov transition graphs into continuous stochastic dynamics through a transition-derived Finsler geometry. Its joint first- and second-moment construction captures directional preference and anisotropic accessibility, which guide source–target coupling, geodesic interpolation, and stochastic conditional paths. Our experiments suggest that this is particularly useful for strongly directional or anisotropic dynamics. On the synthetic Sheet benchmark, ablating either component degrades intermediate-state recovery, while on Pancreas the advantage of FFM becomes more pronounced in higher-dimensional representations. The iTracer experiment further demonstrates the flexibility of the Markov transition kernel as an interface between heterogeneous measurements and generative dynamics. Expression, RNA velocity, lineage information, and their combinations can all be incorporated through the same representation. Nevertheless, additional modalities do not uniformly improve recovery. FFM therefore inherits both the information and the limitations of the supplied transition kernel, highlighting the importance of how experimental measurements are translated into transition probabilities. These findings should be interpreted within the scope of the supplied dynamical prior. FFM does not resolve the non-identifiability of cellular dynamics from snapshots alone, and interpreting its geodesics as most-probable transition curves requires the stated diffusion-consistency and small-noise assumptions. Moreover, the Gaussian conditional paths used for stochastic training are tractable approximations rather than exact bridges of the graph-implied reference diffusion. Future work could explicitly model uncertainty in the transition graph and develop richer conditional path families. Another promising direction is conditional FFM, in which experimental covariates or perturbations parameterize the induced geometry, allowing the model to capture condition-specific changes in trajectory dynamics.

Flow Matching: We give a brief introduction to Flow Matching for a more in depth treatment consider Holderrieth and Erives [21]. The idea behind flow matching is to find a vector field that transports one distribution into another and can generate samples by integrating said vector field. More formally one can think of it as trying to find a vector field 

ut : [0 , 1) × Rn → Rn such that 



ddt xt = ut(xt),x0 ∼ p0, x 1 ∼ p1.

Once such a vector field has been learned, samples from p1 can be generated by sampling x0 ∼ p0 and integrating the vector field from t = 0 to t = 1 . In general, this vector field is not unique, which gives us some choice. For example, if p0 = p1 = p,then any vector field satisfying ∇ · (pu ) = 0 leaves the distribution p invariant. More generally, the endpoint distributions p0

and p1 do not uniquely determine the intermediate probability path. We exploit this freedom by choosing a probability density path (pt)t∈[0 ,1] connecting p0 and p1, and then finding a vector field whose flow realizes this path. Even for a fixed path 

pt, such a vector field need not be unique (one could make the choice optimal by choosing ut as the minimizer of some action). In general, we do not have p0 and p1 available in closed form, and hence the corresponding marginal path pt is in-tractable. This is particularly helpful when the vector field measurement is noisy, as is often the case for RNA-velocity estimates.
