Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models excel at instruction following, yet most remain feed-forward: they condition actions only on the current observation and discard both long-horizon temporal con-text and the hierarchical structure of VLM representations. We present PRIME W0 , a hierarchical conditional flow-matching framework that closes this gap through three synergistic designs: (1) per-layer KV injection from a pretrained VLM into an Adaptive MoE action expert; (2) dynamics-aware conditioning via asynchronous world-model subgoal-image prediction and a DINOv2 historical-frame semantic bridge; and (3) Action Dynamic Manifold (ADM) flow matching with covariance-structured noise that respects robot kinematic coupling. PRIME W0 is trained in four stages—VLM pre-training, DAT/NTP visuomotor pre-training, DiT flow-matching decoder assembly with WM injection, and end-to-end SFT—and achieves state-of-the-art results on LIBERO and real-robot benchmarks, with systematic ablations validating each architectural component.

# 1 Introduction 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models [6, 15, 5] have emerged as a dominant paradigm for generalist robot policies, casting manipulation as conditional generation over visual and language tokens. By pretraining on large-scale internet data and fine-tuning on robot demonstrations, VLAs achieve impressive breadth across tasks and embodiments. Recent flow- and diffusion-based VLAs further improve action expressiveness by replacing autoregressive decoders with continuous generative processes, enabling multi-modal action distri-butions and smoother trajectories [5]. 

The dynamics–semantics gap. Despite these advances, most VLA policies remain feed-forward : at each control step they condition action generation only on the current observation and instruction, without an explicit model of how the scene will evolve. This creates two coupled limitations. First, temporal myopia :long-horizon tasks with occlusion, contact ambiguity, or multi-phase manipulation require tracking motion trends and anticipating near-future scene layout, yet repeating the full VLM on every historical frame is prohibitively expensive. Second, semantic compression : many architectures route action decoding through a single compressed VLM representation, discarding the hierarchical semantic–spatial structure accumulated across intermediate layers that is critical for fine-grained motor control. Flow-matching action decoders exacerbate both issues. During denoising, early steps benefit from high-level task semantics while late steps require precise perceptual grounding and kinematically consistent trajectories. A policy that conditions only on the present frame and a collapsed VLM summary cannot simultaneously supply forward temporal anchors and layer-wise semantic detail.

# 2 Related Work 

## 2.1 Vision-Language-Action Models 

Recent vision-language-action (VLA) foundation models have moved beyond task-specific visuomotor policies toward scalable, generalist, and deployment-oriented robot control. π0.7 [14] proposes a steerable general-ist robotic foundation model that uses diverse context conditioning, including language commands, task-performance metadata, and subgoal images, to exploit demonstrations, autonomous rollouts with failures, and non-robot data. LingBot-VLA [35] emphasizes practical deployment by training on large-scale real-world data from multiple dual-arm robot configurations and improving adaptation cost, training throughput, open code, and benchmark construction. JoyAI-RA [39] studies multi-source and multi-level pretraining by inte-grating web data, egocentric human manipulation videos, simulation-generated trajectories, and real-robot data, with explicit action-space unification to bridge embodiment gaps. PokeVLA [41] takes a complementary 

> 2

lightweight direction, pretraining a compact VLM with spatial grounding, affordance, and embodied reason-ing data, and then injecting manipulation-relevant representations into the action space through goal-aware semantics learning, geometry alignment, and an action expert. Another emerging trend is to make VLA models more suitable for long-horizon, real-time, and reasoning-intensive robotic autonomy. Xiaomi-Robotics-0 [8] focuses on fast and smooth real-time execution by ad-dressing inference latency through asynchronous execution and temporal alignment of consecutive predicted action chunks. Habilis-β [29] pushes VLA deployment toward fast-motion and long-lasting on-device control, introducing continuous-run evaluation metrics such as Tasks per Hour and Mean Time Between Intervention to measure both productivity and reliability. HALO [31] incorporates embodied multimodal chain-of-thought reasoning through textual task reasoning, visual subgoal prediction, and action prediction. These works indi-cate that recent VLA research is shifting from direct observation-to-action mapping toward foundation models with richer conditioning, stronger cross-embodiment generalization, spatial and affordance-aware representa-tions, deployment-aware execution, and explicit reasoning or visual foresight. Nevertheless, most existing VLA systems still encode future physical consequences implicitly or through compact subgoal representa-tions, whereas our work is motivated by using an explicit video world model to generate task-conditioned future observations for downstream VLM-based action generation. 

## 2.2 World Models and Video Prediction 

Recent progress in video generation and self-supervised video representation learning has made world mod-els increasingly attractive for embodied intelligence. Unlike conventional VLA policies that map current observations directly to actions, world models aim to capture how scenes evolve over time, thereby pro-viding predictive priors about object motion, contact dynamics, and task progress. V-JEPA 2 [3] shows that self-supervised pretraining on internet-scale video can produce representations useful for understanding, prediction, and robotic planning, and further adapts the model into an action-conditioned world model for zero-shot image-goal planning. LingBot-World [34] pushes open-source video-based world simulation toward high-fidelity, long-horizon, and real-time interactive generation. DreamDojo [11] further demonstrates the po-tential of large-scale egocentric human videos for robot world modeling by learning from 44k hours of human interaction videos and introducing continuous latent actions as unified proxy actions. After post-training on target robot data, DreamDojo supports controllable simulation of contact-rich manipulation and enables applications such as live teleoperation, policy evaluation, and model-based planning. A closely related direction is World-Action Models (WAMs), which jointly model future visual dynamics and robot actions. DreamZero [37] argues that world-action models can act as zero-shot policies by learn-ing physical dynamics through joint video-action prediction, achieving strong generalization to unseen tasks, environments, and embodiments. Motus [4] proposes a unified latent action world model with a Mixture-of-Transformers architecture, integrating understanding, video generation, and action experts within a flex-ible generative framework. MotuBrain [33] extends this line with unified multiview modeling, shared cross-embodiment action representations, and efficient deployment recipes for long-horizon control. Genie Envi-sioner [20] presents a unified world foundation platform that combines instruction-conditioned video genera-tion, flow-matching action decoding, neural simulation, and benchmarking. Cosmos Policy [17] fine-tunes a pretrained video model into a robot policy by representing robot actions, future states, and values as latent frames inside the video diffusion process, enabling both visuomotor control and test-time planning. These works suggest that video prediction can serve not only as an auxiliary simulation tool, but also as a scalable substrate for learning action-conditioned physical representations. Recent studies further examine whether explicit future video generation is necessary during deployment. Fast-WAM [38] separates the benefit of video co-training from test-time future imagination and shows that competitive performance can be achieved without generating future frames at inference, suggesting that video modeling may primarily improve world representations during training. GigaWorld-Policy [36] similarly adopts an action-centered WAM design, using video generation as a training-time constraint while making future-video generation optional at inference for faster deployment. Being-H0.7 [22] moves future-aware reasoning into a latent space by aligning deployable latent queries with future-observation-informed posterior representations, thereby retaining predictive benefits without pixel-space rollout. DiT4DiT [23] takes another route by coupling a video Diffusion Transformer with an action Diffusion Transformer and using intermediate 

> 3

denoising features, rather than fully reconstructed future frames, as temporally grounded conditions for action prediction. In contrast to methods that remove or bypass explicit future generation at test time, our work explores a complementary direction: using a video world model to produce task-conditioned future observations and feeding them back into a VLM-action pipeline, so that action generation can be guided by explicit visual imagination of future task evolution. 

## 2.3 World Models for VLA Policy Learning 

Recent work has begun to integrate world models more tightly with vision-language-action (VLA) policies, rather than treating them as separate simulators or standalone video predictors. WorldVLA [10] proposes an autoregressive action world model that unifies action generation and future-image prediction in a single framework, where the world model learns environment dynamics to improve action generation and the action model in turn supports visual understanding for world modeling. RynnVLA-002 [9] further develops this uni-fied VLA–world-model formulation by jointly learning future image states and subsequent actions, showing that the integrated world model can substantially improve real-world VLA success rates. GigaBrain-0 [32] takes a data-centric route, using world models to generate diverse training data, including video generation, real-to-real transfer, human-transfer, view-transfer, and sim-to-real-transfer data, thereby reducing the de-pendence on expensive real-robot data while improving generalization across appearance, object placement, and camera-view variations. These works indicate that world models can enhance VLA policies not only by predicting future observations, but also by providing scalable data, forward scene predictions, and richer supervision for action learning. Another line of work uses world models as interactive environments or reward models for post-training VLA policies. NORA-1.5 [13] augments a VLA backbone with a flow-matching action expert and further improves reliability through preference optimization, where rewards are derived from an action-conditioned world model and action-quality heuristics. VLA-RFT [18] performs reinforcement fine-tuning with verified rewards in a world simulator, allowing VLA policies to obtain dense trajectory-level supervision without costly real-world interaction. World-VLA-Loop [21] extends this idea into a closed-loop framework that jointly refines the video world model and the VLA policy: policy failures are fed back to improve action-outcome alignment in the world model, which then provides better virtual rollouts for subsequent policy optimization. VLA-MBPO [40] further studies practical model-based reinforcement learning for VLA models by adapting unified multimodal models for data-efficient world modeling, enforcing multi-view consistency through interleaved view decoding, and using chunk-level branched rollouts to mitigate compounding errors. Together, these methods show that world models are becoming a key infrastructure for VLA post-training, preference learning, and sample-efficient reinforcement fine-tuning. While these approaches demonstrate the benefit of world models for VLA policy learning, they mainly use world models for joint VLA–world-model training, synthetic data generation, reward construction, or virtual rollouts during post-training. Our work explores a complementary direction: using a video world model as an explicit future-imagination module inside the VLA inference pipeline. Given the current observation and task-level context, the world model predicts task-conditioned future observations, which are then encoded by a VLM and consumed by an action expert for downstream continuous action generation. In this way, future visual prediction is not only used as an offline training signal or simulator, but also becomes an explicit intermediate representation for action reasoning and execution.

## 3.2 Four-Stage Training Pipeline 

PRIME W0 is trained in four progressive stages that build from general vision–language understanding to a fully integrated continuous control policy (fig. 2). 

Stage 1: VLM Pre-training. We start from (or continue) large-scale vision–language pre-training of the back-bone FVLM on multimodal corpora (captioning, visual question answering, and related alignment objectives). This stage establishes semantic grounding that subsequent robot stages must preserve rather than overwrite. 

Stage 2: Action Tokenization & NTP Pre-training. We train a geometry-aware discrete action tokenizer T

(DAT) on the robot demonstration corpus (section 3.3), then pre-train the VLM with a next-token prediction (NTP) objective over unified visual–language–action token sequences (section 3.4). Mixed VL–robot co-training maintains language competence while endowing the model with broad visuomotor understanding across embodiments and tasks. 

Stage 3: DiT / Flow-Matching Pre-training with WM Injection. We freeze the VLM backbone and re-place the autoregressive action head with a continuous flow-matching action decoder (DiT-style MoT / Adaptive MoE experts; sections 3.7 to 3.9). The expert receives per-layer VLM KV representations through cross-attention (eq. (1)). A historical-frame encoder (section 3.6) supplies long-horizon visual context, and a compact world model W is integrated via asynchronous subgoal-image injection (section 3.5; architec-ture in section A): predicted subgoal frames from ot−1 are encoded as visual tokens and fused into the VLM sequence at timestep t without blocking on future-frame capture. Stage 3 optimises the flow-matching loss 

LF M with MoE load-balancing Lbal while denoising continuous actions (and associated state tokens) under world-model conditioning.

## 3.3 Training Data Mixture 

Robot demonstration data for Stages 2–4 form a heterogeneous mixture of approximately 10,000 hours 

spanning laboratory teleoperation, portable egocentric collection, and simulation (fig. 3). • Teleop data (60%, ∼6,000 h). Demonstrations from laboratory setups, industrial data-collection facto-ries, and curated public robot datasets. These typically provide multi-view RGB (including third-person cameras) with proprioception and language annotations, and form the primary source of high-quality expert trajectories. • UMI data (30%, ∼3,000 h). Egocentric trajectories collected with portable UMI-style handheld interfaces and fisheye wrist cameras. Relative to teleop, UMI increases spatial/scene diversity at the cost of missing fixed third-person views; during training we randomly mask third-person inputs so that the policy learns under both multi-view and ego-only observation regimes. • Simulation data (10%, ∼1,000 h). Procedurally reconstructed scenarios with randomised objects and physics parameters, used to densify coverage of contact-rich and long-horizon behaviours that are expen-sive to collect physically. All sources are mapped to a shared Cartesian / SE(3) end-effector action space before DAT tokenisation (section 3.4), enabling joint training without embodiment-specific heads.

FAST [25] improves upon binning by applying DCT + BPE for high compression, but its variable-length output com-plicates batching during NTP training, and it does not incorporate task-aware geometric constraints. ## 3.5 World Model and Asynchronous Subgoal-Image Injection 

Introduced in Stage 3, the world model supplies forward scene priors to the VLM by predicting explicit subgoal images and injecting them asynchronously as visual tokens (eq. (2)); see section A for architectural details. 

Motivation. A purely feed-forward VLA policy conditions only on the current frame , lacking an internal model of environmental dynamics . This deficiency degrades phase consistency in long-horizon settings with occlusion or ambiguous contact. We therefore introduce a lightweight World Model (WM) that predicts the 

subgoal RGB observation the scene should evolve toward at the next timestep. Unlike latent-only world models, W operates directly in image space: its output is decoded into a full visual frame, re-encoded by the shared vision encoder, and injected into the VLM as standard image tokens—preserving interpretability and alignment with the VLM’s native visual interface.

To endow the policy with temporal dynamics awareness without re-running the full VLM on every past frame, we introduce a dedicated historical-frame encoder . Rather than reusing shallow features from the current-frame pathway, this module transforms cached past observations into semantic representations compatible with the flow-matching action decoder. 

DINOv2 feature extraction. For the buffered historical observations {ot−τ , . . . , ot−1}, we first extract robust visual descriptors with a frozen pre-trained DINOv2 encoder Edino :

Vision–language semantic bridging. Because the DINOv2 feature space differs from the VLM’s language-model embedding space, we apply a lightweight projection to map historical visual features into token repre-sentations that the action decoder can consume alongside instruction embeddings:

This vision–language bridging mechanism allows the action-generation module to exploit long-range temporal dependencies en-coded in past frames while avoiding the computational cost of repeatedly forwarding historical images through the full VLM backbone. 

Integration with the action decoder. The historical tokens Whist are concatenated with current image tokens, instruction tokens, and WM subgoal-image tokens (eq. (14)) and passed as conditioning context to the flow-matching action expert. Within the Adaptive MoE stack, the aggregated history vector ¯ht−τ :t−1

## 3.7 Hierarchical Temporal-Gated Adaptive MoE Action Experts 

The Adaptive MoE module forms the action-generation backbone introduced in Stage 3, specialising be-haviour across manipulation phases while reading per-layer VLM representations via cross-attention; decoder hyperparameters are summarised in section C. 

Design motivation. In conventional VLA architectures, action decoding typically shares the same FFN and attention parameters as vision–language reasoning, which limits dynamic behavioural specialisation across manipulation phases (approach, contact, fine adjustment, transfer). ## 3.9 Action Dynamic Manifold 

Action Dynamic Manifold (ADM) unifies the flow-matching decoder, cross-attention conditioning, and ODE inference introduced in Stages 3–4 into a single dynamic manifold-constrained action generation framework. 

Action manifold hypothesis. We adopt the Action Manifold Hypothesis : feasible robot action se-quences do not uniformly populate the full Euclidean space RH·da , but instead lie near a low-dimensional smooth manifold M ⊂ RH·da shaped by physical laws, task constraints, and contact dynamics. The learning objective should therefore be understood as projecting noisy samples onto M, rather than blindly denoising in the ambient space. Under this view, ADM is equivalent to conditional flow matching that directly regresses clean actions—the network output can be interpreted as a prediction on the manifold, while the velocity loss implicitly encodes the geodesic projection dynamics from noise to M.

# 5 Discussion 

Why do temporal behaviours emerge? When the policy is conditioned on both historical tokens Whist 

and WM-predicted subgoal images bo (wm ) 

> t

, the learned mapping π(At | ot, ℓ, Whist , bo (wm ) 

> t

) must remain con-sistent across varying temporal contexts and forward scene predictions. This temporal consistency constraint 

discourages memorising isolated observation–action pairs and encourages policies that track phase, anticipate contact, and modulate motion dynamics. Behaviours such as subgoal-aligned control, reduced action jerk, and phase-dependent MoE routing arise as efficient solutions to this constrained learning problem rather than from task-specific supervision. 

Relationship to dynamics-aware manipulation. PRIME W0 represents a step toward dynamics-grounded generalist manipulation : policies that reason about how scenes evolve rather than mapping static appearances to actions. This is complementary to purely feed-forward VLAs and to methods that use world models only as offline data generators. By injecting explicit subgoal images asynchronously, PRIME W0 re-tains the interpretability of visual foresight while preserving real-time deployability—the WM branch overlaps with current-frame processing rather than serialising inference. 

Limitations. 

1. Subgoal-image fidelity : ADM and the VLM depend on W producing visually coherent subgoal frames. For rapidly changing dynamics or scenes far from the WM training distribution, misaligned subgoals may mislead the policy. 

> 17

2. Historical window length : the DINOv2 historical encoder captures a fixed temporal window τ ; very long-horizon tasks may still require explicit memory mechanisms beyond cached frames. 3. MoE routing stability : although temporal gating mitigates expert collapse, extremely imbalanced task distributions can still skew expert utilisation despite Lbal .4. Tactile and force feedback : PRIME W0 infers dynamics from vision and proprioception only; integrating wrist force-torque or tactile sensing could improve contact-rich manipulation. 

Future directions. Promising extensions include end-to-end differentiable subgoal prediction, scaling WM capacity with stronger video foundation models, combining Whist with learned episodic memory, and extend-ing ADM to whole-body humanoid platforms where kinematic coupling is even more pronounced. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We introduced PRIME W0, a hierarchical conditional flow-matching VLA that couples per-layer VLM KV injection, asynchronous world-model subgoal-image prediction, historical-frame semantic bridging, and Adaptive MoE / ADM action generation on a covariance-structured action manifold. A four-stage recipe (fig. 2) progressively builds VLM grounding, DAT+NTP visuomotor competence on a ∼10,000-hour teleop / UMI / simulation mixture (fig. 3), a DiT-style flow-matching action expert with frozen-backbone pre-training, and end-to-end SFT. At inference, WM subgoal generation runs asynchronously from ot−1, so the policy remains real-time while still conditioning on explicit forward scene priors. 

PRIME W0 achieves state-of-the-art results on LIBERO and an in-house real-robot suite. Ablations confirm that DAT+NTP, manifold-constrained flow matching with correlation priors, WM subgoal injection, historical bridging, and Adaptive MoE each contribute measurably to long-horizon and contact-rich performance. We argue that closing the loop between semantic reasoning, dynamics foresight, and manifold-constrained control is a promising path toward robotic policies that are simultaneously instruction-grounded, temporally coherent, and physically legitimate.

# A World Model Architecture 

Our world model Φwm is a conditional image/video generator trained on human-centric and robot manipula-tion footage. At inference it takes the previous multi-view observation ot−1, optional proprioceptive history, and language instruction ℓ, and predicts a subgoal RGB image bo (wm ) 

> t

∈ RH×W ×3 at the current timestep (eq. (12)). Architecturally, Φwm comprises a spatio-temporal VAE encoder–decoder paired with a compact flow-based denoiser (5M–300M parameters in our scaling study). The decoder outputs full visual frames rather than abstract latent codes; frames are re-encoded by the shared vision encoder Eϕ before injection into the VLM token sequence (eq. (14)). WM inference is launched asynchronously from ot−1 while ot is captured and prefilled, so end-to-end latency is approximately max( Twm , T vision ).

# B Historical Frame Encoder 

The historical-frame encoder (section 3.6; hyperparameters in section B) uses a frozen DINOv2 backbone 

Edino to extract patch features from {ot−τ , . . . , ot−1}, followed by a two-layer projection with LayerNorm to produce historical language tokens Whist (eq. (16)). We use τ =8 frames at the control frequency of the target platform. The projection dimensions match the VLM embedding width ( d=4096 in our implementation). Only the projection layers are trained; DINOv2 weights remain frozen.
