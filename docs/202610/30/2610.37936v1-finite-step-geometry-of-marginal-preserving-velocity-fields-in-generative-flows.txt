# Abstract 

Generative flows can admit different veloc-ity fields that induce the same continuous marginal evolution but behave differently af-ter numerical discretization. We study this finite-step non-equivalence while keeping the marginal path and numerical method fixed. Projected modified equations characterize the component of local integration error that changes the marginal law and give sufficient conditions for higher marginal convergence order. For affine fixed-shape distributions, we characterize exact finite-step reachability and derive spectral obstructions and sharp nearest-reachable Wasserstein errors. For dif-fusion probability-flow ODEs, we construct skew-score perturbations that preserve the exact marginal path under exact scores and quantify their leakage under score error. Con-trolled Gaussian and mixture experiments re-cover the predicted order, reachability, and obstruction phenomena. 

# 1 WHY THE VELOCITY FIELD STILL MATTERS 

A generative flow turns a simple random input into a sample by following a velocity field. Diffusion probability-flow ODEs and flow-matching models im-plement this idea with a neural network (Song et al., 2021; Lipman et al., 2023; Albergo et al., 2025): ˙Xt = vt(Xt), X0 ∼ ρ0.

Here ρt denotes the distribution of particles at time 

t. Sampling requires a numerical approximation to this motion, and each velocity evaluation usually costs one network call. With only a few calls, the numerical distribution can differ substantially from the intended one. Can we improve that approximation by changing the velocity without changing the intended continuous dis-tributions ? A distribution records probability mass, not particle identities. Different motions can therefore produce the same continuous distributions but different numerical outputs.

# 2 WHAT IT MEANS TO KEEP THE DISTRIBUTION FIXED 

The Gaussian rotation illustrates a general constraint. The continuity equation describes how a velocity redis-tributes probability mass: 

∂tρt + div( ρtvt) = 0 . (5) Adding ut leaves this equation unchanged exactly when div( ρtut) = 0 . (6) The quantity ρtut is a probability current; zero diver-gence means that this additional motion creates no net local gain or loss of mass. We call it a marginal gauge .Under the regularity and transport-uniqueness assump-tions stated in Section A, v + u then produces the same continuous distributions as v. The constraint must hold throughout the interval, not just at its first node. A correction need not move along density contours to satisfy it. 

The numerical response is simply the output distribution. Start a step with particles distributed as ρt, apply the chosen numerical map Ψ v+ut,h , and inspect their new distribution: 

Rt,h (u) = (Ψ v+ut,h )#ρt. (7) Ewsey Obzherin, Valentin Khrulkov 

The symbol # ρt means the distribution obtained by applying the map to a draw from ρt. Exact integration always returns the prescribed ρt+h for an admissible correction. One numerical step need not. A low-rank restriction or an amplitude cap limits which responses are available. We use this single output map in three ways. At small 

h, we ask what its leading distributional error is. At a fixed h, we ask whether any allowed correction reaches 

ρt+h exactly. If it cannot, we minimize the distance to that target. We use W2, the Wasserstein distance that measures the least root-mean-square displacement needed to transport one distribution to another. The formal reachable-set and minimization definitions are in Section A.1. 

Why local success is not enough. Later steps can amplify local error. Under Lipschitz stability, the final transport error is bounded by local errors weighted by their subsequent amplification (Section A.2). This mo-tivates separate local, own-field integration and image-quality checks. 

# 3 WHICH INTEGRATION ERRORS CHANGE THE LAW? 

For general smooth fields, we test whether the omitted motion changes probability mass. 

Euler misses acceleration, but not all accelera-tion matters. For w = v + u, the exact trajectory begins as x + hw + h2aw/2 + O(h3), where 

aw = ∂tw + ( ∇w)w (8) is the acceleration along the trajectory. Euler keeps only x + hw . Nevertheless, its leading distributional error depends only on the weighted divergence of the omitted acceleration: (Ψ wh, E)#ρt − ρt+h = h2

2 div( ρtaw) + O(h3). (9) This expansion is weak: it compares expectations of smooth test functions. If div( ρtaw) = 0, the leading particle error merely rearranges mass and the leading density error disappears. We can separate any field into a component that changes the distribution and an orthogonal component that only rearranges it. Write Π ρ for the projection onto the former, the closure of gradient fields in L2(ρ). For Euler the relevant component is Πρt aw. (10) It can vanish even when the full acceleration is nonzero. The weighted projection also supplies the local Wasser-stein error coefficient (Benamou and Brenier, 2000; Ambrosio et al., 2008); its construction and norm iden-tity appear in Section A. 

The same idea applies beyond Euler. A mod-ified equation represents a numerical method as the exact flow of a nearby velocity, expanded in powers of h (Hairer et al., 2006). Its successive coefficients 

Epmeth , E pmeth +1 , . . . describe errors introduced by a method of classical order pmeth . We test each coeffi-cient with the same mass-conservation condition as in Eq. (6). 

Order result. If the coefficients Epmeth , . . . , E q−1 all satisfy div( ρtEj ) = 0, the local law error is O(hq+1 )and stable repeated steps have terminal W2 error O(hq ). This statement holds for a smooth, uniformly positive density path on a flat torus, with a uniformly controlled modified expansion, sufficient map agreement and Lip-schitz stability. Section A.3 states these conditions and the leading error coefficient precisely; Corollary 8 quantifies the required accuracy of approximate cancel-lation. These are sufficient conditions, not a general existence theorem for a correction. For the expanding Gaussian, a fixed rotation attains local h3 law error, and a moment lower bound excludes local O(h4) for any fixed finite-energy lift (Proposition 31). This sharp local result does not forbid further terminal cancellation. The method’s classical order is unchanged. 

Controlled check: state error versus law error. 

Expansion–rotation tests give trajectory and marginal tail slopes 0 .984 and 1 .968, matching first-order particle and second-order law error. A separate fixture com-bines large full acceleration with negligible projected acceleration, showing why curvature is not a law-error proxy (Sections A.5 and D.4). 

Two questions must remain separate. An or-der claim requires estimates uniform in h, whereas fixed-step exactness may select a separate field for each budget. An h-dependent family yields an order guar-antee when it satisfies the uniform hypotheses and cancellation budgets of Corollary 8. A budget-specific exact correction alone does not supply those estimates. One can select a correction by minimizing the squared size of the projected leading error (Eq. (35)). Afinite dictionary may only observe or cancel part of this quantity, however; an unobserved remain-der can remain. The optimization, approximation and unresolved-projection conditions are given in Sec-tion A.4. Next we study a family where the complete

# 4 WHEN CAN ONE STEP BE EXACT? 

Rotation repaired the two-dimensional expanding Gaus-sian. It cannot repair every target: rotations act in planes, and this puts a concrete restriction on the changes they can make. We derive exact feasibility and the smallest remaining error for affine fixed-shape distributions. 

A family whose shape can be tracked exactly. 

Write ρt = L(mt + Σ 1/2

Gaussian and normalized fixed-degree Student families with fi-nite covariance are examples. Arbitrarily changing a distribution’s radial shape is outside this family. An affine velocity must satisfy two requirements: its instantaneous covariance change must agree with the prescribed path, and its Euler endpoint must have the target covariance. Matching one does not guarantee the other. Theorem 12 gives a necessary-and-sufficient ma-trix criterion for the general case, including the separate mean condition mt+h = mt + h ˙mt. This is a feasibility equivalence, not a general efficient anisotropic solver. We specialize to a covariance-I source. 

Rotation adds expansion in pairs. If the instan-taneous covariance grows equally in every direction, the affine generator has the form

Exactness result. A positive target covariance Σ ⋆ is reachable exactly if and only if every target variance is at least a2 and every variance strictly above a2 occurs with even multiplicity (Section B.1). When an isotropic target radius exceeds |a|, all directions can be corrected exactly in even dimension; odd dimension leaves one direction unpaired. At equality no rotation is needed in either dimension. A target smaller than the Euler radius cannot be repaired by adding rotation. This is a single-step obstruction: for odd d ≥ 3, feasible affine fields βI + K(t) can match an expanding spherical terminal law in three Euler steps, but not one or two (Proposition 16). Intermediate laws need not match.

# 5 WHY THE CHOICE OF SOLVER MATTERS 

The continuous path fixes admissibility, but the solver fixes the response: a correction that repairs Euler need not repair Heun on the same timesteps. In a constant two-dimensional expansion–rotation block, write the generator as λI + ωJ . The exact flow multiplies the radius by ehλ . A numerical method instead multiplies it by |R(hλ + ihω )|, where R is its scalar amplification function: R(z) = 1 + z for Euler and R(z) = 1 + z + z2/2 for Heun. Exact marginal matching is precisely 

|R(hλ + ihω )| = ehλ . (14) Changing the method changes the useful frequencies. Bounded exact branches exist for small h, with limits 

|λ| for Euler and |λ|/√3 for Heun (Corollary 26); they still vary with h. The comparison is with the intended expansion ehλ , rather than with unit amplitude as in an absolute-stability test. This argument extends to constant generators of the whole path L(etS Z) when the rotations commute with the symmetric growth matrix S. Within each growth eigenspace, planar rotations must satisfy Eq. (14); a leftover zero-frequency direction must already have the correct scalar multiplier. Consequently an odd-dimensional growth block can be obstructed even when the total dimension is even. Theorem 25 gives the complete classification. The response formula and derivative-kernel analysis are in Section C and Theo-rem 27. These conclusions concern frozen autonomous linear fields. Nonlinear, time-varying fields require the actual multistage map, not a Jacobian in R. Frequencies of order 1 /h fall outside the fixed-field order theorem (Section 3). 

Controlled check: native fitting versus solver transfer. Across ten independently refit Digits la-tent PCA/class-Gaussian models ( d = 16, Euler with four field calls), finite-step calibration reduces pre-scribed class-scale RMSE to 0 .0238 ± 0.0010, from uncorrected Base 0 .461 ± 0.002 and leading-order cali-bration 0 .174 ± 0.002 (mean ± standard error across refits); the independent sampling floor is 0 .0262 ±

0.0013. Under two-step Heun, the transferred Eu-ler correction gives 0 .773 ± 0.005, versus native Heun 0.0252 ± 0.0019 and Base 0 .134 ± 0.002. This is a con-trolled latent-distribution experiment, not an image-generation benchmark (Section C.4).

Calibration itself has a finite-step limitation. 

The implemented objective compares paired feature means from common starting particles. With fixed features and iid input pairs, its expected squared dis-crepancy contains both the population mean error and a batch-variance term. In the expanding Gaussian of Section 1, the exact useful rotation can therefore lose to zero under a finite batch. At h = .1, batch size 1000 alone selects frequency .68280, while a population en-ergy penalty 10 −5 alone selects .68214; combining them selects zero. Section F.3 gives the exact joint thresh-old (Proposition 42) and compact limit (Corollary 43). This is a controlled calculation for fixed features, not a reconstruction of a neural fit. A low logged loss need not recover the best law-preserving correction. The relevant scales differ. For bounded rotation fre-quencies, the squared population feature discrepancy is 

O(h4), whereas the extra paired-minibatch contribution at a useful rotation is of order h2/n b. A fixed energy penalty can dominate both as the step shrinks. The compact limit in Corollary 43 expresses the joint effect through 1 /(nbh2) and λh/h 4 (Fig. 3). Its hypotheses include uniform convergence and a global optimization tolerance of o(h4). Thus the calibration analysis ex-plains an objective-level obstruction without equating a training history, its selected-amplitude field and an asymptotic optimum.

# 8 CONCLUSIONS 

With the marginal path and solver fixed, our results give sufficient order conditions, exact affine reachabil-ity criteria and sharp nearest-reachable errors. On pretrained models, gains depend on the solver, time window and call budget. Independent native-window fits can succeed in regimes where redistributing an ex-isting profile does not; improved own-field integration still need not improve state error or FID. Continuous marginal freedom is inherited. The weighted gradient/solenoidal geometry is classical in Wasserstein analysis (Ambrosio et al., 2008). For diffusion marginals, we use the formulation of Horvat and Pfister (2024) and observable score-error bounds of Khelifa et al. (2026). Antisymmetric invariant-law currents appear in Ma et al. (2015). EDDY’s revised Stein-operator analysis conditions its no-bias claim on batch independence and charges bias to dependence (Vinograd et al., 2026).

ECCV Section 5/supplement and journal Section 8.4 report no clear acceleration from these solenoidal ablations, distinct from matrix-preconditioner gains. We fit low-rank temporal fields to a finite-step distributional proxy and test solver/window transfer. 

Other uses of field freedom. Flux Matching fixes a time-dependent marginal path by matching reference weighted-flux divergence (their Appendix E, Eqs. (53)–(56)); their Appendix C.4 proposes divergence-free score-model augmentation and their Appendix C.2 discusses field-dependent finite-step behavior at constrained boundaries (Pao-Huang et al., 2026). Helmholtz-SDE optimizes marginal-compatible stochastic paths for variational inference (Smith et al., 2026); Gauge Flow Models learn a bundle architecture rather than impose our weighted-solenoidal constraint (Strunk and Assam, 2025). Vuong et al. (2025) study learned diffusion fields as gradient flows. 

Distributional numerical error is not a new subject. Finite-time weak expansions (Talay and Tubaro, 1990), weak modified equations and higher-order invariant-measure sampling (Debussche and Faou, 2012; Abdulle et al., 2014; Laurent and Vilmart, 2022; Bronasco, 2025) are classical. Hurault et al. (2026) derive exact-score Euler–Maruyama weak/Fr´ echet errors, including the ODE limit; Pierret and Galerne (2025) compute Gaussian diffusion solutions and Wasserstein discretization errors. Irreversible dynamics and numerical preservation. Leli` evre et al. (2013) optimize Gaussian-invariant nonreversible linear drifts for convergence to equilibrium; Duncan et al. (2017) analyze splitting bias and asymptotic variance. Continuous-target preservation therefore need not preserve discrete sampling. The B-series volume-preservation impossibility, apart from the exact-flow series, quantifies over all divergence-free fields (Iserles et al., 2007); our reachability question instead selects a field for one specified law/path. 

The closest Gaussian finite-step comparison. Claustre et al. (2026) study commuting Gaussian schedules, a necessary Euler drift bound and stable-convex-path exactness. Changing their variance path changes drift; factorization at a fixed path changes regression variance without changing drift. We vary skew motion within a prescribed affine fixed-shape path, characterizing its numerical orbit, target-law intersection and nearest errors under rank/norm budgets. Bures–Wasserstein identities are standard (Gelbrich, 1990; Bhatia et al., 2019). 

Other numerical design variables. Transport, rectification and minibatch coupling change transport geometry (Khrulkov et al., 2023; Lavenant and Santambrogio, 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Pooladian et al., 2023; Bansal et al., 2026). Strain/vorticity analysis links Jacobians to truncation error/stability without our fixed-path constraint (Tao and Choi, 2026). Scheduling, learned timesteps and bespoke/differentiable solvers optimize complementary variables (Gao et al., 2023; Sabour et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2024; Shaul et al., 2024; Tong et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026); our nearby-call Heun context neither benchmarks all of them nor claims an optimal solver. 

Incremental contribution. The leading projected Euler objective is a baseline. Our contribution is the set of sufficient marginal-order conditions, affine orbit–slice exactness results, and constrained spectral projection formulas established under the stated hypotheses. We do not claim arbitrary-path solvability or priority over all related formulations.
