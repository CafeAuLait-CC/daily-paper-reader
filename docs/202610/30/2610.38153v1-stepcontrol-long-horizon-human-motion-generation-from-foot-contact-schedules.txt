Abstract
Controllable human motion generation has largely focused
on specifying what action should occur or where the body
should move. We study a complementary question: can full-
body motion be controlled by specifying only when each
foot contacts the ground? We introduce StepControl, an
autoregressive diffusion model conditioned on a future bi-
nary left/right foot-contact schedule, without requiring tar-
get root and body poses, or semantic prompts. StepCon-
trol represents contact as explicit temporal events and in-
jects them through dedicated cross-attention with classifier-
free guidance. We further introduce a stochastic mixture
noise schedule and warmup strategy for stable long-horizon
autoregressive generation.
Experiments show that Step-
Control follows prescribed contact timing more accurately
than adapting existing spatially guided motion generators
to contact constraints. It maintains effective contact control
over long continuous rollouts and responds to edited sched-
ules such as cadence changes, stop-and-go pauses, limping
gaits, and bilateral jumping. Our results show that mini-
mal foot-contact schedules provide an effective and intuitive
temporal interface for controllable motion generation.

Recent advances in motion generation demonstrate im-
proved diversity and fidelity, expressing rich semantics and
styles of motion [8, 14, 25, 31, 39]. These methods largely
learn from skeletal motion data and often focus on the hu-
man motion itself without explicitly modeling interactions
with the environment. However, many motions are strongly

ric stepping patterns, without specifying body poses. In the
real world, a possible source of contact information may
be diverse multi-modal sensors, such as inertial, pressure,
tactile sensors, or sound. The contact schedule naturally
complements motion estimation when the visual informa-
tion is not available due to occluding objects or when a hu-
man moves out of the viewing frustum.
Compared to semantic context or kinematic priors, the
minimal input of binary contact information is extremely
low-dimensional. Therefore, naively concatenating the in-
put often results in ignoring the contact information [7, 8].
Incorporating such information as post-processing is also
not desirable, creating unnatural artifacts, such as foot pin-
ning and stiff steps, that severely degrade the quality of mo-
tion [18, 34, 40]. Sparse temporal events are also difficult to
preserve under repeated autoregressive rollouts [38, 39], as
small prediction errors can accumulate across rollout win-
dows [2, 4] and eventually cause phase mismatches, pop-
ping artifacts, or gait collapse.
We introduce StepControl, an autoregressive motion dif-
fusion model conditioned on a foot-contact schedule. A key
challenge is that binary contact schedules are sparse and
contain sharp transition events that can be easily overlooked
by a motion denoiser. We therefore explicitly represent con-
tact states, strike onsets, lift releases, and signed transitions
as event-aware features, which condition a Transformer-
based diffusion denoiser through dedicated cross-attention.
For autoregressive synthesis, we adopt frame-wise diffu-
sion levels [4, 15, 37], maintaining clean history together
with progressively refined future states in a shared tempo-
ral window. Since the corresponding future contact sched-
ule is available within this window, the model can antici-
pate upcoming contact transitions and adapt the preceding
motion accordingly, rather than forcing a sudden kinematic
response at the moment of contact. Progressive refinement
further maintains smooth continuity as generated frames are
committed back into the motion history.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• Contact-Timing Control: We introduce a motion gen-
eration pipeline conditioned on a minimal foot-contact
schedule, demonstrating that two binary channels can di-
rect rich full-body locomotion without spatial trajectories
or semantic prompts.
• Event-Aware Contact Conditioning:
We show that
learning an event-aware conditional motion prior achieves
superior contact adherence compared to separately en-
forcing contact as spatial guidance.
• Long-Horizon Controllability & Interactive Editing:
We provide empirical evidence that effective temporal
control persists over continuous 20 s autoregressive roll-
out and responds causally to user-guided schedule edits.
2. Related Works
2.1. Autoregressive Motion Diffusion
Diffusion models demonstrate their capability to generate
diverse and complex human motions. Early methods gener-
ate full-body motion directly in the motion space [29, 36],
while later methods improve fidelity and efficiency through
retrieval augmentation [35] or compact latent spaces [5, 6].
Most of these methods generate fixed-length clips from text
prompts or action labels. In contrast, we use frame-level
foot contacts as a temporal control signal.
Long-horizon motion generation extends beyond fixed-
length synthesis by recurrently conditioning on previously
generated motion.
Several diffusion-based approaches
adopt causal or autoregressive generation to produce contin-
uous motion streams [27, 32, 38, 39]. Related works such
as TEDi [37], Diffusion Forcing [4], and EgoForce [15] fur-
ther use temporally varying diffusion levels to progressively
refine motion over time. We adopt this progressive formula-
tion for contact-conditioned generation, allowing upcoming
foot-contact events to influence the preceding motion.
2.2. Controllable Motion Generation
Human motion can be controlled with semantic signals,
such as text and action labels, or with spatial signals, such
as root trajectories, keyframes, and joint paths. GMD [17]
guides motion with a global trajectory, while OmniCon-
trol [33] and PriorMDM [26] support spatial constraints on
selected joints. Kimodo [25], DART [38], and ARDY [39]
further support keyframe, trajectory, or goal-based control.
In contrast to these spatial controls, a foot-contact schedule
provides direct temporal control over locomotion by spec-
ifying when each foot should be planted or lifted, without
fixing its position or the future root trajectory.
Temporal conditioning has also been explored in music-
driven dance generation.
Existing methods use acous-
tic and rhythmic features to coordinate motion with mu-
sic [1, 19, 30], and Beat-It [13] explicitly separates beat
events from continuous music features. However, a mu-
sical beat does not directly indicate a foot strike, identify
the active foot, or specify the length of a stance phase. We
use explicit left- and right-foot contact labels, which pro-
vide direct control over foot-strike timing, stance duration,
cadence, and pauses.
2.3. Foot Contact in Motion Generation

foot-ground consistency. Binary contact labels are included
in motion representations [7], while training objectives pe-
nalize foot velocity during predicted contact to reduce ar-
tifacts such as foot skating and floating [24, 29, 30]. In-
verse kinematics and other kinematic corrections are also

3.3. Progressive Autoregressive Rollout
StepControl generates long-horizon motion using a sliding
window of W = 30 frames, consisting of S = 10 clean his-
tory frames and F = 20 progressively refined future frames
as shown in Fig. 2(b). Each frame is associated with its
own diffusion level k ∈{0, . . . , 99}, where k = 0 denotes a
clean state and larger values indicate increasing uncertainty.
Progressive Noise Schedule
During autoregressive roll-
out, future frames are assigned increasing diffusion levels
according to their temporal distance from the committed
history. Specifically, we use the fixed rollout schedule
  \ma t hbf { k}_ { \ m a thrm {roll}} = [4, 9, 14, \ldots , 99]. \label {eq:rollout_schedule} 
(10)
Thus, the nearest future frame is close to being finalized,
whereas frames farther into the future remain increasingly
noisy. Rather than independently regenerating an entire fu-
ture segment at each autoregressive step, each future frame
is progressively refined as it approaches the current time.
Since every frame is generated while observing the contact
schedule over a constant future horizon, the model can pre-
pare for upcoming touchdowns and lift-offs at each step of
the rollout. We compare this against chunk-wise rollout in
the Supplementary Material.

Evaluation Metrics
To evaluate motion quality and tem-
poral synchronization, we measure:
• Balanced Contact Accuracy (%): The unweighted av-
erage of stance recall and swing recall, computed from
3D foot joint velocities and heights of decoded motions.
• Contact Transition F1 (Tr. F1, %): While Balanced
Accuracy is insensitive to temporal phase shifts, Tr. F1
quantifies frame-exact synchronization. We identify On-
set (0 →1, foot touchdown) and Release (1 →0, foot
lift-off) events, compute the F1-score of each against the
target schedule, and report Tr. F1 = 1
2(F1onset +F1release).
• Stance Foot Slip (mm/frame): Mean horizontal dis-
placement of foot joints during frames where the target
schedule specifies stance.
• Floor Penetration (mm): Mean vertical distance of gen-
erated foot joints below the physical reference floor level.
• Root-Aligned Lower-Body MPJPE (mm): Mean per-
joint position error over lower-body joints (hips, knees,
ankles, and toes) after per-frame root translation and
heading alignment, following [16].
• Boundary Jerk (mm/frame3): Mean root-relative joint
jerk at the observed-to-generated transition averaged over
non-root joints.
Baselines
We compare StepControl against two autore-
gressive motion generation baselines, DART [38] and
ARDY [39]. We adapt their spatial conditioning to con-
tact schedules using foot-height constraints, while provid-
ing text prompts consistent with their training distributions.
Implementation and conditioning details are provided in the
Supplementary Material.
4.2. Long-Horizon Motion Generation
To evaluate whether temporal contact control survives re-
peated autoregressive rollout, we generate continuous se-
quences of increasing horizons (1 s, 5 s, 10 s, and 20 s) on
the LAFAN1 [10] test sequences using the progressive roll-
out of Sec. 3.3, and compare with the baselines under an
identical evaluation protocol (Tab. 1). StepControl main-
tains stable contact timing across 20-second continuous
rollouts with zero floor penetration. At 20 s, raw StepCon-
trol reaches 42.27% transition F1, compared with 14.60%
for DART and 15.98% for ARDY, while retaining compa-
rable lower-body error.

5. Conclusion
We presented StepControl, an autoregressive diffusion
framework that generates full-body motion from foot-
contact schedules without predefined root trajectories or
target poses. StepControl expands sparse binary contacts
into event-aware features and injects them through dedi-
cated cross-attention. Its mixture-noise training and pro-
gressive rollout support continuous long-horizon generation
from a short observed prefix. Our experiments show that the
model follows prescribed and edited contact schedules over
extended rollouts.
StepControl jointly predicts root trajectories and foot
joints within a single kinematic denoising process, without
explicitly enforcing that stance feet remain stationary. As a
result, its raw output exhibits more foot slip than methods
using spatial optimization or foot-locking post-processing.
A lightweight planar correction largely reduces this slip
without changing body articulation, but remains a post-hoc
rather than structural solution. Some residual foot slip and
occasional foot floating are inherent limits of the kinematic
formulation, and unseen contact patterns such as single-foot
hopping remain challenging. Future work could integrate
physical contact constraints or differentiable inverse kine-
matics into diffusion sampling to reduce foot sliding with-
out post-processing. Sparse temporal conditioning could
extend beyond locomotion to broader interaction scenarios
where visual observations are incomplete or unavailable.

A. Baseline Implementation Details
Because existing autoregressive motion models are funda-
mentally designed for text or 3D spatial keyframe condi-
tioning rather than sparse temporal contact schedules, we
establish standardized adaptation protocols that allow both
baselines to utilize their native architectures while condi-
tioning on target contact schedules.
A.1. DART Baseline Adaptation
Architecture and Pipeline
DART [38] generates con-
tinuous human motion using autoregressive latent motion
primitives modeled by a Motion Variational Autoencoder
(MVAE) operating at 20 fps. Each primitive encompasses
H = 2 history frames and F = 8 future frames (a 10-frame
local temporal horizon). Generation proceeds by sequen-
tially predicting latent primitive distributions conditioned
on clean past history and natural language prompts.
Contact Conditioning via Test-Time Spatial Guidance
Since DART lacks a dedicated temporal contact condition-
ing interface, we adapt it to follow target contact schedules
through test-time gradient-based spatial guidance:
1. Matched Text Conditioning: DART requires semantic
text guidance during sampling. For each test sequence
and editing mode, we supply matched text prompts con-
sistent with its training distribution (e.g., “a person is
walking forward” for natural rollouts, “a person is mov-
ing with a fast cadence” for fast editing, and “a person
is repeatedly jumping with both feet” for bilateral jump-
ing).
2. Initial-Noise Contact Optimization: At each autore-
gressive step, we optimize the diffusion initial noise for
50 iterations using the Adam optimizer (lr = 0.05),
jointly over up to four primitives, and commit only the
first primitive. The optimization objective enforces foot-
ground contact constraints on decoded joints:

C.3. Rollout Strategy Comparison
We compare progressive rollout with chunk-wise autore-
gressive rollout, which generates 20 future frames at once
from 10 history frames and uses the last 10 of the gener-
ated frames as the history for the next chunk. Both use
the same mixture-trained model, history and future lengths,
CFG scale, and LAFAN1 test sequences, without post-
processing.
Progressive rollout outperforms chunk-wise
rollout across all metrics (Table A4), and the same trend
holds for transition F1 at the 20-second horizon.
C.4. Analysis by Contact-Transition Rate
The 20-second rollouts are split into non-overlapping 5-
second windows and grouped by the number of contact tran-
sitions per second. Events are pooled within each group,
and onset and release F1 are averaged. Event 9D contact
conditioning yields higher transition F1 than the raw 2D
contact input in every rate group, and remains ahead of both
spatially guided baselines (Table A5).
D. Unconstrained Planar Stance Foot Correc-
tion (StepControl +Opt.)
In the main paper,
we report both raw generations
(StepControl) and an optional post-processed variant
(StepControl +Opt.). Here, we formally describe the pla-

Motivation and Core Principle
Unlike prior works that
rely on heavy test-time gradient descent or inverse kinemat-
ics (IK) solvers that can distort natural knee flexion and limb
articulation, our correction operates purely at the level of
the global horizontal root trajectory (x-z ground plane). It
directly cancels residual stance-phase horizontal foot drift
without altering joint angles, vertical foot clearances, or lo-
cal body poses.

E. Network Architecture and Training Details
Transformer Architecture
The contact-aware motion
denoiser is built upon a spatio-temporal Transformer archi-
tecture:
• Window Size: W = 30 frames (1.5 s at 20 fps), divided
into S = 10 history frames (0.5 s) and F = 20 future
frames (1.0 s).
• Transformer Layers: 8 transformer encoder layers.
• Hidden Dimension: d = 512, with feed-forward inner
dimension dff = 2048 and dropout 0.1.
• Attention Heads: 8 multi-head self-attention heads with
head dimension dk = 64.
• Cross-Attention Blocks:
Per-layer multi-head cross-
attention over contact tokens produced from the 9D event-
aware features by a two-layer MLP with SiLU (E1:W →
Z ∈RW ×512, 9 →512 →512).
• Activation and Normalization: GELU activations in the
feed-forward layers with pre-layer normalization (Pre-
LN); the contact embedding MLP uses SiLU.
Diffusion Hyperparameters and Noise Schedule
• Total Diffusion Steps: K = 100 discrete noise levels
(k ∈{0, . . . , 99}).
• Variance Schedule: Cosine variance schedule with offset
0.008 and maximum β of 0.999.
• Progressive Rollout Slope: kroll = [4, 9, 14, . . . , 99]
across future frames t ∈[S + 1, W] with stride ∆k = 5.
• Training Noise Distribution: Stochastic mixture sam-
pling with 70% probability assigned to the structured
slope kroll and 30% to uniform IID noise kt ∼U{0, 99}
in both training stages.
Optimization and Training Setup
• Optimizer: AdamW with β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, and
weight decay 0.01. Gradient clipping is 1.0 and the EMA
decay is 0.999.
• Learning Rate: 10−4 for HumanML3D pretraining and
5 × 10−5 for LAFAN1 fine-tuning. Fine-tuning is ini-
tialized from the pretrained EMA weights with a newly
initialized optimizer.
• Batch Size and Schedule: Batch size 128 for 20 epochs
in each stage (3,660 updates for pretraining and 5,000 for
fine-tuning), with training seed 2026.
• Loss Weights: λvel = 20, λh = 20, and λc = 0.1, and
λp = 0.1. Conditional and unconditional branches are
weighted equally.
