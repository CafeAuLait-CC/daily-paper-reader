# Composing and Steering Diffusion Models with Generalized Feynman-Kac Correctors

# Abstract 

Diffusion models have become the leading paradigm in generative modeling, yet many applications require sampling from distributions that are not those of any single pretrained model, but are instead defined through combinations of several models, possibly tilted toward high values of a downstream reward. Naively combining the scores of the models yields incorrect samples, while existing corrections are restricted to specific families of targets. In this work, we introduce the generalized Feynman–Kac correctors (GFKC), a principled framework for sampling from an arbitrary target function of several pretrained diffusion models. We show that the geometric interpolation between an arbitrary initial function and the target function of the models satisfies a Feynman–Kac partial differential equation, and derive its potential in closed form. To evaluate this potential during generation, we further generalize the log-probability ODE to track the density of each model along trajectories driven by a different velocity field. The resulting algorithm operates entirely in the log domain, incorporates reward-based steering at no additional conceptual cost, and corrects the generation on the fly, without retraining or fine-tuning the models. We validate the method on analytic Gaussian models, where it closely matches the ground-truth targets, and on diffusion models trained on MNIST, where it samples from non-trivial combinations of the models and enables effective steering. 

# 1 Introduction 

Diffusion models [1, 2, 3] have become the leading paradigm in generative modeling. By learning to reverse a gradual noising process, they produce samples of remarkable quality and diversity, and they are now the method of choice across a wide range of domains, including image synthesis [4, 5, 6, 7], audio [8] and video generation [9], inverse problems [10], and the design of molecules and proteins [11, 12, 13]. Beyond their empirical success, diffusion models rest on a solid mathematical foundation: in the continuous-time formulation of [3], generation amounts to simulating a stochastic or ordinary differential equation driven by a learned score, whose marginals evolve according to an explicit partial differential equation. This structure makes it possible to reason about, and to modify, the generative process in a principled way. In many applications, however, the distribution of interest is not that of a single pretrained model. One may wish to combine several models, each trained on different data or capturing a different concept, and to sample from a distribution that is defined in terms of all of them: their product, which retains only what all models agree on; their mixture; or more elaborate combinations, such as distributions favoring the regions where one model assigns high probability and another does not. Training a new model for every such target is costly and often impractical, and a natural alternative is to reuse the pretrained models at inference time [14]. Yet, naive approaches that simply combine the scores of the models do not reproduce the correct intermediate distributions, and therefore yield biased samples [15]. Recently, [16] showed that this bias can be corrected exactly by means of the Feynman–Kac formula [17]: the generated particles are assigned weights that evolve along the generation, and are periodically resampled within a sequential Monte Carlo scheme [18]. These Feynman–Kac correctors, however, are derived separately for specific families of targets, namely annealed distributions, products of experts, and the geometric averages underlying classifier-free guidance [19].

This is the task of steering, or reward-guided generation [20]. A principled formulation consists in maximizing the expected reward, regularized by the Kullback–Leibler divergence to the pretrained distribution, whose solution is the pretrained distribution exponentially tilted by the reward. One approach is to fine-tune the pretrained model toward this tilted distribution [21, 22], which, however, requires additional training for every reward. Inference-time methods, such as classifier guidance [4] and SMC-based guidance [23, 24], instead approximate the tilted distribution during generation, without modifying the model, but are typically formulated for a single pretrained model. In this paper, we unify and generalize these two lines of work. We introduce the generalized Feynman–Kac correctors (GFKC), which enable sampling from an arbitrary target function of several pretrained diffusion models, with an optional reward-based tilt. Our main result, Theorem 1, shows that the geometric interpolation between an arbitrary initial function and an arbitrary terminal function of the models satisfies a Feynman–Kac PDE, and provides its potential in closed form. To evaluate this potential along the generation, we derive a generalization of the log-probability ODE [25, 26], which tracks the density of every model along trajectories generated by a different velocity field. The resulting algorithm operates entirely in the log domain, performs the correction on the fly during the generation process, and requires neither retraining nor fine-tuning of the models. The framework of [16] is recovered as a special case. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews continuous-time diffusion models, the Feynman– Kac correctors, and reward-guided tilting. Section 3 presents our main result, the generalized log-probability ODE, the incorporation of steering, and the resulting GFKC algorithm. Section 4 illustrates the method in two settings of different nature: an analytic setting of overlapping Gaussian distributions, where the generated samples are compared with exact ground-truth densities, and a high-dimensional setting of diffusion models trained on the MNIST dataset [27], where we demonstrate both the sampling from non-trivial combinations of the models and the effect of steering. All numerical experiments are fully reproducible, and the code, together with the pretrained models, is publicly available at https://github.com/WaelMattar/GFKC .

However, the resulting dynamics do not reproduce the desired intermediate distributions, and the generated samples are therefore biased [15]. To address this issue, [16] proposed the Feynman–Kac correctors (FKC). Their key observation is that, for each of the aforementioned targets, the time derivative of the desired path of distributions can be written as a continuity equation of the form (4), augmented with a term of the form p g , where g is commonly referred to as the potential function ,see [17]. In other words, it satisfies a Feynman–Kac partial differential equation (PDE), with a velocity field and a potential that are explicitly computable from the pretrained scores. By the Feynman–Kac formula [17], such a PDE can be simulated by a population of weighted particles: the particles are transported by the velocity field, while their log-weights accumulate the potential along the trajectories. The weights are then used within a sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) scheme [17, 18], in which particles are periodically resampled in proportion to their weights, which prevents weight degeneracy and focuses the computational effort on the relevant regions of space. Our contribution. The FKC framework [16] provides exact weighted sampling for specific families of targets built from pretrained models, while reward-guided methods [20] tilt the distribution of a single pretrained model toward high values of a reward. In this paper, we unify and generalize both approaches. We derive the Feynman–Kac potential for an arbitrary target function h1 of N diffusion models, interpolated geometrically from an arbitrary initial function h0, with an optional reward-based tilt. The resulting GFKC algorithm performs the correction on the fly, during the generation process, and requires neither retraining nor fine-tuning of the agents.

# 4 Numerical illustrations 

We illustrate the performance of Algorithm 1 in two settings of inherently different nature: the first involves analytic generative models for two overlapping bivariate Gaussian distributions, while the second consists of diffusion models trained on the MNIST dataset [27]. 

4.1 Overlapping bivariate normal distributions 

We consider a two-dimensional synthetic setting in which all quantities of interest are available in closed form, which allows the output of the GFKC algorithm 1 to be compared against exact ground-truth densities. The setting consists of N = 2 agents, each given by a diffusion model whose data distribution is an isotropic bivariate Gaussian with unit 8covariance, 

p1 = N (μ1, I 2) and p2 = N (μ2, I 2), (17) with means μ1 = (3 , 3) and μ2 = (3 .5, 3.5) . Since ∥μ1 − μ2∥ = √2/2 ≈ 0.71 is smaller than the unit standard deviation of each component, the two distributions overlap substantially, and their high-density regions are difficult to separate by sampling from either model alone. Recall that we adopt the convention that t = 0 corresponds to noise and t = 1 to data. Each agent j ∈ { 1, 2} defines a path of marginals pj (·, t ) = N (tμ j , I 2), t ∈ [0 , 1] , which interpolates between the standard Gaussian at t = 0 and the data distribution pj at t = 1 . In particular, all agents share the same initial distribution N (0 , I 2), and the scores

4.2 Experimenting with the MNIST dataset 

We now move from the analytic Gaussian setting to a high-dimensional setting in which the agents are learned diffusion models. Whereas in the Gaussian experiments all quantities were available in closed form, here each agent is a diffusion model trained on a subset of the MNIST dataset [27], the data space has dimension d = 28 × 28 = 784 ,and neither the scores nor the densities pj for j = 1 , 2 are known analytically. The two agents are trained on two sets of digit classes that share exactly one class. The shared class thus plays the role of the region where the agents agree, while the two remaining classes, each seen by only one agent, correspond to the regions in which they disagree. To make the distinction between the shared and the agent-specific classes as clear as possible, we select the classes so that the three digits involved are pairwise far apart. To this end, we view each image as a probability measure on the uniform 28 × 28 grid in [0 , 1] 2, obtained by normalizing its pixel intensities, and compare images through the Wasserstein-1 distance W1 with Euclidean ground cost, computed exactly by solving the corresponding optimal transport problem. Distances between classes are measured in two complementary ways: as the W1 distance between the class-mean images, and as the average W1 distance over 1,000 randomly drawn pairs of images from the two classes. Among all triples of digits {a, b, c }, we then select the one maximizing the smallest of the three pairwise 11 distances, and assign to the shared class b the digit whose distances to the other two are largest. This procedure leads to the choice 

S1 = {1, 6}, S2 = {1, 7}

so that the digit 1 is shared by both agents, while the digits 6 and 7 are seen exclusively by the first and second agent, respectively. Each agent is trained on the images of its two classes from the MNIST training and test sets, with pixel intensities rescaled to [−1, 1] and images flattened into vectors in R784 . Since the classes of MNIST are not equally represented, each training batch is drawn with equal probability from the two classes of the agent, so that the resulting model assigns comparable mass to both digits. As in the Gaussian setting, we adopt the convention that t = 0 corresponds to noise and t = 1 to data. The forward noising process is the variance-preserving diffusion with a linear noise schedule, β(u) = β0 + ( β1 − β0) u with β0 = 0 .1 and β1 = 20 , where u = 1 − t denotes the forward time. The corresponding marginals are xu = α(u) x0 + σ(u) ε, with ε ∼ N (0 , I d), log α(u) = − 12 β0u − 14 (β1 − β0)u2 and 

σ(u) = p1 − α(u)2. Each network is trained with the standard noise-prediction objective, from which the score is recovered as sj (x, t ) = −ˆεj (x, t )/σ (t).Both agents share the same architecture, a fully connected network acting directly on the flattened images. The diffusion time is encoded through a 64 -dimensional sinusoidal embedding, followed by a linear layer and a SiLU activation. The embedding is concatenated with the input image and passed through three hidden layers of width 

2,048 with SiLU activations, followed by a linear output layer of dimension 784 . Each agent is trained for 300 ,000 

iterations using the Adam optimizer with learning rate 2 × 10 −4 and batch size 512 , with the diffusion time sampled uniformly from [10 −3, 1 − 10 −3].Unlike in the Gaussian setting, the target h1 cannot be evaluated in closed form. # 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we introduced the generalized Feynman–Kac correctors, a principled framework for sampling from arbitrary target functions of several pretrained diffusion models, with optional reward-based steering. Our main result in Theorem 1 provides, in closed form, the Feynman–Kac potential of the geometric interpolation between an arbitrary initial and terminal function of the agents, and, combined with a generalized log-probability ODE, leads to Algorithm 1, which is a practical algorithm that operates entirely in the log domain and corrects the generation on the fly, without retraining or fine-tuning. The numerical experiments confirm that the method accurately recovers non-trivial targets in an analytic setting, and that it scales to learned diffusion models in high dimension, where it isolates, combines and steers the concepts captured by the individual agents. Several directions remain open, including the reduction of weight degeneracy in high dimension, for instance through adaptive resampling or lower-variance divergence estimators, cheaper look-ahead estimates for steering, and the application of the framework to large-scale models and scientific domains such as molecular design.
