Abstract 

Guidance methods are essential tools for steering the generation process in dif-fusion models, improving the generalizability of stochastic robotic controllers and enabling high-quality controlled image synthesis. Despite strong parallels between flow-matching and diffusion, integrating guidance into flow-matching models remains quite challenging. Recent work has proposed general guidance frameworks for flow-matching, however, they are direct adaptations of diffusion guidance methods and can prove unstable. In this work, we conduct a theoret-ical analysis of guidance in flow-matching and show that a naive translation of diffusion-based guidance to flow-matching breaks the coupling distribution used during training, resulting in weaker control over sampling accuracy. Building on this insight, we propose Coupled Guidance , a flow-matching-specific guid-ance mechanism derived from a geometric perspective that leverages the coupling structure inherent to flow-matching training. To validate the effectiveness of our approach, we conduct extensive experimentation on a custom robotic collision avoidance task using M ANISKILL 3, four robotic dexterous manipulation tasks from the A DROIT benchmark, and three image generation tasks, namely inpainting, super-resolution, and deblurring, using the ImageNet-128 and CelebA-HQ-256 datasets. Our findings show that our framework improves sample quality and guidance stability across all tasks, without imposing any additional overhead. 

1 Introduction 

Flow-matching [35, 37] has recently emerged as a powerful class of generative models, unifying advantages from both normalizing flows [43, 23] and diffusion models [26, 29]. The diffusion-inspired MSE loss in flow-matching removes the need for backpropagation through an ODE solver, improves numerical stability, and introduces a beneficial geometric bias [61]. Meanwhile, Neural ODE parameterizations [8] offer highly expressive continuous-time dynamics and efficient sampling. Owing to this combination of features, flow-matching achieves strong results across various domains of generative AI, including robotic control [62, 4, 66], image generation [56, 52], audio synthesis [63, 31], and forecasting [22, 49]. As flow-matching continues to scale to more complex domains, mechanisms for controlling its sampling process become increasingly important. Guidance has played this role with great success in diffusion models [25, 1, 30], enabling interactive editing [15], improved action consistency [5], and improved robustness in downstream tasks. In robotics, for example, diffusion-based controllers are often trained offline, hence may never encounter obstacles during training. Guidance allows the injection of collision-avoidance heuristics at inference time [68, 67], adapting to previously unseen environments. In general, guidance methods steer the generation by perturbing model parameters [47],

reweighting distributions via task objectives [41], or conditioning on auxiliary cues, such as class labels [14]. Despite their success in diffusion models [33, 69, 68, 53, 67], analogous techniques for flow-matching remain underdeveloped. Recently, a flow-matching guidance framework, obtained by adapting diffusion-based guidance mechanisms to flow-matching, demonstrated promising results in locomotion and image synthesis. However, these adaptations are direct translations of their diffusion counterparts and do not explicitly account for the training coupling that is intrinsic to flow-matching but absent in diffusion models.

In this work, we revisit training-free guidance for flow-matching from the perspective of the latent-data coupling on which flow-matching is built. This coupling, fixed during training, ensures that the model learns a consistent joint evolution of latent variables and data. We show that naive guidance violates this coupling, producing mismatched trajectories that hinder controllability and sampling ac-curacy. Leveraging this insight, we propose 

Coupled Guidance , an efficient correction to the guided sampling process that preserves the training coupling. Our method employs a coordinated pair of guidance terms: a data-guidance component acting in data space and a corresponding latent-guidance component acting in latent space . Together, these terms maintain the structure of the flow-matching coupling, resulting in robust and controllable guided sampling with negligible additional overhead and minimal hyperparameter tuning. We evaluate the proposed Coupled Guidance method on robotic motion control with obstacle avoidance, four dexterous-hand manipulation tasks, and three image generation problems, namely inpainting, deblur-ring, and super-resolution (see Figure 1). In sum, the main contributions of our work are as follows: i) A correction of guidance for flow-matching that jointly controls guidance in data space and in latent space; ii) A theoretical bound demonstrating the necessity of synchronizing guided latent and data distributions; iii) Extensive empirical evaluations on robotics and image generation tasks to validate the proposed guidance mechanism. 

2 Related Works 

Diffusion Models and Flow-Matching Diffusion models [14, 55] are a class of generative frame-works that operate by adding random noise to data samples to learn a reverse denoising process in order to generate new samples. Although diffusion models have been very effective in various generative tasks [68, 10, 15, 27], they suffer from multiple shortcomings, such as noise distribution assumptions and computationally expensive denoising processes. These have led to a number of recent works on solving these issues [51, 29, 72, 11]. Flow-matching [35] remedies these shortcomings by directly learning a continuous transport vector field, mapping the latent distribution to the data distribution. Unlike diffusion models, flow-matching enables the choice of any latent distributions (not necessarily Gaussian) and coupling, enhancing the fidelity of generative modeling and simplifying the transport plans [37]. As a result, high-quality samples can be achieved with very few sampling steps, as low as a single function evaluation [20]. Due to such characteristics, flow-matching has been adopted widely in various domains of AI, ranging from audio [63, 31] and image generation [56, 52] to forecasting [22, 49], and robotic control and planning [62, 4, 66]. 

Guidance Methods In many generative tasks, it is essential to control the sampling process to meet certain criteria, such as generating new images given specific text prompts [32]. Guidance methods are effective mechanisms for controlling the sampling process and can be applied in different ways. One way is to apply guidance using a pretrained classifier to condition the sampling process [12, 55, 73, 34]. This approach, however, limits the diversity of samples as the conditioning is based 2on a limited set of classes. In addition, such a method requires large-scale retraining if the criteria are changed. For instance, in the context of obstacle avoidance, over 500K training samples are required to capture various goal configurations and obstacle layouts to cover a simple workspace [12]. Training-free guidance methods [53] lower the computational burden by applying and optimizing a cost function directly at inference time, hence eliminating the need to retrain the generative model. These approaches have been widely applied to diffusion policies for robotic control applications in which responding to novel or unexpected situations is paramount [6, 68, 18, 46, 67]. Despite the great success of training-free guidance methods, their direct application to flow-matching is not trivial. Feng et al. [19] propose a comprehensive framework for the translation of diffusion guidance to flow-matching. This approach enables the adaptation of several guidance methods from diffusion to flow-matching and provides a strong baseline for our work. Nevertheless, these adaptations do not account for the training coupling. As we show in the following section, this omission can result in the velocity field being evaluated at points that lie far outside the coupling region, where the model has not been trained.

5.2 Robotic Control as an Inpainting Task 

We emulate a two-level hierarchical motion controller in which a high-level planner provides partial information about future trajectories to a low-level controller, thereby turning control into an inpaint-ing problem. To this end, we train two flow-matching models per task. The high-level planner is a 

conditioned model that, given an observation history, predicts a horizon of h = 24 future state–action pairs (ot, a t)ht=1 . The low-level controller is trained on the same dataset but remains unconditioned .At test time, the controller receives sparse supervision from the planner and its task is to infer a complete action sequence consistent with this partial trajectory information. Intuitively, the planner acts as a world-aware module that provides intermittent waypoints in the trajectory space, while the controller acts as an action expert that fills in the missing degrees of freedom. We evaluate robustness to guidance sparsity under both naive and coupled guidance on the four Adroit dexterous manipulation tasks [48, 21]: Door (latch opening), Pen (reorientation to a target angle), Hammer (nail hammering), and Relocate (object relocation) (Figure 2). Different seeds correspond to variations in task configuration (e.g., door pose, target angle, nail position, and object/goal locations). As ΠGDM and ΠGDM +behave similarly, we present results for ΠGDM in Figure 3 and include additional results in Appendix C.1. For all runs we used 300 seeds. As shown in Figure 3, coupled guidance is substantially more robust to sparse guidance than naive guidance, without requiring task-specific tuning. Even when sweeping a wide range of guidance scales, naive guidance degrades sharply once 20% or fewer dimensions are guided, whereas coupled guidance maintains near-fully-guided performance down to approximately 5% guided dimensions. Naive guidance can perform well with careful tuning, but requires different guidance scales across sparsity regions, whereas coupled guidance remains stable across all sparsity levels without tuning. Although adding noise may make an accurate reconstruc-tion more difficult, in practice it helps stabilize the guid-ance process, and σy = 0 is actually the regime in which training-free guidance is most prone to instability. It has been shown in the previous studies [65, 2, 60] that nonzero measurement noise σy acts as an implicit regularizer, i.e. it smooths the posterior score so that the linear guidance term dominates for most of the trajectory [2], reduces the effective curvature of the likelihood gradient [60], and in ΠGDM directly Tikhonov-regularizes the covariance inversion via the σ2 

> y

I term. When σy approaches 0, these stabilizing effects vanish: the guidance sharpens, instability increases, and approximation errors in the score are no longer masked by a forgiving posterior geometry. This makes the noiseless setting pertinent for our study as it highlights a failure mode where the stabilizing effects of coupled guidance are necessary. 

Inpainting In this setting, naive ΠGDM +exhibits stable and reliable performance across all metrics, whereas ΠGDM shows worse performance, producing visible artifacts. This behavior, illustrated in Figure 4 is consistent with the analysis in Section 3, as ΠGDM relies on the Jacobian ∂bx1/∂x t, which becomes poorly conditioned when guided trajectories deviate from the training coupling. Coupled guidance has little effect on ΠGDM +, whose stability primarily stems from the identity Jacobian approximation. However, it significantly stabilizes ΠGDM by preserving the training coupling. As a result, coupled ΠGDM consistently outperforms naive ΠGDM and ΠGDM +across all image quality metrics. 

Super-resolution In this sparse guidance setting, both ΠGDM +and ΠGDM fail to produce mean-ingful reconstructions, yielding poor image quality across all evaluation metrics, as shown in Figure 4. Like in the inpainting case, introducing coupled guidance has negligible impact on ΠGDM +,whose performance is fundamentally constrained by the identity Jacobian approximation and thus poorly suited to this task. In contrast, coupled guidance substantially stabilizes ΠGDM by mitigating excessive latent-side drift, enabling reliable sampling throughout the trajectory. This stabilization translates into a clear improvement in sample quality, with the coupled ΠGDM variant consistently outperforming all other methods. 

Deblurring In this task, both ΠGDM + and ΠGDM benefit from coupled guidance. ΠGDM +by default produces high-quality samples under naive guidance, indicating that the identity Jacobian 8approximation is well suited to this type of degradation. Still, coupled guidance further improves the reconstruction quality across all metrics in this setting. Coupled guidance likewise stabilizes ΠGDM and enables reliable inference; however, even with this stabilization, its performance remains below that of ΠGDM +. Overall, both methods benefit from coupled guidance in this setting even if this time ΠGDM + emerges as the strongest method. This observation provides direct empirical evidence that coupled guidance helps control the magnitude and timing of the Jacobian-induced corrections by preserving the training coupling, thereby preventing instability without degrading the guidance signal. 

6 Limitations 

Simplified theoretical setting. Our analysis is conducted under a simplified formulation using the unweighted quadratic flow-matching loss. A more general treatment would require extending the theory to weighted Bregman divergences, which cover the diversity of flow-matching objectives in the literature [36]. In addition, our theoretical study currently applies only to linear couplings; extending the analysis to more sophisticated geometries [7] remains out of scope. Finally, Theorem 1 relies on the existence of a continuous limit, whereas practical schedules we consider do not have such a limit. 

Noiseless image inverse problems. Our work primarily targets noiseless inverse problems, where guidance-induced coupling mismatch is most pronounced and cannot be mitigated by observation noise. In noisy inverse problems, noise implicitly regularizes the posterior and stabilizes Jacobian-based guidance, partially alleviating the issues we address. Moreover, in such settings the effect of coupled guidance interacts with the residual scaling schedule and the noise variance term, making it less straightforward to differentiate improvements due to coupling preservation from those induced by schedule design. As a result, the relative gains of coupled guidance may be less pronounced in noisy inverse tasks, although the proposed framework remains applicable. Still, since many applications require noiseless inverse guidance (photo editing, content super-resolution, general deblurring), we deem this restriction reasonable. 

7 Conclusion 

We proposed coupled guidance , a training-free mechanism that exploits the geometric structure of flow-matching couplings to stabilize guided generation. By jointly leveraging information from both latent and data spaces, our approach achieves competitive or superior performance across robotics and image inverse problems while requiring minimal hyperparameter tuning and negligible computational overhead. Extensive experiments show that coupled guidance improves robustness to strongly out-of-distribution guidance signals and guidance sparsity, while improving the quality of generated samples across the board, over all tested dimensionalities and application domains. The observed behaviors validate our theoretical insights and demonstrate that coupled guidance offers a 9simple, scalable improvement to existing methods. We believe that this approach will extend naturally to more complex coupling structures and that it opens the door for more fine-grained latent control.

14 B Experimental details 

B.1 Baseline selection 

As stated in the main text, the baselines for training-free guidance are scarce. We took Feng et al. [19]’s five variants as our starting point and considered each carefully. First gM C requires a large number of parallel samples per step, making it computationally intractable at our experiment’s dimensionalities, an issue which the original paper also faces. For most of our experiments (super-resolution and inpainting and our guidance sparsity experiments), gcov −A and 

gcov −G reduce analytically to ΠGDM and gsim-inv-A (which we refer to as ΠGDM+) respectively. We hence choose to benchmark our method on these two baselines as they cover all 4 tractable methods from Feng et al. [19]. This setup also covers FlowChef [45], which gives proportional guidance to gcov −A, and in turn 

ΠGDM, with a factor that is absorbed during our tuning. We choose not to include PnP-Flow [42], which is broadly outperformed by FlowChef, and in turn by Feng et al. [19]. The remaining training-free guidance works [16, 44, 28] operate exclusively in latent space, rely on noise-level terms incompatible with noiseless tasks, or do not scale to high dimension tasks; none of them are directly applicable here. For the baseline choice of collision avoidance, we cannot directly use ΠGDM , as it would require finding an adjoint operator . Concretely, this means that we would need to find a function h such that, with p the collision avoidance pipeline, p ◦ h ◦ p(x) = p(x). This means that given the environment and the value of the Euclidean Signed Distance Function, we need to be able to find a set of joint positions that solves to the same ESDF. This is highly non trivial, and if implemented would have a prohibitive cost to use in a robotics setting. Since basic energy minimization does not have such a constraint on the guidance operator, we use it in its simplest form : 

gcollision = −λt∇bx1 || bx1 − y|| 

B.2 Robotics 

For the robotics tasks we use the same UNet architecture as in Chi et al. [9], with 3 levels of depth respectively 256, 512 and 1024. They are trained with a time resolution of 1/128 for 1000 epochs on the "expert-v2" datasets available in the Minari python library [70]. The reported scores use 300 environment seeds. 

B.3 Image Generation 

For the image generation tasks, a 2 billion parameter UNet is used as defined in the code provided by Feng et al. [19], with minor fixes to the training code to make it more stable. They are trained using a batch of size 128, base learning rate 10 −4 with AdamW [40], warm-up then cosine annealing [39]. For both training and inference we tested and found negligible performance impacts for running the model in mixed precision: the training is done using torch automatic mixed precision, and all methods use BF16 [58] for the reported scores. We noticed an improvement in base model performance when normalizing data which was not done in Feng et al. [19]’s code. The models are hence trained with normalized data, but we noticed that both ΠGDM and gsim-inv-A suffered from doing guidance in normalized space with guidance scale varying across two orders of magnitude from task to task. To fairly evaluate the baselines, the model is called in normalized space but the velocity field is then unnormalized before applying guidance:
