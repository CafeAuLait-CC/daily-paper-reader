## Abstract 

Topology-agnostic motion generation aims to synthesize motions for diverse characters with arbitrary skeletons. Unlike conventional motion generation, which assumes a fixed skeletal template, this setting must handle diverse joint configurations and inconsistent joint semantics across char-acters. Existing methods address this challenge through dense full-joint modeling, which incurs substantial com-putational cost and hinders learning transferable motion patterns across diverse skeletal topologies. We present ETA-Motion, a topology-agnostic motion generation frame-work based on sparse joint learning. Instead of learning motion from all joints, our method uses a compact sub-set of joints to reduce redundancy and trains the motion generator on the resulting sparse skeleton. To facilitate correspondence across diverse topologies, we introduce semantic joint embeddings that combine textual and vi-sual cues, together with topology-aware training objectives for preserving skeletal structure. Although ETA-Motion is trained only on sparse skeletal observations, it supports full-skeleton motion generation during inference. Exper-imental results demonstrate that ETA-Motion outperforms existing topology-agnostic baselines while significantly im-proving efficiency and generalization to unseen motions and unseen topologies. 

## 1. Introduction 

Recent advances in generative modeling have significantly improved human motion generation. A wide range of ap-proaches, including autoregressive modeling, masked mod-eling, and diffusion models, have enabled the synthesis of realistic and controllable motions under diverse condi-arXiv:2610.38259v1 [cs.CV] 28 Oct 2026 tions [2, 3, 8, 9, 14, 23, 24, 26, 29–34]. Most of these meth-ods, however, are developed under a fixed-template setting, where all training samples share the same skeletal struc-ture, joint ordering, and kinematic hierarchy. Under this as-sumption, joint semantics are implicitly aligned across the dataset [7], and modeling skeletal attributes such as joint identity or topology is rarely treated as a primary challenge. Topology-agnostic motion generation aims to directly synthesize motions conditioned on an arbitrary input skele-ton, without relying on a fixed skeletal template. This setting is substantially more challenging because joint in-dices no longer carry consistent semantics across samples, and the underlying articulation structures vary significantly across characters. Recent works such as AnyTop [4] and How to Move Your Dragon [16] take important steps to-ward this setting by learning a single model over diverse skeletons while preserving full joint dimensionality during motion modeling. Despite this progress, dense full-joint modeling intro-duces two fundamental limitations. First, it incurs sub-stantial computational overhead as the number of joints in-creases. More importantly, maintaining the full joint dimen-sionality throughout training encourages the model to learn topology-specific details that are not always necessary for transferable motion generation. As a result, the complexity of topology-agnostic motion learning is not merely an effi-ciency issue, but is also closely tied to motion quality and generalization. To address this challenge, we present ETA-Motion, a topology-agnostic motion generation framework based on sparse joint learning. Instead of training on full-joint motion data, ETA-Motion learns motion dynamics from a compact subset of joints, reducing redundancy in motion modeling, while improving computational efficiency. To support cor-respondence across diverse skeletons, we further introduce semantic joint embeddings that combine textual and visual cues, together with topology-aware objectives for preserv-ing structural consistency. As illustrated in Fig. 1, although the model is trained on sparse joint subsets, it generates mo-tions for target skeletons with arbitrary topologies at infer-ence time. Our main contributions are as follows. • We propose a sparse joint subset learning framework for topology-agnostic motion generation, which replaces dense full-joint training with a compact sparse represen-tation to reduce redundancy and computational cost. • We design a topology-aware motion learning scheme with semantic joint embeddings and structural objectives, enabling transferable motion generation across diverse skeleton topologies while preserving skeletal consistency. • Extensive experiments show that training on sparse joint subsets improves not only computational efficiency, but also motion quality and topology preservation over exist-ing topology-agnostic baselines. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Human Motion Generation 

Human motion generation has advanced substantially with the development of modern generative models. Exist-ing approaches can be broadly categorized into two ma-jor paradigms: discrete token-based methods and diffusion-based methods. The first line of work represents human motion as compact discrete tokens, typically through vector quantization, and performs motion generation in the token space [8, 9, 14, 23, 24, 30, 34]. These approaches enable efficient and stable motion generation through compact dis-crete representations. The second line of work employs dif-fusion models to model human motion through denoising in continuous space [2, 3, 15, 26, 29, 31–33]. Recent work ex-tends motion diffusion to physics-based control by blending pretrained controllers through reinforcement learning [20]. These methods have demonstrated strong performance in producing realistic and diverse human motions. Despite their different generation mechanisms, both lines of work implicitly assume a fixed human skeletal template, where joint numbers, joint ordering, and kinematic relations are predefined and shared across all samples. As a result, in-dividual joints are rarely treated as explicit modeling units, and skeletal topology is treated as a predefined condition rather than a modeling variable. In addition, HumanML3D [7] has largely standardized the motion representation used in modern motion genera-tion frameworks. Following its formulation, motion is typ-ically represented as a combination of joint positions, rela-tive rotations, local velocities, and foot-contact labels. This design explicitly employs a redundant motion representa-tion, where multiple kinematic attributes are jointly mod-eled. Consequently, MDM [26] introduces auxiliary kine-matic objectives, such as geodesic rotation losses and foot-sliding penalties, to maintain consistency among these mo-tion features. 

2.2. Topology-Agnostic Motion Generation 

Generating motions across diverse skeletal topologies re-mains challenging due to the limited availability of large-scale topology-diverse motion datasets. To alleviate this is-sue, several works [12, 25] leverage strong priors from large video generation models. While these approaches benefit from large-scale video knowledge, they rely on multi-stage pipelines involving intermediate video generation, leading to increased computational cost and error accumulation. Recent efforts have begun to directly address topology-aware motion generation without relying on large pretrained priors. AniMo [28] adopts VQ-VAE-based discrete motion tokenization and performs species-aware motion generation in the token space. Diffusion-based approaches have emerged as a promising direction for topology-aware motion generation [4, 16]. By incorporating skeletal-aware attention mechanisms, these approaches enable a single model to operate on diverse skeletal topologies. 2.3. Efficient Motion Generation 

Recent studies show that reducing redundancy in motion representations improves not only computational efficiency but also generation quality. Several works have ques-tioned the necessity of the redundant kinematic represen-tation popularized by HumanML3D, demonstrating that simplified motion representations improve generation qual-ity while reducing modeling complexity [18, 19]. Sim-ilarly, Less is More [1] observes that dense motion se-quences contain many redundant frames and proposes a sparse keyframe diffusion framework, leading to both im-proved motion quality and reduced computational cost. Several works further explore compact motion represen-tations to improve efficiency. SALAD [11] introduces a skeleton-aware latent representation that compresses both temporal and skeletal dimensions, retaining only a small set of atomic joints within the latent space while preserv-ing motion dynamics. SFControl [13] employs a two-stage framework that first synthesizes sparse key-joint trajectories and subsequently reconstructs full-body motions, demon-strating that low-dimensional skeletal representations effec-tively support controllable motion synthesis. Motivated by these findings, we investigate sparse joint learning to reduce skeletal redundancy and enable efficient motion generation across diverse topologies. 

## 3. Method 

3.1. Preliminaries 

Each asset in the dataset is represented as A = ( J , E, M),where the joint set J = ( N, P) contains semantic joint names N and rest-pose P. The edge set E consists of di-rected parent-child connections that define the kinematic hierarchy of the skeleton. The rigged mesh M = ( V, W)

consists of mesh vertices V and skinning weights W, where 

Wv,i denotes the influence weight of joint i on vertex v.Given a skeletal motion sequence, the mesh is deformed according to the motion of the underlying joints and the pre-defined skinning weights. Therefore, our generator does not directly model mesh deformation; instead, it predicts skele-tal motion features, from which the posed mesh is obtained through the skinning function. 

3.2. Topology-Agnostic Motion Representation 

Motion representation is particularly important for topology-agnostic motion generation. Representations that are highly tied to a specific kinematic chain, such as joint rotations or parent-relative joint offsets, encourage the model to learn motion features with skeleton-specific meanings. While this is acceptable in fixed-template mo-tion generation, where all samples share the same skeletal structure, it becomes problematic in topology-agnostic motion generation, where the input skeleton itself varies across samples. Consequently, such representations make it difficult to learn transferable motion features across diverse topologies and often lead to performance degradation. To avoid this issue, we directly model 3D joint positions. Inspired by MARDM [19] and ACMDM [18], we repre-sent a motion sequence using 3D joint positions, denoted by X ∈ RF ×J×3, where F and J denote the numbers of frames and joints, respectively. Both F and J may vary de-pending on the motion sequence and input skeleton. Specif-ically, we represent the root joint in global coordinates and all non-root joints relative to the root joint. This position-based representation allows the model to represent motion without relying on skeleton-specific kinematic parameteri-zations. 

3.3. Architecture 

Our model is a conditional diffusion model that generates motion sequences from a text description and an arbitrary skeleton. As illustrated in Fig. 2, our model adopts a Diffu-sion Transformer (DiT) backbone with AdaLN-Zero condi-tioning [22]. Given a noisy motion sample Xt, the network predicts the corresponding clean motion X0.Before being processed by the DiT backbone, the input motion sequence is transformed into motion tokens through conditioning and embedding. Following AnyTop [4], we prepend the clean rest pose P ∈ RJ×3 to the noisy mo-tion sequence Xt. This prepended rest pose serves as an explicit structural reference, providing a clear signal of the target skeleton that should be preserved throughout gener-ation. The transformer therefore receives (F + 1) × J to-kens. When all joints are used during training, J corre-sponds to Jmax , the maximum number of joints in the train-ing set. and the padded joints are ignored using a validity mask. During inference, J corresponds to Jtarget , the actual num-ber of joints of the target skeleton, so no padding is required. Since the DiT backbone operates on token sequences, it supports varying token lengths induced by different num-bers of joints. The DiT backbone consists of alternating spatial atten-tion, temporal attention, and feed-forward network blocks. Spatial attention models interactions among joints within each frame, while temporal attention captures motion dy-namics across frames for each joint. The validity mask is applied in the spatial attention blocks so that padded joints neither attend to other joints nor are attended to. Temporal attention is computed independently for each joint across frames, and padded joint slots are excluded from super-vision. The computational complexity of spatial attention scales as O(F J 2), whereas that of temporal attention scales as O(JF 2). Consequently, using all joints during training leads to a rapidly increasing attention cost as Jmax grows. This observation motivates our sparse joint learning. 

3.4. Sparse Joint Learning 

Motivated by the quadratic complexity of spatial attention, ETA-Motion adopts a sparse joint subset training strategy. While this design is primarily introduced for computational efficiency, it is also motivated by an important observa-tion: dense skeletal motions contain substantial redundancy. Neighboring joints within the same articulated part often follow highly correlated motion patterns, while their rela-tive geometric relationships are already determined by the reference skeleton. Consequently, learning motion dynam-ics from every joint is often unnecessary, and a representa-tive subset of joints is sufficient to capture the underlying motion dynamics. Accordingly, we train the motion generator using a com-pact subset of joints under a joint budget of K. Since 

K ≪ Jmax , the spatial attention complexity during train-ing is reduced from O(F J 2max ) to O(F K 2), substantially reducing both computation and memory usage while also minimizing unnecessary padding in batched training. Given an input skeleton, we construct a sparse skeleton by selecting K joints from the original skeleton, as shown in Fig. 3. While multiple joint selection strategies are pos-sible, we adopt farthest point sampling (FPS) starting from the root joint as a simple and deterministic heuristic. We then redefine the skeletal connectivity by reconnecting each retained joint to its nearest retained ancestor in the original kinematic hierarchy, yielding a sparse yet structurally con-sistent skeleton. Additional comparisons with alternative joint selection heuristics are provided in the supplementary material. During training, the motion generator observes only sparse skeletons. Yet, this sparse training setup generalizes to full target skeletons at inference time. The clean rest pose 

P provides an explicit structural signal for the target skele-ton, allowing the learned motion dynamics to transfer to full skeletons with different joint counts. 

3.5. Semantic Joint Embedding 

A key challenge in topology-agnostic motion generation is that joint indices do not carry consistent semantics across different skeletons. The same index often corresponds to different anatomical structures depending on the target topology, making it difficult for the model to establish cor-respondence across skeletons and learn transferable motion patterns. Therefore, explicit semantic encoding is essential for topology-agnostic motion generation. To this end, we construct a semantic joint embedding by combining textual and visual cues for each joint. Similar to AnyTop [4], we use joint names as textual cues, and fur-ther augment them with visual cues derived from the rigged mesh. Let H ∈ R(F +1) ×J×d denote the motion tokens ob-tained by concatenating the projected rest pose and noisy motion sequence, where each token is a d-dimensional fea-ture vector. We compute the semantic joint embedding as 

E = Etext + Evis , where Etext ∈ RJ×d and Evis ∈ RJ×d

denote the textual and visual joint embeddings, respectively. We add E to the motion tokens along the joint dimension while broadcasting it across the temporal dimension, yield-ing H′ = H + E. The resulting tokens H′ are then passed to the transformer backbone. 

Textual Joint Embedding. We derive the textual joint embedding from the joint names N defined in the asset rep-resentation. These joint names are encoded using a pre-trained T5 text encoder and projected into the motion la-tent space through a linear layer to obtain the textual joint embedding Etext . However, textual cues alone are insuf-ficient for establishing accurate correspondence across di-verse skeletons. Joints with the same textual label of-ten exhibit substantially different visual characteristics and motion roles depending on the target topology. For ex-ample, joints labeled as arm correspond to forelimbs of quadrupeds or wings of flying creatures in different skele-tons. Moreover, asset-provided joint names are often noisy, incomplete, or inconsistent across rigs. These limitations make it difficult to establish accurate correspondence using textual cues alone.

## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Dataset. We evaluate our approach on the Truebones Zoo dataset [27], which contains over 1,000 motion sequences from 74 species with diverse skeletal topologies. Since the dataset does not provide text annotations, we use the cap-tions released with How to Move Your Dragon [16] (here-after, Dragon). We split the dataset into 65 training species and 9 test species. Following Dragon [16], we hold out one motion sequence from each training species for evaluation. We report results on three settings: (1) Seen , where both the skeleton and motion are observed during training; (2) Un-seen Motion , where held-out motions from training species are used for evaluation; and (3) Unseen Topology , where the model is tested on unseen species to measure generalization to novel skeletal topologies. Additional preprocessing de-tails are provided in the supplementary material. 

Implementation Details. For the main baseline compari-son, ETA-Motion is trained with a joint budget of K = 20 .We provide additional analysis on the effect of varying K

in the ablation study. Our model is based on a 4-layer DiT with latent dimension 128 and is trained with 100 diffusion steps. We train all models with a batch size of 16 on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 or 4090 GPU. For the main baseline comparison, we report results at 600K train-ing steps in consideration of the training budgets used in the baseline papers. Ablation studies are conducted at 300K steps to evaluate the relative effects of each design choice within our model. 

Evaluation Metrics. Following Ganimator [17] and Any-Top [4], we report Coverage to measure how well gener-ated motions reproduce motion patterns observed in the ref-erence motion set. To evaluate topology preservation, we report two topology-aware metrics: Bone Length Variance (BLV) and Capsule Collision Ratio (CR) , where lower val-ues indicate better structural consistency and fewer self-collisions. Since these automatic metrics do not directly evaluate semantic alignment, we additionally conduct a user study with 30 participants. Participants are asked to select the best result among Ours, AnyTop, and Dragon for two questions: skeleton-motion compatibility and text-motion alignment . We report the percentage of responses in which each method is selected as the best result. Additional de-tails of the metric implementation and the user study are provided in the supplementary material.

## 5. Conclusion 

In this paper, we present ETA-Motion, an efficient topology-agnostic motion generation framework based on sparse joint learning. Unlike prior approaches that rely on dense full-joint motion modeling, ETA-Motion learns motion dynamics from a compact joint subset while gen-erating motions for full target skeletons at inference time. To support transferable motion generation across diverse skeletal topologies, we introduce semantic joint embed-dings and topology-aware training objectives. Experimen-tal results show that ETA-Motion consistently improves both motion quality and training efficiency over base-lines. Ablation studies further confirm that the performance gains arise from the proposed sparse joint design, semantic joint embedding, topology-aware supervision, and compact position-based motion representation. These results suggest that compact sparse motion representations provide an ef-fective alternative to dense full-joint modeling for topology-agnostic motion generation. 

Limitations and Future Works. Despite its strong effi-ciency and generation performance, ETA-Motion has sev-eral limitations. Our current implementation uses FPS as a simple heuristic for constructing sparse joint subsets. Al-though effective in practice, it may not always retain the most informative joints. In addition, because our model pre-dicts joint positions, practical deployment to rigged meshes requires an additional inverse kinematics stage. This ad-ditional stage often leads to temporal inconsistency or re-construction artifacts. Future work includes integrating a dedicated position-to-rotation module, as explored in recent studies [5, 6], to further improve practical usability.

## S1. Analysis of Joint Selection Heuristics 

In addition to the FPS-based joint selection method presented in the main paper, we compare FPS with connectivity-aware, motion-aware, and random selection. All methods include the root joint and select the same number of joints under the same joint budget K. The connectivity-aware variant applies farthest-point sampling using topological hop distances on the skeleton tree. The motion-aware variant selects the remaining joints with the largest temporal variance in local 6D rotations, while ran-dom selection samples the remaining joints uniformly at random. Table S1 compares different joint selection heuristics un-der the same budget K = 20 . While no alternative consis-tently outperforms FPS across all settings, random selection achieves the best coverage on unseen topologies, suggesting improved robustness to topology shifts. Together with the joint-budget analysis in Table S2, these results suggest that the joint budget has a larger impact than the specific selec-tion heuristic.

S2.2. Capsule Collision Ratio (CR) 

CR measures self-collision frequency using a capsule-based geometric proxy. Each bone is modeled as a capsule cen-tered on the line segment connecting a parent-child joint pair. The capsule radius for each bone is set to 2% of its mean temporal bone length. We consider only non-adjacent bone pairs, namely pairs of bones that do not share a joint. For each frame and each valid bone pair, we compute the minimum distance between the two bone segments in 3D. A collision is counted when this distance is smaller than the sum of the two capsule radii. CR is then defined as the frac-tion of colliding (frame , bone-pair ) entries over all tested frames and non-adjacent bone pairs. Lower CR indicates fewer self-collisions and better physical plausibility. 

## S3. User Study Details 

We provide additional details of the user studies used to evaluate semantic aspects that are not directly captured by the automatic metrics. All studies were conducted with 30 participants. In all user studies, we showed a rest-pose skeleton and an animal image, and displayed the text prompt prominently together with the generated motion re-sults. 

S3.1. Baseline Comparison 

For the baseline comparison, participants were shown mo-tion results generated by Ours, AnyTop, and Dragon for the same text prompt and target skeleton. The order of the three methods was randomized for each question. For each ex-ample, participants were asked to select the single best re-sult for the following two questions: skeleton-motion com-patibility : “Which motion is the most natural and appro-priate for the given skeleton?” and Text-motion alignment :“Which motion best matches the given text description?” We report the preference score of each method as the per-centage of responses in which it was selected as the best result. S3.2. Joint Budget Comparison 

For the joint budget comparison, participants were shown pairs of motion results generated with different joint bud-gets K ∈ { 5, 10 , 20 , 50 , full }. For each pair, the order of presentation was randomized, and participants selected the preferred result using the same two questions as in the base-line comparison: skeleton–motion compatibility : “Which motion is the most natural and appropriate for the given skeleton?” and text–motion alignment : “Which motion best matches the given text description?” We aggregate the pair-wise comparison results using the Bradley–Terry model, which converts pairwise wins and losses into a global pref-erence score for each joint-budget setting, and report the resulting scores in the main paper.

S4.2. Effect of Visual Joint Embedding 

We further evaluate the robustness of the visual joint em-bedding on the pig asset used in the main paper, whose joints are labeled with arbitrary identifiers (e.g., joint1 ,

joint2 ) rather than semantic names. We compare motion samples generated by models trained with and without the visual joint embedding using the same text prompt: “An animal runs energetically forward.”

These results demonstrate that visual joint embeddings improve robust-ness to uninformative joint labels and facilitate practical motion generation for rigged assets with noisy joint annota-tions.
