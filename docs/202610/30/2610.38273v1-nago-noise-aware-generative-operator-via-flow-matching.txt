# ABSTRACT 

We introduce NAGO , a noise-aware generative operator for efficient sampling from invariant measures of stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) in function space. Our central idea is to treat the source measure as a design variable rather than a fixed Gaussian default. We establish admissibility conditions for well-defined infinite-dimensional flows and show that the finite-band source–target covariance ratio controls the spectral stiffness of the marginal velocity field. This analysis yields an admissibility-constrained spectral-matching rule: choose a source that is sufficiently rough for well-posedness, then minimize covariance mismatch over the resolved Fourier modes. On three representative stochastic-field benchmarks, this source design improves few-step spectral accuracy and several complementary distributional and physical statistics. In a controlled Matérn-field ablation, it reduces the accurate RK4 budget from about 30 steps for Gaussian functional flow matching to 3–5 steps, a 6–10 × reduction in neural ODE evaluations. These gains are strongest for stationary multiscale fields and depend on the metric, resolution, and guidance protocol. 

# 1 Introduction 

Stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) constitute a fundamental mathematical framework for modeling the evolution of infinite-dimensional random systems under uncertainty and provide a rigorous description of diverse spatiotemporal phenomena, including statistical physics [Huang, 2009] and composite materials [Hornung, 1992]. However, in many practical applications, such as geophysical modeling [Lindgren et al., 2011], turbulent flow [Kida and Murakami, 1987, Hairer and Mattingly, 2006, Carmona, 1999], and thin film growth [Hairer, 2013], the exact evolution of a single realization is often less important than the long-term statistical behavior of the system [Eckmann and Hairer, 2000]. A central problem in this context is the characterization of invariant measures [Da Prato and Zabczyk, 1996, 2014], which represent the most compressed yet complete description of long-term stochastic behavior, as discussed in Appendix B. For many SPDEs of physical and statistical interest, these invariant measures take the form of Gaussian or non-Gaussian probability measures on suitable function spaces, with representative examples including the Ornstein– Uhlenbeck equations [Särkkä and Solin, 2019] and nonlinear models such as the stochastic Allen–Cahn equation [Xu et al., 2025] and the stochastic Navier–Stokes equation [Bensoussan, 1995], which may admit unique ergodic invariant measures under appropriate conditions. Despite their importance, sampling invariant measures of SPDEs remains challenging due to the infinite-dimensional nature of the dynamics, the absence of closed-form solutions in nonlinear regimes, and the intricate interaction between noise, dissipation, and spatial operators. Classical numerical approaches, including long-time integration of discretized SPDEs [Lord et al., 2014, Da Prato and Zabczyk, 2014] and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling in function space [Chen, 2025], may approximate invariant measures but are

These challenges have motivated growing interest in data-driven and generative modeling approaches that aim to learn invariant measures directly, bypassing explicit long-time simulation. Recent advances in generative modeling [Ho et al., 2020, Song et al., 2021, Lipman et al.] and operator-learning frameworks [Kovachki et al., 2023, Li et al., 2021] have shown considerable promise: generative models provide expressive distributional approximations, while neural operators enable mappings between functional distributions. Recent progress has been made toward generative modeling directly in Hilbert or Banach spaces. Rahman et al. [2022], Seidman et al. [2023] proposed GANO and VANO, while Pidstrigach et al. [2024], Lim et al. [2023], Kerrigan et al. [2022, 2023], Franzese et al. [2023] develop the theoretical foundations for diffusion models and flow matching in infinite-dimensional settings. More details about related work are reviewed in Appendix A. Nevertheless, an important gap remains between the theory of functional generative modeling and its practical use for multiscale SPDE data. Most implementations still adopt standard Gaussian noise as the source distribution by default. For invariant measures with nontrivial spatial regularity, this choice can be poorly conditioned: the discretized Gaussian noise injects excessive high-frequency energy, forcing the learned velocity field to suppress unresolved modes and thereby making training and inference resolution-dependent. Existing remedies, such as interpolation schedules [Chen and Vanden-Eijnden, 2025], Schrödinger bridge formulations [Shi et al., 2023], and adaptive numerical solvers [Chen et al., 2024], can improve the transport dynamics or numerical integration, but they do not directly modify the source measure that induces the stiffness. We address this issue by treating the source measure as a design variable in functional flow matching. This perspective leads to an admissibility-constrained spectral-matching principle: the source must first be rough enough for function-space well-posedness, and should then be chosen to reduce covariance mismatch over the frequencies resolved by the model. Based on this principle, we propose NAGO , a noise-designed generative operator for sampling from multiscale SPDE invariant measures. By selecting an admissible Matérn source with a finite-band spectrum aligned to the target, NAGO 

reduces high-frequency mismatch and yields better-conditioned marginal velocity fields. We support this approach with an infinite-dimensional analysis, a quantitative link between source–target covariance ratios and spectral stiffness, and an operational source-selection rule. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We identify a function-space mechanism behind source design: source regularity determines admissibility, while the resolved source–target covariance ratio governs spectral stiffness. • We develop an admissibility-constrained spectral-matching rule for selecting Matérn sources from target samples, including both smoothness and correlation-length selection. • Across three stochastic-field benchmarks, we demonstrate improved few-step generation and complementary distributional statistics. A controlled ablation reduces the accurate RK4 budget by 6–10 × relative to a Gaussian-source model; a separate post-training benchmark shows an 89 .1× raw-throughput ratio to the SNS solver.

# 3 Methodology 

The framework of the proposed Noise-Aware Generative Operator ( NAGO ) is presented in this section. We first introduce the problem setting and establish the notation used throughout the paper. We then extend the theoretical framework for infinite-dimensional generative models, focusing on how different noise measures affect the marginal velocity field in flow matching. Finally, we describe the complete NAGO framework together with the associated algorithmic strategy. 

3.1 Problem Setting 

Our goal is to generate independent functional samples from the invariant distribution μ defined in Def. (2.3), without explicitly simulating the underlying SPDE over long time horizons. In addition to unconditional generation, a learned generative model should support reconstruction under partial observations and enable principled uncertainty quantification through guided sampling.

4.2 Generation at Varying Resolutions 

We next separate same-resolution generation at 64 2 from transfer to 32 2 and 128 2. As shown in Fig. 3 (C3), the Matérn source better preserves the reference spectrum across these grids. At 128 2, it retains 93 .99% of energy in the frequency band shared with training and places 2.39% in newly available modes, compared with 23 .64% and 76 .92% for the Gaussian source (Appendix F.9). These results show preservation of the learned shared-frequency distribution and suppression of spurious new-band energy, not reconstruction of an unseen continuum tail. Indeed, the 128 2 Matérn palinstrophy ratio remains 6.72, so fine-scale extrapolation is still imperfect.

# 5 Conclusion and Limitations 

In this work, we introduced NAGO , a noise-aware generative operator for sampling invariant measures of SPDEs. The key idea is to impose function-space admissibility first and then align the source and target covariance over the modes represented by the model. This separates the theoretical roughness requirement from the practical objective of reducing spectral stiffness. Across three stationary stochastic-field benchmarks, the selected Matérn source improves few-step spectral accuracy and several complementary distributional statistics; on the controlled Matérn target, it reduces the accurate RK4 budget from about 30 to 3–5 steps. The scope is nevertheless limited to stationary fields on periodic regular grids, where spectra can be estimated reliably and Matérn sampling is FFT-diagonalizable. Cross-resolution results preserve shared frequencies but do not recover an unseen spectral tail, and guidance comparisons remain metric-and hyperparameter-dependent. Extending the admissibility-constrained criterion to irregular geometries, nonstationary spectra, and learned anisotropic sources is an important direction for future work.

# A Related Work 

In recent years, both operator-learning models and generative modeling techniques have advanced rapidly. We begin by briefly reviewing existing research on neural operators and their applications to modeling SPDEs. We then discuss recent progress in generative modeling within scientific computing and highlight how our approach differs from prior work in both areas. 

A.1 Neural Solvers 

Numerous neural solvers have been proposed to approximate the solutions or operators of PDEs and SPDEs using neural networks. Raissi et al. [2019] introduced Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), in which a neural network is optimized using PDE constraints as self-supervised losses to obtain PDE solutions. These methods have also been extended to solve SPDEs [Zhang et al., 2020, Neufeld and Schmocker, 2024], although they are difficult to generalize across different types of SPDEs. Among neural operators, the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) [Kovachki et al., 2023, Li et al., 2021] and DeepONet [Lu et al., 2021] are among the most widely used due to their representative architectures, which were designed with universal approximation properties. The primary objective of neural operators is to learn mappings between distributions in function spaces. Many other related operator-based approaches have been proposed to address specific challenges, such as irregular domains and data [Liu et al., 2025b,a], as well as long-term forecasting [Liu et al., 2024a,b]. These methods have demonstrated strong performance across a variety of tasks; however, their applicability remains largely restricted to PDE-related problems. By incorporating time-dependent randomness, Neural SPDE [Salvi et al., 2022] and DLRNet [Gong et al., 2023, Hu et al., 2022, 2025] have been proposed for modeling SPDE operators. The distinction between modeling SPDE operators and PDE operators lies in the fact that the former accounts for infinite-dimensional, time-dependent randomness, whereas the latter does not. In the work of Salvi et al. [2022], kernel-based methods were employed to handle time evolution by learning the semigroup operator generated by the differential operator. In the work of Gong et al. [2023], the regularity features of SPDEs were combined with neural networks to improve generalization; however, the computational cost becomes prohibitively high for high-order signatures. However, in real-world SPDE dynamics, the driving noise is rarely observable; only system states at selected spatial and temporal points are typically available. Moreover, numerical solvers require extremely fine temporal discretization, making full-trajectory prediction computationally costly. In this work, we therefore focus not on forecasting the SPDE evolution, but on sampling from its stationary distribution once equilibrium is reached. For example, in thin-film growth described by the EW/KPZ equations under fixed operating conditions, our interest lies in the stabilized surface statistics rather than the transient dynamics. Importantly, all objects of interest reside in function spaces, making operator-learning models essential for accurately capturing the underlying infinite-dimensional structure. 

A.2 Generative Models 

In recent years, rapid progress has been made in generative modeling, including DDPM [Ho et al., 2020], DDIM [Song et al.], score-based models [Song et al., 2021], and flow matching [Lipman et al.]. Beyond finite-dimensional settings, these ideas have been extended to infinite-dimensional generative models that aim to directly represent distributions over function spaces. For example, Pidstrigach et al. [2024], Lim et al. [2023], Kerrigan et al. [2022], Franzese et al. [2023] propose score- or diffusion-based approaches that are invariant to discretization and operate directly in Hilbert or Banach spaces, thereby enabling a variety of applications in scientific machine learning. Although FFM [Kerrigan et al., 2023] establishes functional flow matching, it uses Gaussian sources and does not empirically isolate source design. Other functional models adapt GAN or VAE architectures [Na et al., 2025, Rahman et al., 2022, Seidman et al., 2023], while recent work designs interpolants or conditional velocity fields [Albergo et al., 2025, Chen and Vanden-Eijnden, 2025, Chen et al., 2025, Boffi et al., 2025]. Functional Mean Flow targets one-step generation in Hilbert space [Li et al., 2025], and FunDPS develops guided diffusion sampling and an infinite-dimensional Tweedie formula for PDE inverse problems [Yao et al., 2025]. NAGO is complementary: it isolates the source measure and links its continuum admissibility and finite-band covariance ratio to spectral stiffness. Source design has also emerged in finite-dimensional flow matching. LeDiFlow learns a data-dependent image prior to shorten probability paths [Zwick et al., 2025]; Better Source, Better Flow learns condition-dependent text-to-image sources with variance and directional regularization [Kim et al., 2026]; and Optimal Transport Flow Matching by Design constructs a low-frequency image prior that admits an approximately optimal identity coupling [Malnick et al., 2026]. These works establish that source distributions matter beyond our setting. Our contribution is narrower and different: an analytic, admissibility-constrained source rule for probability measures on function spaces, with a spectral-stiffness analysis and cross-resolution SPDE evaluation. 14 NAGO: Noise-Aware Generative Operator via Flow Matching 

A crucial design choice in diffusion-based generative models is the noise schedule, i.e., how noise levels are varied during the forward and reverse processes. Prior work has shown that the noise schedule significantly affects model behavior: for example, different noise schedules can shift optimal performance and robustness across tasks and resolutions, and the choice of schedule often needs to be tailored rather than fixed [Chen, 2023]. Subsequent research has undertaken both theoretical and empirical analysis of noise schedules, proposing new metrics and schedule designs to improve generation quality and training dynamics [Hang et al., 2025, Guo et al., 2023]. Moreover, studies focusing on specific application domains (e.g., design plausibility in conditional diffusion) have explored parametric noise scheduling to balance generative fidelity and structural constraints [Fan et al., 2023]. Together, these works highlight that noise choice and scheduling are fundamental for stable, high-quality generation in diffusion models. 

A.3 Difference between finite and infinite dimensional generative models. 

Generative models differ fundamentally depending on whether they are formulated in finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces or in infinite-dimensional function spaces. In finite-dimensional settings, the target distribution is defined on a fixed space Rd, where the dimension d is prescribed by the chosen representation. For example, an image model may operate on a fixed pixel grid, while a latent-variable model may operate on a fixed-dimensional feature vector. In this regime, probability distributions can often be described through densities with respect to the Lebesgue measure, and generative dynamics such as diffusion processes, score-based models, normalizing flows, or flow matching can be formulated as stochastic or deterministic differential equations on Rd. The associated analysis typically relies on finite-dimensional calculus, including gradients, divergences, Jacobians, change-of-variables formulas, and numerical discretizations of ordinary or stochastic differential equations. Function-space generative modeling has a different mathematical structure. Here the target object is a probability measure on a space of functions or fields, such as a Hilbert space, a Banach space, or a Sobolev-type space. Samples are not merely finite vectors, but discretized observations of continuum objects. Typical examples include Gaussian random fields, solutions of stochastic partial differential equations, invariant measures of stochastic dynamics, and continuous physical fields. This change of state space affects the basic theoretical language of generative modeling. In infinite-dimensional spaces, there is no translation-invariant Lebesgue reference measure. Consequently, densities, likelihoods, and scores cannot in general be interpreted in the same way as in Rd. Instead, the construction of generative dynamics must be formulated at the level of probability measures, operators, covariance structures, and infinite-dimensional evolution equations. Questions such as existence, uniqueness, regularity, and stability become central, since the flow must be well defined as a continuum object rather than only after a finite discretization. The second major difference concerns the role of spatial resolution. In a finite-dimensional model, the resolution is usually part of the definition of the data representation. Changing the resolution changes the ambient dimension and therefore typically defines a different finite-dimensional modeling problem. For example, an image model trained on 

64 × 64 images and another model trained on 256 × 256 images are usually treated as models on different Euclidean spaces. By contrast, in function-space generative modeling, different resolutions should be understood as different discretizations of the same underlying continuum measure. A model that is genuinely defined in function space should therefore be discretization-consistent: as the grid is refined, the learned distribution and the induced generative dynamics should converge to a well-defined limiting object. Equivalently, the model should not rely on artifacts of a particular grid size. This distinction is especially important for multiscale SPDE data. The target invariant measures often have nontrivial spatial regularity and strongly nonuniform spectral structure. If the source noise is chosen without regard to this regularity, its discretized high-frequency content may dominate the induced transport dynamics. For instance, standard discretized Gaussian noise can inject substantial energy into modes that are not representative of the target measure. The learned velocity field must then spend significant capacity suppressing these unresolved high-frequency components. This can lead to spectrally stiff marginal velocity fields, unstable training, and generation behavior that changes with the spatial resolution. Thus, in function-space generative modeling, the source noise is not merely an implementation detail. It directly affects whether the learned flow is well conditioned, resolution robust, and compatible with the target continuum measure. This perspective motivates the regularity-aware noise design considered in this work. Rather than fixing the source distribution to be standard Gaussian noise at every discretization level, we choose a noise measure whose regularity is matched to that of the target invariant measure. The goal is to reduce high-frequency mismatch at the level of the continuum formulation, thereby producing better-conditioned marginal velocity fields and more stable discretization-consistent generation. 15 NAGO: Noise-Aware Generative Operator via Flow Matching 

# B Invariant measures 

In many engineering and scientific applications, the quantities of interest are not deterministic functions but uncertain spatial fields. For example, the permeability of a subsurface medium may vary sharply across space and cannot be fully determined from limited observations. Representing such a parameter by a single fixed function therefore ignores the uncertainty that is essential for prediction and decision-making. A more appropriate formulation is to model the parameter as a random field and characterize its distribution through a probability measure on a function space. In SPDE-based modeling, the invariant measure of a stochastic dynamics provides such a statistical description: it encodes the long-term spatial structure, variability, and correlations of the field. Sampling from this invariant measure then produces statistically plausible realizations of the unknown medium, which can be used for uncertainty propagation, inverse problems, risk assessment, and design optimization. 

Why are multiscale invariant measures challenging for functional generative models? Multiscale SPDE invariant measures often contain spatial structures across a wide range of frequencies. A functional generative model must therefore learn not only the distribution of samples, but also a resolution-consistent transport mechanism between probability measures on function spaces. In practice, many implementations use standard Gaussian noise as the source distribution. This choice is convenient, but it can be poorly matched to the target invariant measure. When the source noise is much rougher than the data, it injects excessive high-frequency energy into the generative path. The induced marginal velocity field must then transport rough noise into smoother target fields, which creates strong spectral imbalance and leads to stiff dynamics. This stiffness can make training unstable and sampling expensive, especially when the spatial resolution changes. 

Why are interpolation schedules or adaptive solvers not enough? Existing methods often try to improve stability through interpolation schedules [Tong et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2024], guidance strategies, or specialized ODE solvers. These techniques can be useful, but they mainly act on the generative path or on the numerical integration after the source and target measures have already determined the geometry of the flow. If the initial noise measure is strongly mismatched with the target regularity, the resulting marginal velocity field is already poorly conditioned. Downstream heuristics may reduce some symptoms of stiffness, but they do not remove its source. As a result, they may require additional tuning, increase computational cost, or fail to remain robust across discretization resolutions. 

Our perspective: design the noise measure in function space. We instead treat the source noise measure as a design variable. The central idea is that the geometry of the functional flow should be controlled at the level of probability measures, rather than corrected only through downstream mechanisms. By choosing a noise distribution whose regularity is compatible with that of the target invariant measure, we can reduce high-frequency mismatch before it is inherited by the marginal velocity field. This leads to better-conditioned flows, more stable training, and improved cross-resolution behavior.

# E Algorithm of NAGO 

Why spectral analysis matters for scientific data. For data generated by PDEs and SPDEs, the frequency domain provides a natural description of both physical structure and numerical difficulty. The decay of spectral energy reflects the regularity of the underlying random fields, while the distribution of energy across modes determines how much large-scale and small-scale variation must be transported by the generative flow. This is particularly important in function-space generation: a source noise distribution with excessive high-frequency energy may look harmless after discretization, but it can induce a spectrally stiff marginal velocity field and cause resolution-dependent artifacts. Conversely, a generated sample can have a small pointwise error while still failing to reproduce the correct multiscale energy distribution. We therefore use spectral diagnostics as a central evaluation tool. They allow us to test whether the learned model preserves the target measure across spatial scales and whether the proposed regularity-aware noise design improves stability beyond visual sample quality or grid-based errors alone. 30 NAGO: Noise-Aware Generative Operator via Flow Matching

# F Experiments Details 

This section provides the experimental details needed to reproduce and interpret the reported results. All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA A40 GPU with 48 GB of memory. We document the full data preprocessing pipeline, model architecture, optimizer settings, learning-rate schedule, batch size, and training duration. Unless otherwise stated, all comparisons are performed under the same experimental configuration across different noise choices. In particular, we keep the backbone architecture, training budget, optimizer, and evaluation protocol fixed, and vary only the noise formulation. This controlled setup allows us to isolate the effect of noise design on training stability, sampling efficiency, and generation quality. We also include ablation studies to examine the sensitivity of the proposed method to key hyperparameters, including the noise smoothness, correlation length, regularization strength, and sampling-step configuration. These experiments are designed to identify parameter regimes in which the method remains stable and to assess whether the observed improvements are robust to reasonable hyperparameter changes. The complete experimental results are reported in the appendix. In addition to the summary results presented in the main text, we provide per-metric comparisons, convergence curves, run-to-run variability, and qualitative visualizations. Where appropriate, we also include diagnostic plots, such as training loss curves and stability indicators across samples or time steps, to give a more complete picture of model behavior. Overall, the results show that Matérn noise provides consistent advantages over Gaussian noise under matched experimental settings. During training, it leads to faster convergence and reduced sensitivity to hyperparameter choices. During inference, it improves numerical stability and produces more reliable samples, with fewer resolution-dependent artifacts and unstable trajectories. These findings support the central claim of this work: regularity-aware noise design improves both the optimization behavior and the inference-time robustness of functional generative models for multiscale SPDE data. 

F.1 Baselines 

Our baselines are constructed from standard choices commonly used in flow-matching generative models, but we report them according to their two key components: the source noise distribution and the neural backbone. This notation makes the comparison more transparent for our purpose, since the central question of this work is how the choice of noise affects training and sampling under otherwise matched settings. For example, “Gaussian + FNO”, also known as DiffusionPDE [Huang et al., 2024] denotes a standard flow-matching model using Gaussian source noise and an FNO backbone, which is also known as FFM [Kerrigan et al., 2023], while “Matérn + FNO” replaces only the source noise with the proposed regularity-aware Matérn noise. We use the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) as the primary backbone because it is a widely used operator-learning architecture for PDE data and can efficiently capture global interactions through spectral representations. When U-Net is used, the model is instantiated as a conventional flow-matching model on Euclidean grids and is therefore treated as a standard baseline rather than as part of our operator-based formulation. Across all backbone choices, we keep the optimizer, training budget, data preprocessing, interpolation schedule, sampling protocol, and evaluation metrics fixed unless otherwise stated. Remark F.1 (Non-monotonic error with respect to ODE steps) . In several experiments, we observe that increasing the number of ODE integration steps does not always monotonically improve the generation error. For example, in Table 2, the Matérn FNO error increases when the number of steps is increased from a small value to a larger one, and a similar non-monotonic behavior appears in guided generation. This phenomenon does not contradict the standard intuition that smaller ODE time steps reduce numerical discretization error. In a learned flow model, the total sampling error is affected not only by the solver discretization error, but also by the approximation error of the learned marginal velocity field. Increasing the number of integration steps reduces the former, but it also increases the number of evaluations of the learned velocity field. Therefore, small biased errors in the learned field may accumulate along the sampling trajectory. This accumulation can be more significant in spectrally stiff regimes, where high-frequency components are more sensitive to velocity-field errors.

F.11 FFT Matérn Sampling Cost 

For the stationary periodic-grid setting used here, the Matérn covariance is diagonal in the Fourier basis. For a batch of 

B fields on an N d grid, we draw white Fourier coefficients, multiply each coefficient by (κ2 + ∥k∥2)−α/ 2, impose Hermitian symmetry for real fields, and apply an inverse FFT. This requires O(BN d log N ) time and O(BN d) memory. No dense N d × N d covariance matrix is constructed or factorized. This advantage does not directly extend to irregular domains where Fourier diagonalization is unavailable.

# G Limitations of This Study 

The current theory and implementation assume stationary measures on periodic regular grids. Under these assumptions, the spectrum is diagonal in a Fourier basis and Matérn samples are generated efficiently by FFT. On irregular geometries, nonperiodic domains, or nonstationary targets, estimating a reliable spectrum and applying the same sampler are nontrivial. The finite-band selection rule also depends on the sample size and on the band used to estimate bβ; uncertainty in this estimate is not yet propagated into source selection. The empirical evidence is also bounded. Most comparisons use one trained checkpoint per configuration, so repeated evaluation seeds measure conditional sampling variability rather than training variability. The anisotropic experiment uses an isotropic source and shows accurate recovery of a stationary directional target, but it does not establish uniform superiority or address spatially varying anisotropy. Cross-resolution tests preserve the represented frequency band but do not reconstruct an unseen spectral tail. Finally, the guidance results depend on the metric and on how guidance strength is normalized across solver steps. We also emphasize that the scope of this work is sampling from invariant measures of SPDEs, rather than general PDE solution generation or image generation. For deterministic PDE prediction, generative models are usually less efficient than neural operator methods when the goal is to approximate the forward solution map, since iterative sampling is not naturally optimized for fast surrogate modeling. Therefore, we do not position the proposed method as a replacement for operator-learning approaches in standard PDE forecasting tasks. Similarly, this work does not focus on image-generation benchmarks. Our resolution-consistency analysis is motivated by the function-space nature of SPDEs, where samples are discretizations of underlying random fields and should remain consistent under changes of spatial resolution. This consideration is fundamentally different from common image-generation settings, where low- and high-resolution images are usually treated as distinct finite-dimensional data distributions rather than discretizations of the same function-space object. 43 NAGO: Noise-Aware Generative Operator via Flow Matching
