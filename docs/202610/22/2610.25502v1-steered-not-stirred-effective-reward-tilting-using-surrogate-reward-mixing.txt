ABSTRACT
Continuous-time generative models, from diffusion models, flow matching, and
now flow maps, owe their practical value to high-fidelity generation and steerability.
In many applications, a ground-truth latent “oracle” reward R∗is unavailable
or expensive to evaluate, e.g., human judgments or experimental measurements.
Although the oracle reward is not directly observable, it can often be approximated
through multiple cheaper surrogates that, individually, provide incomplete
characterizations but collectively reveal substantial information about the
downstream task. In this paper, we present a method for composing such surrogates
to steer a pretrained model toward the distribution tilted by an unavailable R∗.
Given a small feedback set of oracle rankings or numerical rewards, we introduce
Mixing And Reward Tilting for INference-time Improvement (MARTINI), which
learns a surrogate composition for plug-and-play use with standard inference-time
steering methods. Our theory characterizes surrogate information limits and
relates population recovery error to ideal-target KL at small tilt strength. For
preferences, the bound additionally accounts for an irreducible mismatch between
the Plackett–Luce population target and the conditional mean.
Experiments
demonstrate improved oracle-tilt fidelity in language generation and ImageNet
alignment, and improved joint pose accuracy and validity in protein–ligand
cofolding, without inference-time oracle queries.

INTRODUCTION
In many real-world deployments, generative models are judged not only by sample fidelity to the
pretraining distribution, but by whether their generations satisfy downstream task-specific objec-
tives (Zhang et al., 2026). Consequently, it remains imperative to align generative models to desired
rewards through algorithmic frameworks such as inference-time steering or post-training (Uehara
et al., 2025) that, in effect, sharpen the model distribution on samples with high downstream utility,
where that utility is too costly or inaccessible to supervise at pretraining scale. The central discrepancy
between pre-training and downstream task alignment, therefore, requires high-quality post-training
data under an oracle reward, whose acquisition is often bottlenecked due to high collection costs (Gao
et al., 2023), from capturing the nature of human preferences (Rafailov et al., 2023; Ethayarajh et al.,
2024) to experimental measurements needed to evaluate biomolecular design (Pathak et al., 2025).
In lieu of carte blanche access to a reward oracle that is perfectly predictive of a desired downstream
task, practitioners often have access to multiple weakly informative signals in the form of surrogate or
proxy reward models. In text-to-image generation, for example, human preferences are approximated
by aesthetic, alignment, and learned preference scores such as, ImageReward (Xu et al., 2023),
CLIP (Radford et al., 2021), and PickScore (Kirstain et al., 2023). In molecular and protein design,
physical plausibility checks (Buttenschoen et al., 2024) and machine-learned interatomic poten-
tials (Wood et al., 2025) similarly serve as proxies for physical assays or high-fidelity simulations.
In each example, cheap proxy reward signals are individually imperfect and only weakly correlated

proxies to recover the latent oracle objective, rather than standard multi-objective optimization that
treats surrogates as separate objectives. This motivates a fundamental question: How can multiple
weak reward surrogates be composed to steer a pretrained diffusion or flow model towards the
reward-tilted distribution favored by an unavailable oracle?
Present work. We study steering toward the reward-tilted distribution of a latent oracle reward,
R∗, available only on a small annotated feedback set, using multiple cheap surrogates and no query
access to R∗at inference. More precisely, we formulate two settings representative of real-world
use, in which the feedback set supplies either preference rankings over candidate samples or direct
scalar reward values. We introduce MARTINI (Mixing And Reward Tilting for INference-time
Improvement, Figure 1), which learns a composition of the surrogates from that feedback and allows
plug-and-play deployment in existing reward steering frameworks. MARTINI exploits complementary
surrogate information to improve steering toward R∗, targeting a different solution concept from
Pareto optimization. We summarize our main contributions as follows:
1. Methods. We develop a novel algorithm in MARTINI that exploits the problem structure of
steering towards a gold reward R∗, witnessed only on a feedback set, by learning a composition
function over cheap reward surrogates that can readily interface with existing steering pipelines.
2. Theory.
We characterize the information limit of the surrogates and identify the exact
KL-optimal composition. Conditional-mean regression minimizes recovery error and the leading
small-guidance KL coefficient. We also bound reward gain and gap, and relate preference fitting
to these quantities through the discrepancy between its population target and the conditional mean.
3. Results. We evaluate MARTINI on language generation, ImageNet text alignment, and protein–
ligand cofolding, as well as toy experiments, spanning FKS (Singhal et al., 2025), FMRG (Huang
et al., 2026), MFM (Potaptchik et al., 2026), and Itô steering (Pan et al., 2026) methods.

BACKGROUND AND PRELIMINARIES
Notation. We denote by X = Rd the state space of the generative dynamics and by p1 the
data distribution on this space, using embedded representations for discrete data. A reward is a
scalar function R : X →R. A superscript asterisk marks the gold reward R∗, unqueryable at
inference. We use R to denote the vector of k surrogate rewards R = (R1, . . . , Rk) : X →Rk,
and r = (r1, . . . , rk) ∈Rk values it takes. A composition function g : Rk →R turns the surrogates

its induced tilt. All such tilts assign zero probability to sets of zero p1-probability. In particular, we

Specifically, ¯v quantifies the additional information about R∗that the surrogates do not contain.
Finally, SCC and PCC denote the Spearman and Pearson correlation coefficients, respectively.

Continuous treatment of discrete data. Let V be a vocabulary and L a sequence length, such that
token sequences satisfy y ∈VL. Here, X = RL×|V|, where d = L|V|, and we use tokenwise one-hot

this representation preserves the text distribution exactly. We apply the preceding interpolant and
flow-map construction to p1, with x1 = Enc(y) and x0 ∼N(0, I). The intermediate marginals pt
have smooth densities and converge in distribution to p1 as t →1. Learned continuous terminal
predictions are then decoded by tokenwise argmax. Rewards on one-hot encodings are defined by
the corresponding text rewards.

METHOD
MARTINI learns a composition of fixed surrogates from an offline feedback set Dfeedback, distinct
from the generator’s training data Dtrain. Feedback consists of numerical oracle rewards or rankings;
the fitted composition drives inference-time steering without oracle queries.
3.1

At a given tilt strength β > 0, we seek a surrogate tilt qg approximating q∗∝p1 exp(βR∗),
using k cheap surrogates stacked together as R. We reiterate that R∗is not available at inference;
instead, we only have knowledge of the gold reward through a feedback set Dfeedback. We seek
gopt ∈arg ming KL(q∗∥qg) over measurable compositions with finite, positive tilt normalizers.

Relation to multi-objective optimization. In multi-objective optimization (MOO), the Rj are
distinct objectives. For maximized rewards, x dominates x′ if Rj(x) ≥Rj(x′) for every j, with
at least one strict inequality. Undominated feasible candidates form the Pareto set, where their reward
vectors form the Pareto front, and scalarizations express trade-offs among the objectives.
Our surrogates instead provide imperfect information about one oracle R∗. A candidate can dominate
another on every surrogate yet receive a lower oracle reward, so the surrogate Pareto set can exclude
the oracle optimum (lemmas 4 and 5 and Figure 2). We therefore allow nonmonotone compositions,
although no composition can recover information absent from its inputs. Further discussions are in §K.

3.3
THEORETICAL RESULTS
Our setting raises two questions: Q1. when does learning to predict R∗from surrogates lead to useful
steering, and Q2. how well can any composition of the surrogates do? The objectives in Equations (3)
and (4) use oracle feedback, whereas our goal is to approximate q∗. Our results connect these
questions and identify the limits imposed by information about R∗missing from the surrogates.
We examine four quantities: Steering divergence, KL(q∗∥qg), measures fidelity to the oracle tilt,
and reward gain and reward gap compare the induced distribution’s expected R∗reward with that of
p1 and q∗respectively. Finally, recovery error measures how accurately g ◦R predicts R∗under p1.
We analyze population quantities and ideal tilts under the assumptions stated in §G.1. The KL and
reward expansions in the theorems hold for fixed compositions as β ↓0, while the recovery identities
and bounds are exact. For both theorems, we use mean-aligned representatives (Assumption G.3),
which preserve the induced tilt. For conditional generation, i.e. p1(·|c) we fix the context c in the
analysis and drop the notation for brevity.

Part (i) answers Q1 by connecting the value-fitting objective directly to steering: reducing population
MSE reduces the leading KL error. This also explains why the unrestricted regression target µ is useful
even though the exact KL-optimal composition gopt can differ from it. Proposition 1 and Corollary 1
show that KL(q∗∥qµ) −KL(q∗∥qgopt) = O(β4) as β ↓0. Thus, using µ instead of gopt incurs only
fourth-order excess KL, providing a distributional justification for MSE fitting in this regime.
Part (iv) answers Q2: fitting can reduce the discrepancy from µ, but recovery error remains at least
¯v, the variation in R∗unexplained by the surrogates. Improving the fit of g therefore addresses only
one source of error, and reducing this floor requires more informative surrogate inputs (Corollary 5).
Parts (ii)–(iii) relate recovery error to expected reward after steering under R∗. At leading order,
reducing recovery error raises the gain lower bound and lowers the gap upper bound. When
Varp1(µ) > 0, sufficiently accurate approximation of µ guarantees positive gain for small enough
β > 0, even if ¯v > 0 (Corollary 2). Useful steering therefore does not require perfect oracle recovery.

it to learned surrogate compositions through asymptotic gain and gap bounds.

EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate MARTINI on language generation, text-to-image alignment, and protein–ligand cofolding,
fitting g offline and steering without inference-time oracle queries, with synthetic experiments in §I.
Evaluation. For language and images, we assess recovery using held-out PCC and MSE for values,
or SCC and PL loss for preferences. Experimental tilts use the sampler law p1,ϕ in place of p1.
We evaluate fixed finite target tilts β = 1 and sweep sampler guidance separately, probing behavior
beyond our small-β guarantees. Composition fidelity is measured by KL(q∗∥qg), estimated from
unsteered samples. We assess generated samples using embedding-space energy distance and
distances on the R∗marginal, including Wasserstein distances and Gaussian and KDE estimates
of KL. We also report the absolute reward gap and a diversity-adjusted cost Q (prior: 1 and lower
is better), which evaluates reward gains accompanied by diversity loss. Definitions and estimators
appear in §C. Specific protein cofolding metrics are described in §4.3.
Language and Image Baselines. Best-of-N (BoN), reported in Appendix ablation tables, selects
the highest-R∗candidate, requiring N oracle evaluations per sample. Deployable steering baselines
are the best single surrogate (BSS), uniform weighting, and ParetoFlow (Yuan et al., 2025), plus
ReNO’s fixed reward weights for images (Eyring et al., 2024). BSS is selected on training feedback
using either PCC or SCC depending on regime. We also evaluate SLOP’s calibrated selection
protocol (Wang et al., 2026). SLOP requires R∗values, so its preference results serve only as a
value-calibrated reference.

PROTEIN-LIGAND CO-FOLDING
Protein–ligand cofolding provides a setting with experimental scalar feedback. We ask whether a sur-
rogate composition learned from binding-affinity measurements can improve the accuracy and physi-
cal validity of the jointly generated poses. Qualitative results shown in Figure 3 (right) and Figure 21.
Setup. Using the value regime, we fit a composition of five surrogates to experimental pKD mea-
surements from HiQBind (Wang et al., 2025b), restricting the feedback set to PoseBusters-valid
monomers and multimers (Buttenschoen et al., 2024). The fitted composition g ◦R then steers the
DeCAF-Boltz flow map (Scarpellini et al., 2026), distilled from Boltz-1 (Wohlwend et al., 2025), with-
out further experimental feedback. We evaluate on 186 complexes from the post-2023 subset of Runs
N’ Poses (Škrinjar et al., 2026), beyond Boltz-1’s training cutoff. As these evaluation complexes lack
measured affinities, we assess transfer through pose accuracy and physical validity, reporting best@1
metrics averaged over five seeds. Dataset filtering, surrogate definitions, and steering details are in §F.
Metrics.
Our success rate is the percentage of predictions with RMSD below 2 Å that pass
PoseBusters validity checks (Buttenschoen et al., 2024). We also report accuracy and validity
rates alongside lDDT-PLI, which measures local protein–ligand distance agreement within a 6 Å
pocket (Mariani et al., 2013), as well as structural diversity detailed in §F.3.
Results. The BSS is smina (Figure 8). MARTINI (correlation g) achieves the highest reported best@1
(§F.3) success rate (64.5%) over both potentials-only steering and smina + potentials by 2.7 percentage
points (Table 2). Relative to potentials-only, lDDT-PLI improves by 1.9 points, indicating better
local geometry, with similar diversity (0.0133 versus 0.0132). PB-validity falls by 6.5 points, even
though MARTINI trades validity for accuracy, all the accurate predictions also satisfy validity criteria.
Ablation study results and success rates for reward-selected poses are presented in Appendix F.4.

RELATED WORK
Reward-based control. Generative models can be aligned through reinforcement learning (Black
et al., 2024) or preference optimisation (Rafailov et al., 2023; Wallace et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2026),
and steered at inference through guidance (Bansal et al., 2023; Singhal et al., 2025), posterior
sampling (Chung et al., 2023), or flow-map optimal control (Huang et al., 2026).
MARTINI
complements these methods by learning the steering reward from offline oracle feedback.
Reward compositions and limited feedback.
Reward ensembles can mitigate over-
optimisation (Coste et al., 2024).
ReNO uses fixed reward weights (Eyring et al., 2024),
while SLOP calibrates pooled scores to maximize estimated gold reward (Wang et al., 2026). We
study approximation of a fixed oracle tilt from values or rankings, characterizing the information
limit imposed by the surrogates. Multi-objective methods balance distinct rewards (Ramé et al., 2023;
Yuan et al., 2025). Limited oracle access also motivates query-efficient fine-tuning (Uehara et al.,
2024), protein steering (Yang et al., 2025), and optimisation over generated samples (Willis et al.,
2025). We use feedback to learn how fixed surrogates predict a single oracle, keeping the generator
unchanged. Complementing analyses of proxy error and ensembling (Paes et al., 2026), we relate
fitting loss and surrogate information limits to steering fidelity and reward bounds.

compatible steerers without inference-time oracle queries. Our theory links fitting to ideal-tilt

ETHICS STATEMENT
Steering pretrained generative models, including language models, can amplify biases or errors in
the base model, oracle, or surrogate rewards, and can generate harmful content. Improvements in
measured reward do not establish safety, fairness, or factual correctness. Likewise, computational
protein–ligand metrics do not replace experimental validation. Applications require task-specific
evaluation and appropriate safeguards.

We fit a neural composition gθ using the appropriate value or preference objective with weight decay,

B.1
IMAGENET
We use the released model of Potaptchik et al. (2026) (mfm-xl2), a SiT-XL/2 transformer distilled
from the Decoupled MeanFlow ImageNet model (Lee et al., 2026b). It generates class-conditional
ImageNet images at 256 × 256 in the latent space of the Stable Diffusion VAE (sd-vae-ft-mse;
latents of shape 4 × 32 × 32, scaled by 0.18215), with noise at t = 0, data at t = 1 and the linear
interpolant xt = (1 −t)ε + t x1. The model is conditioned only on the ImageNet class label: the text
prompt never enters the model, and acts only through the rewards — R∗and the surrogates score the
decoded image against it.
Reward models. The oracle uses Skywork/Skywork-VL-Reward-7B. The four surrogates
are RE-N-Y/hpsv21, RE-N-Y/ImageReward, openai/clip-vit-base-patch16, and
yuvalkirstain/PickScore_v1. For each image and steering prompt, the oracle score is the
Yes/No logit margin,
R∗(x) = logitYes(x) −logitNo(x).
Target. q∗∝p1,ϕ exp(βz(R∗)), β = 1, is estimated by self-normalised reweighting of 8192
independent draws from the model’s reverse SDE (32 steps); the effective sample size of these
weights is reported per class in Table 4.
Feedback and fitting. ImageNet uses n=32 scalar R∗evaluations (value) or M=128 ranking queries
of m=8 candidates each (preference), drawn from the class’s pool. We fit separate composition
coefficients for each class–prompt pair. A fifth of the value labels (6) and a quarter of the preference
queries (32, built from images disjoint from the fitting queries) are held out; every family is fit on the
remainder, and the family is selected by held-out MSE (value) or Plackett–Luce loss (preference),
averaged over all 19 pairs. The linear family is selected in both regimes. The held-out part is used
only for this selection and is not refit. loss.
Preference utilities. The synthetic annotator sorts z(R∗) + σϵ with independent standard Gumbel
noise ϵ and σ = 0.5, which samples Plackett–Luce rankings with utilities z(R∗)/σ. We standardise
R∗once over the reference pool, fixing its scale across queries. In our eight-candidate calibration (a
standard-normal oracle), σ = 0.5 gives 82.7% pairwise agreement with the unperturbed oracle order.
This noise level is loosely informed by the approximately 81% non-tie human agreement reported on
MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023); agreement with an oracle order and agreement between annotators
are different quantities. A fitted utility f is expressed in PL units, so the tilt it implies on the reference
scale is exp(βσf). In practice we standardise the fitted composition over the pool before use, so the
steering reward and KLg both use exp(βz(f)), which coincides with exp(βσf) when f recovers the
utility exactly.
Pools and data splits. For each class–prompt pair and feedback regime, we draw an independent
pool of 8192 samples and score the oracle and surrogates. Within each regime, the pool supplies

feedback candidates, normalization statistics, and the importance-weighted reference §C. Held-out
recovery is measured on pool rows that no fit or selector touched, subsampled to at most 4000. Oracle

B.2
LANGUAGE GENERATION
We use the released FMLM checkpoint (Lee et al., 2026a) (david3684/FMLM-B-LM1B), trained
on One Billion Words (Chelba et al., 2013). Generation is unconditional, with 128 tokens from
the 30,522-token bert-base-uncased vocabulary generated in 8 flow-map steps, at softmax
temperature 1 and without per-step noise injection (γ = 0). We use FMRG steering and feedback
budgets of n = 256 numerical rewards or M = 256 rankings of m = 8 candidates, labelled by the
same Plackett–Luce annotator as ImageNet (σ = 0.5). Each evaluated setting is run with 5 seeds
of 256 generated samples; metrics are computed per seed and averaged, and |gap| is taken from the
seed-averaged mean.
The oracle is negative per-sequence generative perplexity under Qwen2.5-1.5B (Qwen et al.,
2025), scored in fp32 after removing the [CLS] separators that LM1B’s sentence packing
leaves inside each sample.
The surrogates are unmasked pseudo-log-likelihoods from BERT
and DistilBERT (bert-base-uncased, distilbert-base-uncased; the mean log-
probability the MLM head assigns to each token actually present, in a single forward pass with-
out masking), a CoLA acceptability score (the acceptable-minus-unacceptable logit margin of
textattack/bert-base-uncased-CoLA), and the empirical unigram entropy of the sample.
All four share the generator’s vocabulary, so their differentiable extensions read a distribution over
tokens directly: it is multiplied into each scorer’s word-embedding matrix (between the [CLS] and
[SEP] embeddings), and the entropy is taken of the position-averaged distribution. On one-hot
inputs these reduce exactly to the discrete scores. Held-out loss selects an MLP for values and a
linear composition for preferences.
Pools and steering. For each run, 2048 unsteered samples are scored by the oracle and all surrogates:
1536 rows supply the feedback and 512 are held out for recovery, and the whole pool, importance-
weighted, forms the q∗reference (ESS 1155 of 2048). FMRG follows each flow-map step with two
unit-normalised reward-gradient steps, x ←x + λ ∇xr/∥∇xr∥, where r is evaluated on the endpoint
prediction Xt,1(x) — one call of the two-time denoiser, on the vocabulary simplex — at every step.
The strength grid is λ ∈{0.00375, 0.0075, 0.015, 0.03, 0.06, 0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4}; figures and
tables show λ ≤0.125, where the methods separate. Candidates may recur across ranking queries,
so the M = 256 groups contain 2048 candidate occurrences rather than 2048 distinct samples.

F.1
SURROGATES
We fit the surrogate composition to experimental binding-affinity feedback and evaluate its transfer to
generated structures. The five surrogates provide information about structural quality, binding-site
energetics, and predicted affinity.
SMINA. SMINA is a widely used docking and scoring tool forked from AutoDock Vina and optimised
for high-throughput scoring and for user-specified scoring functions (Koes et al., 2013).
Frustration. The FrustratometeR R package Rausch et al. (2021) is used to calculate local energetic
frustration in protein structures, where high frustration relates to unstable local regions and correlates
with active sites.
MolProbity. MolProbity (Chen et al., 2010) assesses the physical quality of molecular structures
through all-atom steric contact analysis with explicit hydrogens, together with covalent-geometry and
backbone and side-chain torsion-angle criteria.

affinity surrogate. The Boltz-2 weights are used to featurise the complex, but the pose itself is taken
as atomic coordinates from the steering run (e.g. with DeCAF) and then used to constrain the affinity
prediction, so that the prediction head uses the steered poses. Default Boltz-2 settings were used for
this surrogate.

et al., 2025) to penalize stereochemical violations, clashes, and inconsistent chain geometry. The

RELATION TO MULTI-OBJECTIVE OPTIMISATION
Multi-objective optimization treats R1, . . . , Rk as objectives to be balanced. Here they provide
imperfect information about a single oracle reward R∗. We formalize why surrogate Pareto efficiency
need not identify candidates preferred by the oracle.
For rewards to be maximized, y dominates x if Rj(y) ≥Rj(x) for every j, with at least one strict
inequality. The nondominated feasible candidates form the Pareto set P; their reward vectors form
the Pareto front R(P).
Lemma 4 (Increasing compositions are Pareto efficient). If g is coordinatewise strictly increasing,
every maximizer of g ◦R over the feasible domain belongs to the surrogate Pareto set P.
This ordering need not agree with the oracle:
Lemma 5 (The oracle optimum can be dominated). There exist a base law and rewards with
¯v > 0 whose unique oracle maximizer is strictly dominated in every surrogate.
Example. Let x = (a, b) ∈[−1, 1]2 and p1 = Unif([−1, 1]2), with
R1(x) = −(a + 1)2 −b2,
R2(x) = −(a −1)2 −b2,
R∗(x) = −3(a2 + b2) + 2b −3.
The surrogate Pareto set is the segment P = [−1, 1] × {0}, whereas the oracle has the unique
maximizer x⋆= (0, 1/3). The point (0, 0) strictly dominates x⋆on both surrogates, but has lower
oracle reward:
R1(0, 0) = R2(0, 0) = −1 > −10/9 = R1(x⋆) = R2(x⋆),
R∗(0, 0) = −3 < −8/3 = R∗(x⋆).
Figure 2 illustrates the distinction.
Removing the monotonicity restriction allows compositions to favor points outside the surrogate
Pareto set, but does not recover missing information. Here R(a, b) = R(a, −b), so every composition
assigns the same score to x⋆and (0, −1/3) even though their oracle rewards differ.
