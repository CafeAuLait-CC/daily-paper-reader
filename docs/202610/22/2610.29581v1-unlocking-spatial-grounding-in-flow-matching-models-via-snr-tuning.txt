Abstract
Despite the success of foundation models in image synthesis, achieving fine-grained
spatial control remains a significant challenge, typically requiring auxiliary structural
adapters or complex inference-time steering. While modern vision-language (VLM) text
encoders possess the latent capacity to represent spatial coordinates, generative mod-
els often fail to leverage this information. We demonstrate that the signal-to-noise ratio
(SNR) distribution is a critical determinant of spatial grounding in flow-matching mod-
els. Using a novel synthetic benchmark of geometric primitives, we isolate structural
mechanics from textural complexity inherent in natural imagery and introduce a rigorous
color-aware layout metric to quantify spatial fidelity. We show that training on a centered
SNR distribution results in poor layout adherence (25.9%). Instead, we identify high-
noise regimes as a "critical learning phase" for spatial grounding. Shifting the training
focus toward these stages increases layout adherence to 80.8%, effectively reaching the
83.6% reconstruction ceiling of the underlying variational autoencoder. We find that the
necessity for this shift scales with resolution and aspect ratio variance, and that the con-
servative noise schedules employed by models like FLUX.1 are insufficient for precise
layout adherence. Finally, we show that these findings translate to real-world imagery via
the COCO dataset (1.0 vs. 57.3 Average Precision), where state-of-the-art layout adher-
ence is unlocked through distribution tuning alone, without architectural modifications.

Introduction
The rapid evolution of diffusion [17, 46] and flow-matching [29, 34] models is outpacing the
adoption of these models for specialized use cases. In particular, while new state-of-the-art
image synthesis models [4, 23, 47, 50] are frequently released, achieving fine-grained spa-
tial control over their outputs typically requires adopting one of two paradigms. The first
involves training-based methods, which utilize auxiliary structural adapters such as Control-
Net [58], GLIGEN [27], or CreatiLayout [57]. The second relies on training-free methods,
which steer the generation process of frozen backbones during inference to follow a target
layout. However, adapters are often tightly coupled to specific model architectures, making
them costly to retrain for each new release, while training-free methods often struggle with
complex layouts or specific architectural constraints. This creates a critical need to under-
stand why foundation models struggle to generate images that follow layouts from text alone.

Standard pre-trained models typically fail to interpret 2D coordinates embedded in text
prompts, often treating them as non-spatial semantic tokens rather than geometric con-
straints. In contrast, recent advancements in vision-language models (VLMs), such as the
Qwen-VL series [1, 2], demonstrate that multimodal embeddings can successfully encode
spatial relationships and discrete coordinates for tasks like object detection and grounding.
This suggests that their multimodal embedding space already possesses the latent capacity
to represent spatial information. As state-of-the-art text-to-image models increasingly adopt
VLM-based text encoders, it remains an open question why the generative process fails to
leverage this innate grounding capability. Therefore, we investigate whether, and under what
conditions, text-to-image models can learn to follow explicit 2D layout specifications en-
coded solely in text prompts.
To this end, we examine the grounding capabilities of flow-matching models by fine-tuning
them to interpret 2D coordinates directly from text prompts without introducing structural
modifications. To isolate the mechanisms of spatial grounding, we first construct a controlled
synthetic dataset of geometric primitives, enabling exact, pixel-level assessment of layout fi-
delity (Fig. 1(a)). Our analysis reveals that the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) distribution is
the critical determinant of spatial grounding. We find that training on a balanced SNR dis-
tribution actually hinders the model from accurately learning coordinate-based constraints.
Instead, biasing the training toward high-noise regimes, where global structure is established,
allows the model to internalize the relationship between text tokens and spatial coordinates.
Notably, we find that this distribution shift is necessary even at lower resolutions (2562), and
that the conservative shifts employed by recent models like FLUX.1 [24] are insufficient for
precise layout adherence. Finally, we demonstrate that these principles successfully translate
to real-world imagery via the COCO dataset [28] (Fig. 1(b)), showing that state-of-the-art
layout adherence can be unlocked in standard flow-matching models through distribution
tuning alone, without the need for specialized architectural extensions.
Taken together, our results demonstrate that high-fidelity spatial control is a latent capabil-
ity in state-of-the-art flow-matching models that can be unlocked by strategically shifting
the SNR distribution during training, without requiring any architectural modifications. We
show that the high-noise regime serves as a "critical learning phase" for spatial grounding,
forcing the model to internalize geometric constraints when global structure is not yet ob-
servable. Our main contributions are as follows:
• Identification of SNR as a Key Facilitator: We provide a systematic study showing
that biasing the SNR distribution toward high-noise regimes is essential for teaching
models to interpret 2D coordinates embedded in text.
• A Synthetic Benchmark for Spatial Precision: We introduce a benchmark consist-
ing of geometric primitives that enables the exact, pixel-level quantification of layout
adherence, isolating grounding mechanics from the textural complexities of real-world
images.
• Transfer to Real-World Imagery: We provide empirical evidence that these insights
translate to complex real-world imagery via the COCO dataset, achieving state-of-the-
art layout adherence in standard flow-matching models through distribution tuning
alone, bypassing the need for specialized structural adapters.

Related Work
Image Synthesis. Generative modeling has transitioned from Generative Adversarial Net-
works (GANs) [13] to Diffusion [8, 17, 46] and Flow Matching [29, 34] frameworks. Ar-
chitectures have similarly evolved from convolutional U-Nets (e.g., Stable Diffusion [42],
SDXL [39]) toward scalable Diffusion Transformers (DiT) [37] and Multimodal Diffusion
Transformers (MMDiT) [10, 22, 24]. Recent models [23, 50] replace traditional text en-
coders (e.g., CLIP [40] or T5 [41]) with Vision-Language Models (VLMs) [1, 2, 30] to
leverage pre-aligned latent spaces. However, while VLMs excel at spatial grounding tasks
like detection [1, 2], generative models using them still struggle to natively interpret 2D
coordinates within text prompts, necessitating external spatial control.
Training Dynamics and SNR Schedules. The performance of flow-matching and diffu-
sion models heavily depends on the timestep sampling distribution p(t), which determines
the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) levels encountered during training [3, 10, 21].
While
logit-normal distributions are typically tuned to improve visual fidelity based on resolution
[10, 18, 24] or dimensionality [59], their impact on spatial grounding is only beginning to
be examined. Recent work by Wu et al. [51] and Zhang et al. [57] provides evidence that
noise schedules influence layout adherence. However, their findings are coupled with spe-
cialized regional or siamese architectures. In contrast, our work demonstrates that the SNR
distribution alone serves as the primary lever for internalizing spatial-semantic mappings in
standard, structurally unmodified flow-matching models. We show that the acquisition of
spatial grounding from text prompts alone is highly sensitive to p(t), revealing that high-
noise regimes represent the essential window for anchoring textual coordinates to global
structural composition.
Layout-to-Image (L2I) Generation. Layout-to-image approaches address the limitations
of native spatial control by augmenting pre-trained synthesis models with explicit geometric
conditioning (e.g., bounding boxes or masks). This is increasingly viewed as a multi-stage
pipeline, beginning with upstream planning where Large Language Models (LLMs) or au-
toregressive VLMs translate linguistic descriptions into structural layouts [12, 14, 48]. Other
approaches bridge the gap between text and pixels by synthesizing intermediate representa-
tions, such as depth maps, to guide the final generative process [9, 62, 63]. Once a spatial
structure is established, the literature diverges into training-based and training-free strategies.
Training-based L2I methods adapt model architectures to interpret spatial signals via fine-
tuning or dedicated adapters [27, 54, 60]. These works have evolved to handle multi-instance

scenarios by using spatial attention disentanglement [49, 61] or hierarchical composition [6]
to minimize attribute leakage. Beyond image quality, these models often serve as data en-
gines to synthesize paired training data for perception tasks [11, 64]. Recently, MMDiT
architectures have been enhanced with siamese attention to isolate layout signals [57] and
hybrid control frameworks to achieve data-efficient grounding [51]. To avoid the high cost of
retraining, training-free L2I methods intervene in the sampling process of frozen backbones.
These strategies primarily manipulate internal cross-attention maps to ensure semantic iso-
lation of subjects [7, 53, 56] or employ optimization-based guidance to steer the denoising
trajectory toward specified coordinates [5, 26, 31, 38, 52]. Beyond attention manipulation,
compositional strategies decentralize the process by merging localized per-object noise es-
timates or synchronizing global and instance-specific denoising branches [25, 45]. Finally,
probabilistic approaches like Psi-Sampler [55] reinterpret layout adherence as an inference-
time reward alignment problem, using Sequential Monte Carlo sampling to filter trajectories.
While existing L2I methods typically rely on external modules to bypass spatial reasoning
limitations, this study investigates a more fundamental question: can flow-matching models
acquire spatial grounding from text prompts alone, without any structural modifications?

Method
3.1
Synthetic Benchmark for Text-Layout Image Generation
To facilitate training and provide a controlled environment for evaluation, we construct a
synthetic dataset comprising 100,000 training pairs. For rigorous quantitative evaluation,
we hold out a curated benchmark set of 1,000 text-image pairs. Images are rendered at
resolution W × H and contain n ∼U{1,10} rectangles. In our standard benchmark, we set
W = H = 256. Notably, our generation pipeline is agnostic to resolution and aspect ratio,
enabling us to evaluate scalability across higher resolutions (e.g., 10242) and varying aspect
ratios, as detailed in Section 4.2. By utilizing simple geometric primitives, this approach
mitigates the textural complexity and photometric noise present in real-world images. This
simplification enables us to disentangle spatial control from semantic rendering, allowing for
a systematic and quantitative evaluation of a model’s geometric layout compliance.

3.2
Layout Adherence Metric
To assess the spatial fidelity of generated images relative to specified text layouts, we in-
troduce a Layout Adherence Metric based on color-coded segmentation masks. This metric
quantifies the alignment between the ground-truth image Igt and synthesized output Igen by
first transforming both into the perceptually uniform CIELAB color space. We then identify
the set of unique foreground colors C present in Igt, where |C| ≤10 corresponds to the number
of layout rectangles. For each color c ∈C, we derive binary masks for both the ground-truth
and generated images. Specifically, binary masks Mc(I;τ) are derived for both images by
thresholding the perceptual color distance between each pixel p and the target color c:
Mc(I;τ) = {p ∈Ω| ∥I(p)−c∥2 ≤τ},

3.3
Implementation Details
Fine-Tuning. While our primary experiments utilize FLUX.2-4B-base, we also scale our ap-
proach to larger models including FLUX.2-9B-base [23], FLUX.1-dev (12B) [22, 24], and
Qwen-Image (20B) [50]. To enable fine-tuning on a single 80GB A100 GPU, we utilize the
Adafactor optimizer [44] (with constant scale, disabling relative step and parameter scaling)
alongside a fused backward pass and gradient checkpointing. This configuration, combined
with BFloat16 precision, reduces the peak memory footprint of the 20B Qwen-Image model
from 160GB to 44GB at 256 × 256 resolution. We fine-tune for 100,000 steps using a unit
batch size, as layer-wise fused updates are mathematically incompatible with gradient accu-
mulation. This approach leverages recent findings that small-batch training maintains com-
petitive convergence and generalization for large generative models [35]. To ensure training
stability and preserve generation fidelity, we employ an EMA model (0.9995 decay, γ = 10
Karras schedule [19]) and a cosine learning rate schedule (1×10−5 to 5×10−6) following a
5,000-step warmup. Finally, 10% of prompts are nullified for classifier-free guidance (CFG).
Inference. We generate images using 25 steps with a CFG scale of 3.0. We utilize a shifted
logit-normal timestep scheduler (µ = 1.0), which provided the best empirical trade-off be-
tween speed and fidelity.

While increasing µ to 3.0 yields visually stable layouts, we observe a quantitative perfor-

synthetic environment, the model achieves near-optimal layout adherence through distribu-

4.2
Resolution & Aspect Ratio
To evaluate if this architectural feasibility generalizes beyond fixed square layouts, we extend
our approach along two axes: resolution and aspect ratio, which we investigate progressively
(2562 →5122 →10242 and rmax : 1 →2 →3). For resolution experiments, we re-render
our image data at 5122 and 10242 while keeping all geometric and textual properties fixed,
isolating the effect of resolution from layout complexity. For aspect ratio experiments, we
derive resolution buckets (w,h) from a fixed area A = 2562 such that w · h ≈A, where w
and h are multiples of 16. We include all pairs satisfying max(w,h)
min(w,h) ≤rmax, ensuring portrait-
landscape symmetry. Following prior work [10, 18, 24] suggesting that higher resolutions
require increased timestep shifting for optimal image quality, we use the optimal µ from the
previous configuration as a lower bound for subsequent searches. For instance, having found
µ = 1 optimal for 2562, we limit our search to µ ∈[1,3] for the 5122 scale. By applying this
strategy, we can substantially reduce the search space compared to a full µ-sweep for each
setting. Crucially, as bounding box coordinates remain normalized to [0,1000], the textual
prompts are identical across all configurations. This ensures that layout, object count, and
color assignments remain constant, allowing for a controlled comparison of spatial ground-
ing performance across varying scales and geometries.
Resolution. Table 1 summarizes the impact of the logit-normal shift µ on layout adherence
as resolution increases. We observe a clear trend: the optimal µ value is positively correlated
with the image resolution. For the 2562 base resolution, a moderate shift of µ = 1.0 yields
the highest performance (80.8 mIoU2:25), nearly saturating the VAE reconstruction bound of
83.6. However, this configuration fails to generalize to higher resolutions. At 5122, µ = 1.0
results in a significant drop in layout adherence (48.1 mIoU2:25). Instead, higher resolutions
require a more aggressive shift toward earlier timesteps (higher noise levels) to maintain
spatial control. Specifically, peak performance shifts to µ = 2.0 for 5122 and µ = 2.5 for
10242. Notably, with the appropriately tuned µ, our model maintains high layout adherence
across all scales, reaching 82.7 mIoU2:25 at 10242, approaching the VAE’s empirical ceiling.
This suggests that as the resolution increases, a stronger prior on the early denoising phase
is essential for the acquisition of spatial grounding from text prompts alone.

Aspect Ratio. We further evaluate the impact of µ across varying aspect ratios (rmax), as
shown in Tab. 2. Consistent with the resolution experiments, we find that the base configura-
tion (µ = 1.0) is insufficient for handling non-square geometries, with performance dropping
from 80.8 to 67.8 mIoU2:25 when moving from rmax = 1 to rmax = 2. By increasing µ to the
2.0–2.5 range, the model recovers strong layout adherence even for extreme aspect ratios
(rmax = 3). While there is a slight decrease in absolute mIoU2:25 as the aspect ratio becomes
more extreme (likely due to the increased difficulty of placing objects in narrow or highly
elongated canvases), the shift in µ effectively mitigates the drop in spatial control seen with
centered timestep distributions. These results demonstrate that the relationship between the
timestep distribution p(t) and spatial grounding is not static, but rather highly sensitive to the
geometric complexity of the output space. Collectively, these findings indicate that spatial
grounding is heavily dependent on the noise levels encountered during training, necessitating
a higher noise prior as image resolution and aspect ratios increase.
We further find that layout adherence is strongly dependent on object size (tiny vs. large)
but largely density-independent, remaining stable for n ∈[3,10] objects per image. For tiny
objects (< 162 px), median mIoU2:25 is approximately 0.41, versus 0.85-0.95 for large (64−
1282 px) and huge (> 1282 px) objects. However, a similar degradation appears in the VAE
reconstruction ceiling itself, suggesting this size-dependence is a property of the underlying
latent space rather than a model-specific limitation. Full results are in Appendix B.
4.3
Architectural Generalization
To assess if the relationship between spatial grounding and SNR distribution shift (parame-
terized by µ) generalizes across different architectural configurations and text-conditioning
strategies, we extend our evaluation to three larger-scale models: FLUX.2-9B-base [23] and
Qwen-Image (20B) [50], which both utilize a pre-aligned Vision-Language Model (VLM)
encoder, and FLUX.1-dev (12B) [22, 24], which employs a dual-encoder setup consisting of
CLIP-L [40] and T5-XXL [41]. Since these models utilize different VAE architectures with
varying reconstruction fidelities, we measure performance relative to their respective VAE
upper bounds. We use µ = 1.0, the optimal shift identified in Section 4.2 for square images
at 2562 resolution, as a common starting point across architectures and compare it against a
centered SNR distribution (µ = 0.0) as a baseline.
Generalization Across Architectures. The results in Tab. 3 demonstrate that shifting the
timestep sampling distribution is a generalizable mechanism to unlock spatial grounding in
flow-matching models using text prompts only. This effect remains consistent regardless
of the specific transformer backbone or text-conditioning strategy. While our primary ex-
periments use the FLUX.2-4B-base architecture, the ability to recover layout adherence in
9B, 12B, and 20B models by tuning the SNR shift suggests a fundamental property of how
flow-matching models interpret spatial instructions across scales.
Impact of Text Conditioning. We observe a clear correlation between the choice of text
encoder and the model’s sensitivity to the shifting parameter µ. At µ = 1.0, models using
Vision-Language Model (VLM) encoders, such as Qwen-Image and FLUX.2, demonstrate
high layout adherence (≥80.8 mIoU2:25). In contrast, FLUX.1-dev (using T5 and CLIP) re-
quires a significantly stronger shift (µ = 3.0) to achieve comparable grounding performance.
This suggests that VLM-based conditioning provides a more integrated spatial-semantic rep-
resentation that is inherently more "accessible" to the denoising process than the separately
trained embeddings provided by T5 and CLIP encoders.

6
Conclusion
In this work, we investigated the latent spatial grounding capabilities of flow-matching
models, addressing the discrepancy between the rich spatial embeddings of modern vision-
language encoders and the poor native layout adherence of the generative models that lever-
age them. Unlike the prevailing paradigm of introducing auxiliary structural adapters or com-
plex inference-time optimizations, we show that precise spatial control can be "unlocked" by
shifting the timestep sampling distribution. Our systematic evaluation using a novel synthetic
benchmark of geometric primitives allowed us to isolate the mechanics of spatial grounding
from textural complexities. We identified the high-noise regime as a "critical learning phase",
where the model is forced to internalize geometric constraints to establish global structure.
By biasing the fine-tuning process toward these regimes, we enabled models to interpret 2D
coordinates directly from text prompts with strong spatial accuracy. Furthermore, we demon-
strated that these insights generalize to real-world imagery via the COCO dataset, achieving
state-of-the-art layout adherence without any architectural modifications. The implications
of our findings are twofold: first, they suggest that the perceived lack of spatial control in
current models is not a limitation of architectural capacity. Second, our approach offers a
highly efficient, architecture-agnostic path for enhancing spatial controllability, providing a
recipe for integrating native layout adherence into future foundation models.
Limitations and future work. While our approach provides a lightweight and architecture-
agnostic path to spatial grounding, it reveals a trade-off: a strong bias toward high-noise
regimes during fine-tuning, while essential for layout adherence, can compromise high-
frequency textural synthesis. This suggests that the global structural alignment facilitated by
high-noise training may compete with local generative refinement required for high-fidelity
synthesis. Future research should investigate multi-stage or scheduled sampling distribu-
tions that transition from a high-noise bias (to lock in grounding) to a more uniform distri-
bution (to recover textural quality). Furthermore, exploring Direct Preference Optimization
(DPO) [33] or other reinforcement learning techniques [32] presents a promising avenue for
aligning model outputs with both layout constraints and human-perceived image quality.

B
Impact of Object Scale & Density on Layout Adherence
To assess how layout adherence varies with scene complexity and object scale, we evaluate
the impact of object count and object size on the resulting spatial accuracy. For the object
count analysis, we evaluate each value of n ∈{1,...,10} individually, corresponding to the
full range of rectangles per image in our benchmark. For the size analysis, we categorize
individual rectangles into five categories: Tiny (< 162 px), Small (162–322 px), Medium
(322–642 px), Large (642–1282 px), and Huge (> 1282 px).
Object Scale. The relationship between object scale and layout adherence is illustrated in
Fig. 11 (left). We observe a positive correlation between object size and spatial accuracy. As
rectangle area increases, the model’s mIoU2:25 consistently improves. For Large and Huge
objects, the model achieves near-optimal spatial fidelity, with median mIoU2:25 ranging from
0.85 to 0.95. While performance remains robust for Medium objects, we note an increase in
variance, with the lower bound of the distribution dropping to 0.60. The model’s primary
failure modes occur at smaller scales, where Tiny objects drop to a median mIoU2:25 of
approximately 0.41. Crucially, as shown in Fig. 11, our model’s median performance closely
tracks the VAE reconstruction baseline across all size categories, indicating that the model
effectively approaches the average spatial capacity of the underlying latent representation.
However, we note a slight divergence in the upper quartiles (75th percentile) for Tiny and
Small objects, where the VAE baseline maintains a higher performance ceiling. This suggests
that while our model accurately predicts the expected location of Small objects, it does not
yet fully capture the extreme precision occasionally preserved by the VAE reconstruction.
Object Density. We further analyze the impact of object density on layout adherence in
Fig. 11 (right). Counter-intuitively, the model exhibits its highest variance when few rect-
angles are present (n = 1,2), where the mIoU2:25 distribution is broadest. Our investigation
reveals that this is primarily driven by the object size sensitivity discussed previously. Perfor-
mance in single-object images is highly dependent on whether that specific object is Tiny or
Huge. As the number of objects increases, the statistics stabilize, with the median mIoU2:25
remaining remarkably constant up to n = 10. This suggests that the model is robust to scene
complexity and effectively maintains layout adherence in crowded environments, consis-
tently approaching the VAE reconstruction median regardless of object count.

C
Extended Fine-Tuning on Real-World Images
To assess whether layout adherence continues to improve beyond the 5-epoch budget used in
our µ-ablation (Tab. 4), we fine-tune FLUX.2-9B-base at µ = 3.0 for 10 epochs, evaluating
after each. Full epoch-by-epoch results are shown in Tab. 6. The 10-epoch endpoint is also
reported in Tab. 5. Note that the 5-epoch checkpoint comes from this separate extended run
and therefore slightly differs from the µ-ablation’s 5-epoch result (Tab. 4, Tab. 5) due to
training variance. Average Precision (AP) and Average Recall (AR) improve continuously
across all 10 epochs, with AP50 peaking at epoch 9, suggesting diminishing returns beyond
this point. AP and AP50 exceed ground-truth COCO values after 10 epochs (see Sec. 5
for possible explanations), while AR approaches ground-truth. Image quality metrics vary
more: CLIP peaks at epoch 7, remaining fairly stable between epochs 3–8 before declining
slightly. The Inception Score fluctuates more but is generally highest from epoch 5 onward.
FID, while also fluctuating, shows a general improving trend with extended training.
D
Compositional Failures in Pre-Trained Model
As noted in Sec. 5, compositional artifacts (e.g., merged or duplicated anatomical features)
occasionally occur in generated images of our fine-tuned FLUX.2-9B-base model (Fig. 7).
However, as can be seen in Fig. 12 and Fig. 13, such failure modes also occur in images
generated using the pre-trained FLUX.2-9B-base model prior to any fine-tuning. The im-
ages were generated using the standard FLUX.2 sampling configuration (same as in Sec. 5),
demonstrating that compositional failures such as merged elephant heads (Fig. 7, row 4) are
already present in the base model rather than being introduced by our fine-tuning procedure.
