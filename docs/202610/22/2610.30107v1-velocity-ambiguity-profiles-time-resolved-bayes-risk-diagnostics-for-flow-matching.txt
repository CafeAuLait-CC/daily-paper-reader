Abstract 

Flow matching trains a continuum of velocity regression problems but usually reports a loss averaged over interpolation time, obscuring which regions are limited by intrinsic ambiguity and which remain data-limited. We introduce the Velocity Ambiguity Profile (VAP), Av (t), the time-local Bayes-risk floor of velocity pre-diction, and decompose time-resolved loss as LN (t) = Av (t) + R(t; N ), where only the excess term R(t; N ) is reducible by more data or a different estimator. For N training samples and a K-component mixture in the structured example, we prove two statistical benchmarks. In an intrinsic local-regression benchmark, 

Av (t) enters the time-dependent noise factor of the standard nonparametric rate. In separated isotropic Gaussian mixtures, the Bayes velocity becomes locally affine, and a label-oracle residual-regression benchmark has parametric excess-risk scale KAv (t)/N , matched within the same separated residual Gaussian-location subproblem. Controlled Gaussian-mixture experiments validate this diagnostic through a dense transition sweep, calibrated oracle-to-blind and empirical-proxy audits, and a local controlled time-sampler stress test in which a frozen reducible-error proxy reduces reducible loss while cross-cell sensitivity delimits aggressive sampling. On CIFAR-100 DINOv2 feature flow, guided time sampling reduces uniformly evaluated velocity loss by 6.78% and global feature Fréchet distance (FD) by 23 .6% , while class-conditional kernel inception distance (KID) shows no improvement. VAP turns an averaged flow-matching objective into a time-resolved diagnostic for locating ambiguity-limited and data-limited regions. 

1 Introduction 

Flow matching reduces distribution learning to a continuum of velocity regression problems indexed by interpolation time, but training and evaluation usually report a path-averaged loss. This hides where additional data can help and where the target remains intrinsically ambiguous. This paper asks a simple diagnostic question: which interpolation times remain limited by reducible estimation error, and which are already limited by intrinsic velocity ambiguity? A high loss at time t

is actionable only when it exceeds the Bayes-risk floor. It may reflect an under-trained estimator, or it may reflect multiple data realizations remaining plausible under the same noisy interpolant. Finite-data error can be reduced; posterior ambiguity cannot. The conditional-mean orthogonality behind this decomposition is classical. Our contribution is to make the induced posterior ambiguity profile path-specific, expose its mode and information-set structure, and use it for time-local diagnostics.

3.4 Practical Estimation of Av (t)

Large-budget reference profile. In applied flow matching settings, Av (t) is not directly observable. We therefore use the largest-budget validation loss only as an upper-biased reference profile, Lref (t) ≡

LNmax (t). At population risk, Proposition 3 gives Lref (t) = Av (t) + R(t; Nmax ) ≥ A v (t), so a finite-budget reference is not an absolute Bayes-floor estimator. If a current model and the reference are evaluated on the same distribution, their difference removes the unknown floor exactly: 

LN (t) − Lref (t) = R(t; N ) − R(t; Nmax ). (8) This reference-relative gap is a calibrated allocation diagnostic; it does not identify the absolute Bayes floor. Consistency of the reference toward Av (t) additionally requires vanishing training excess and a growing validation sample under the usual moment conditions. 4Collapse diagnostics. If R(t; N ) = Av (t)g(N ), then LN (t)/Av (t) − 1 = g(N ). Thus the clean collapse diagnostic is normalized excess risk, R(t; N )/Av (t), or LN (t)/Av (t) − 1 when Av (t)

is known. Raw losses LN (t) alone saturate to the Bayes floor and should only be interpreted as approximate power laws when the excess term dominates the floor. 

4 Main Theoretical Results 

We now ask when Av (t) predicts the sample-complexity scale, not only the Bayes-risk floor. Theo-rem 4 gives a compact local-regression benchmark in which Av (t) enters a nonparametric rate up to bounded factors in the fixed-positive-noise regime; Theorem 5 gives a separated-GMM label-oracle residual-regression benchmark with parametric scale KAv (t)/N , matched inside the corresponding separated residual Gaussian-location subproblem. This benchmark calibrates the residual-regression view of the flow objective; it is not a lower bound over all action-aware estimators of the full supervised tuple. 

Oracle terminology. We reserve label-oracle residual benchmark for the theorem-level information set: the estimator receives true component labels and fits the residual affine response in each separated cell. A label-and-action oracle is stronger: it also uses the supervised pair to recover a = xt − tu t

and estimate component means directly. An analytic oracle uses the known mixture law only to compute diagnostic quantities such as Av (t) and posterior responsibilities. The oracle-to-blind bridge below compares these controlled oracle arms with estimators that do not receive labels or analytic responsibilities.

R(t; N )/Av (t). Suppose that on a time regime I the excess risk factorizes as

where the exponent of gI (N ) is independent of t on I, and ηI (t, N ) is either bounded above and below by positive constants or differs from 1 by at most a controlled small correction. Then ¯R(t; N ) = 

gI (N ) ηI (t, N ) for t ∈ I. 6 Related Work 

Flow matching, imitation learning, and schedules. Flow matching and rectified-flow objectives transport noise to data [1, 11, 20, 21, 28, 36]. Imitation scaling and behavioral-cloning theory study data composition and policy error [3, 6, 12, 14, 30, 31], while schedule methods change the training-time distribution [5, 9, 13, 15]. VAP instead diagnoses the induced regression problems along interpolation time. 

Statistical theory and adaptive structure. Diffusion and score theory characterize minimax rates under manifold and smoothness assumptions [4, 7, 16, 18, 24], and adaptive regression theory studies intrinsic or compositional structure [22, 29, 32, 35]. Our intrinsic result is a time-resolved velocity analog; the separated Gaussian-mixture result is the scoped label-oracle residual benchmark. 

7 Conclusion 

VAP turns the aggregate flow-matching loss into a time-resolved diagnostic by separating the Bayes-risk floor from reducible excess error. The theory gives two scoped benchmarks: a local intrinsic regression rate with bounded-factor time dependence and a sharper separated-GMM label-oracle residual scale KAv (t)/N . Controlled experiments validate the analytic floor, separated-regime col-lapse prediction, oracle-to-blind calibration, proxy-floor guards, and local time-sampler intervention, whose sensitivity boundary is given in Appendix D.8. On CIFAR-100 DINOv2 feature flow, guided time sampling reduces uniformly evaluated velocity loss by 6.78% and global feature FD by 23 .6% .Macro conditional KID shows no gain, while lower recall and effective covariance rank reveal a diversity cost. Turning these diagnostics into a budget rule or intervention policy remains open. 

Limitations. The intrinsic result is for the local benchmark Gcomp  

> t

, not the full ambient observation, with Av (t) entering as a bounded-factor prefactor. The GMM result is a separated label-oracle residual benchmark, not a full-information lower bound because (xt, u t, t ) determines a = xt − tu t.The natural-data result is limited to the frozen DINOv2 feature space: it makes no pixel-quality, universal-sampler, or uniform-metric-improvement claims.

Remark 11 (Role of Av (t)). Appendix D.8 adds a three-cell d-axis sensitivity check: mixture arms and lower pure exponents preserve the improvement direction, while the most aggressive pure sampler exposes a boundary at the largest cell. Broader generality beyond this partial-merge d-axis family remains open; whether structured non-time signals such as stage-aware reweighting yield further gains is also untested.

B.6 Effective parameters and adaptive-rate conjecture 

Real-world action distributions are far from worst-case: they exhibit multi-modality, low intrinsic dimension, and piecewise smooth structure. This motivates a conjectural adaptive-rate picture 24 in which sufficiently expressive estimators may exploit this structure to converge faster than the worst-case minimax rate of Theorem 4.

B.8 Controlled adaptive-rate evidence: trained network and copying estimator 

We report a controlled flow-matching experiment that separates the trained-network excess-risk rate from the copying (empirical-velocity) rate on a common setting. The target is a separated isotropic Gaussian mixture ( K = 12 , σ = 0 .5, centers on a radius-3 sphere), with the path xt = t ε + (1 − t) a

from the slope of the log paired excess risk against log N over a demonstration-count ladder, using common random numbers so that the dominant Av (t) variance cancels. Two estimators are compared. 

Copying estimator (population-floor reference). The empirical-velocity rule copies the velocity of the nearest training endpoint. Measured against the analytic Bayes-velocity population floor (which 25 removes the finite-sample bias of an empirical floor), its data-end exponent tracks the one-nearest-neighbor localization exponent 2/D across dimension.

The noise-end exponent is resolved in point estimate but with wide intervals, and the budget invariance holds in sign with small magnitude under the equal-epoch budget.

C Proof of Proposition 6 (Curve Collapse) 

We prove regime-wise normalized collapse by combining the factorization structure of the excess risk with the normalization ¯R(t; N ) = R(t; N )/Av (t). Within any regime where the N -dependence has a t-independent exponent, normalization removes the leading time dependence, leaving only bounded prefactor variation in the minimax setting or exponentially small corrections in the separated Gaussian-mixture regime. Claims about cross-regime transition width or high-dimensional sharpening are therefore interpreted as heuristic consequences rather than theorem-level statements.

C.6 Convergence wavefront-auxiliary diagnostic 

We record an auxiliary diagnostic definition that follows from the collapse picture. It does not correspond to any main-text experiment in this paper and should not be read as a budget-selection rule. 

Corollary 52 (Convergence wavefront) . Define the δ-convergence wavefront: 

t∗(N, δ ) := sup {t ∈ [0 , 1] : R(t; N ) ≤ δ} . (78) 

Under the adaptive rate framework, t∗(N, δ ) is increasing in N : as more data are added, the wavefront advances from t ≈ 0 (data endpoint) toward t ≈ 1 (noise endpoint), “sweeping” the interpolation path. The speed of advance is governed by the growth rate of Av (t).

The convergence wavefront provides a natural visualization of data sufficiency: a heatmap of R(t; N )

over the (t, N ) plane, with contour lines tracing t∗(N, δ ) for various δ, resembles a phase diagram. We keep this as an auxiliary diagnostic definition rather than a main experiment in this paper. 

C.7 Summary 

Combining all cases, Proposition 6 is established at the level of a regime-wise normalization principle: 1. Under Theorem 4: bounded-factor collapse (same exponent α = 2 s/ (2 s + d), bounded multiplicative factor ≤ (σu/σ ℓ)d/ (2 s+d)). 2. Under Conjecture 20, single regime: exact collapse within regime with exponent α =2seff /(2 seff + deff ), approximately constant prefactor. 3. Across regimes: heuristic collapse with CV control. 4. Threshold-dependent sharpening: for illustrative τK = C√log K, CV = O((log K)−1/4);stronger high-dimensional rates require additional assumptions. 5. GMM residual benchmark (Theorem 5): exact collapse with α = 1 up to exponentially small corrections. The controlled experimental prediction is: on GMM data with tmax < t merge (τ ) for the chosen threshold, we expect low CV and α ≈ 1. Outside controlled Gaussian-mixture settings, the current paper does not predict or claim normalized collapse.

E.1 Label-and-action Cramér-Rao sanity Bridge estimator definitions. The true-label plug-in oracle _ols estimates each component mean by ordinary least squares (OLS), which here reduces to its labeled sample mean, and uses the known within-component variance and the true component label at prediction time. The blind EM-OLS arm fits a spherical Gaussian mixture with the known component count K to unlabeled training actions using EM. It plugs the fitted means and mixture weights, together with the arithmetic mean of the fitted component variances, into the marginal Gaussian-mixture velocity formula and averages over posterior component probabilities given (xt, t ). Thus, neither fitting nor velocity prediction uses true labels. F.3 Representative task descriptions Turning on a radio. The robot first uses its onboard cameras to identify the radio on the tabletop and approach it. The right gripper is then used to take hold of the device, after which the left gripper depresses the power button; once the radio has been switched on, it is returned to the table surface. What makes this scenario non-trivial is the joint requirement of locomotion plus dual-arm operation: the right hand has to keep a stable grip so that the reaction force produced by the button press does not dislodge the radio. At the start of every rollout the radio is placed at a different pose on the table, although it is always within the robot’s field of view and reach. Inference is allowed at most 6,000

Picking up trash. In this scenario the right gripper holds a refuse container throughout the entire rollout while the robot navigates in turn to three soda cans distributed across the scene. At each can, the left gripper is used to lift the can and drop it into the container kept by the right hand; after all three cans have been collected, the filled container is set back onto the floor. The difficulty arises from a long-horizon mobile manipulation pattern that imposes asymmetric bimanual roles: the right arm acts as a passive but stable carrier, while the left arm is in charge of repeated grasp-and-release motions. Per rollout, both the container and the cans are spawned at randomized positions so that 55 nontrivial navigation is required, and the robot’s initial base pose is also drawn from a bounded distribution to probe generalization. Spraying fruit trees. Here the robot uses vision to find a sprayer, picks it up with the left gripper, and then visits two fruit trees one after the other. Upon arrival at each tree, the right gripper toggles the sprayer’s switch to the on position, performs the watering action, and toggles the switch back off before the platform moves on to the next tree. The chief difficulty is bimanual tool use combined with mobile manipulation: the left arm must hold the fluid container steady so that the spray remains aimed at the target, while the right arm produces the contact force on the switch without disturbing this alignment. The sprayer and both trees are spawned at random workspace positions, forcing the robot to actively search the scene and replan its path; the starting base pose of the robot is also varied across rollouts. Cooking hot dogs. This task strings together articulated-object handling with intermediate buffering of cargo. The robot first opens the refrigerator, takes out two hot dogs, and shuts the door; one of the items is set aside on the tabletop while the other is loaded into the microwave; the buffered hot dog is then transferred from the table into the microwave, the microwave door is closed, and the start switch is engaged. The fundamental challenge is long-horizon planning across multiple workstations (the storage compartment, the staging area, and the cooking appliance), with the kinematics of the appliance doors having to be coordinated with pick-and-place operations. Initial object poses and the robot’s starting base pose are varied between rollouts to evaluate generalization, and the inference horizon is set to 15 ,000 steps. 

Washing a baseball cap. The robot moves to a washing machine, opens its door, and finds the two baseball caps that need to be cleaned. With one cap held in each gripper, it walks back to the washer and inserts both caps into the narrow drum cavity one after the other; the door is then shut and the power switch is pressed. The hard part of this task is a long-horizon mobile manipulation pipeline that combines articulated-object operation with carrying objects in both hands at the same time: the robot has to navigate while its grippers are occupied, and then perform collision-free placements inside a tightly bounded workspace. Both the poses of the two caps and the robot’s initial base pose are randomized across trials, and inference is limited to 10 ,000 steps. 56 ....
