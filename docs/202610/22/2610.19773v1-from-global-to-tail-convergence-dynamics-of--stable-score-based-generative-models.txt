ABSTRACT 

Score-based generative models driven by α-stable Lévy processes have been proposed as an alternative to Brownian diffusion models, with empirical evidence suggesting improved sampling and better modeling of heavy-tailed data. Yet theoretical guarantees for these models remain limited. We develop a non-asymptotic convergence analysis for scale-preserving α-stable generative dynamics, covering both compactly supported and power-law targets. In p-Wasserstein distance, we quantify the effects of early stopping, initialization, score approximation, and time discretization, and show exponential decay of the initialization error. A comparison with Brownian dynamics reveals that global Wasserstein convergence alone does not fundamentally distinguish the two models: both initialization errors decay exponentially with the forward horizon on the common class of heavy-tailed targets. A sharp separation emerges instead in the tails. At any positive generation time, stable dynamics produces power-law tails with index α, whereas Brownian dynamics remains light-tailed. For a target with tail index ν, asymptotically correct extreme-quantile scaling is possible precisely when α = ν. These results identify tail-index matching as a principled mechanism behind the ability of stable generative models to capture extreme events. 

Keywords Score-based generative models · α-stable Lévy processes · Heavy-tailed distributions · Non-asymptotic convergence · Wasserstein bound · Tail-index matching 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Score-based generative models learn to reverse a noising process that maps the data distribution to a tractable reference law (Song et al., 2021). Standard constructions use Brownian motion, based on Gaussian noising transitions. However, its continuous, light-tailed paths evolve through local increments, making distant or rare modes harder to explore and recover (Yoon et al., 2023; Paquet et al., 2024). Similarly, Brownian score-based models may also underrepresent power-law tails (Pandey et al., 2025) and be inadequately initialized for heavy-tailed targets (Fassina et al., 2026). These limitations motivate considering non-Gaussian noising processes (Nachmani et al., 2021; Deasy et al., 2021). arXiv:2610.19773v1 [cs.LG] 14 Oct 2026 Isotropic α-stable noise represents a heavy-tailed alternative, where the parameter α controls the tails and jump scaling. In particular, α = 2 recovers the Gaussian case, while α ∈ (1 , 2) yields heavy-tailed noise. Yoon et al. (2023) use an α-stable noise in the continuous scale-preserving Lévy–Itô model (LIM), with explicit forward transitions and fractional score. LIM and the discrete model of Shariatian et al. (2025) have been reported to accelerate and diversify sampling, improve tail and rare-mode coverage, and better explore high-dimensional spaces (Yoon et al., 2023; Paquet et al., 2024; Shariatian et al., 2025; Popov et al., 2025). Why stable noise helps remains only partially understood theoretically: for the discrete model in the finite-sample regime, Cherkaoui et al. (2026) show that its initialization gain can be offset by a harder estimation problem in the reverse dynamics. In continuous time, the main difficulty lies in constructing a tractable backward dynamics. For jump processes, the exact time reversal is generally no longer an additive Lévy SDE (Conforti and Léonard, 2022; Qi et al., 2026), making it impractical for generative modeling. Popov et al. (2025) circumvent this difficulty by introducing a family of backward SDEs with additive Lévy noise that, while not pathwise reversals, exactly reproduce the forward marginal flow in reverse. Non-asymptotic convergence guarantees are well developed for Brownian diffusion models (Conforti et al., 2025; Strasman et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025), while general Lévy-type denoising Markov models have recently been studied through a unified generator framework (Ren et al., 2026). In contrast, quantitative guarantees for continuous α-stable score-based models remain largely unexplored, particularly for heavy-tailed data. 

Contributions. We develop a convergence theory for continuous α-stable generative models and compare their global and tail behavior with Brownian dynamics. Our main contributions are: • Building on a fractional Tweedie identity, we relate reverse-drift regularity to the Lipschitz modulus of the optimal denoiser and establish the required control for both compactly supported and power-law targets. • For 1 ≤ p < α , we establish a non-asymptotic Wp bound for the practical α-stable sampler, separating early stopping, initialization, score approximation, and discretization errors. For power-law targets, we further show that the Brownian initialization error also decays exponentially despite the loss of the contractive coupling available in the stable case. Thus, global Wasserstein convergence, established so far, alone does not fundamentally separate the two dynamics. • A sharp distinction emerges in the tails. For exact-score dynamics initialized at the reference laws, stable outputs have power-law tails with index α, whereas Brownian outputs are light-tailed at every positive generation time. For a target with tail index ν, the stable extreme-quantile ratio vanishes for α > ν , diverges for α < ν , and converges to a finite nonzero limit precisely when α = ν. Hence, tail-index matching is the unique regime allowing asymptotically correct extreme-quantile scaling. Complete proofs and the protocols for numerical experiments are deferred to the appendices.

5.2 Tail behavior 

Global convergence in Wp does not by itself guarantee that the generated distribution reproduces the extreme behavior of the target. In particular, it does not determine whether the generated law has the correct tail index or assigns the correct mass to extreme events. We therefore complement the Wasserstein analysis with two tail-sensitive quantities motivated by classical Extreme Value Theory (EVT, Resnick, 2007; de Haan and Ferreira, 2006; Ghosh and Resnick, 2010). The first, based on the mean-excess function, identifies the asymptotic tail regime; the second compares generated and target quantiles at increasingly extreme probability levels.

Overall, the exact-score dynamics initialized at the reference laws differ in their tails: Brownian outputs remain light-tailed at finite generation time, while stable outputs inherit the noise tail index. This distinction is not determined by global Wp convergence. Tail-index matching therefore provides a possible mechanism behind the improved tail coverage observed empirically with Lévy-based generative models (Yoon et al., 2023; Paquet et al., 2024; Pandey et al., 2025; Shariatian et al., 2025). 

# CONCLUSION 

Our results distinguish global accuracy from tail reconstruction in α-stable generative models. Our tail analysis includes the index-matched regime ν = α, whereas our global convergence guarantees require stronger tail assumptions. Tail-index matching explains when stable dynamics can reproduce the correct asymptotic scale of extreme events, yet the role of tail constants, anisotropy, and multivariate dependence remains largely unexplored. It is also unclear whether Lévy noise provides advantages 12 beyond tail reconstruction, for instance through faster exploration of multimodal landscapes or improved finite-step sampling. Finally, extending the tail analysis to learned scores is essential: estimation errors in low-density regions may interact strongly with the jump structure precisely where the stable dynamics is meant to improve tail coverage.

Interpretation. At index match, ν = α = 1 .5, the three diagnostics are mutually consistent: the Hill estimate remains close to 1 .5, the extreme-quantile ratio is approximately constant, and the normalized tail constant remains close to one. The quantile plateau is nevertheless below one over the displayed range, so generated extreme quantiles remain smaller than their target counterparts. This illustrates the distinction between matching the tail index and reproducing the target’s extreme scales: agreement with the EI tail constant in panel (c) does not remove the residual target discrepancy measured in panel (b). Away from index match, panel (b) exhibits the predicted directions: the ratio decreases for ν = 1 .2 <α and increases for ν = 3 > α as the quantiles become more extreme. The other diagnostics are less conclusive. For ν = 1 .2, the Hill estimates and normalized tail constants remain below their references; for ν = 3, both drift upward as larger upper fractions are included. Thus the displayed thresholds do not yield joint stabilization of the tail-index and tail-constant estimates. These observations support the qualitative quantile comparison but do not resolve all asymptotic predictions at the available sample size. For the Brownian baseline at ν = 1 .5, the quantile ratio decreases markedly as s ↓ 0, whereas the stable ratio remains approximately flat. This illustrates increasingly undersized Brownian extreme quantiles, consistently with Proposition 14. 

Numerical and statistical controls. Temporal refinements N ∈ { 40 , 80 , 160 }, independent high-resolution score tables, exact-πT initialization, and direct S αS, Pareto, and Gaussian samples provide numerical and estimator controls. The displayed inter-seed bands describe variability across replications and are not confidence intervals; seed-level bootstrap diagnostics use 10000 resamples. At index match, the displayed diagnostics are qualitatively consistent with the predicted tail index, a constant quantile ratio, and the EI tail constant. Away from index match, threshold drift prevents an equally conclusive 43 joint interpretation. Pickands estimates, generalized Pareto fits, and mean-excess ratios are retained as supplementary checks. 

F.5 Implementation and numerical controls 

Stable variables use the convention E[eiuZ ] = e−| u|α

and are generated with the Chambers–Mallows– Stuck construction (Chambers et al., 1976). Random seeds are independent across reported replications; common random numbers are used only for paired resolution controls. Propagation costs O(nN ), and each one-dimensional Wasserstein evaluation costs O(n log n) because of sorting. The reported propagation runs were produced with JAX (Bradbury et al., 2018) in CPU float64 ;the deterministic denoiser calculation uses NumPy (Harris et al., 2020) and SciPy (Virtanen et al., 2020) FFTs in float64 . The environment was Windows 11 with Python 3.12.10, JAX/JAXLIB 0.7.2, NumPy 2.3.3, SciPy 1.16.2, and 16 logical CPU cores. No GPU result is reported.
