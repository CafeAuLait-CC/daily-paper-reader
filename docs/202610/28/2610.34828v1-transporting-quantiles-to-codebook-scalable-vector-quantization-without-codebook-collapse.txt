Transporting Quantiles to Codebook: Scalable Vector
Quantization without Codebook Collapse
Yuwei Zeng1
Zekun Shi1,2
1 National University of Singapore 2 SEA AI Lab
Abstract
Effective discrete representation learning with VQ-VAE serves as a fundamental
component of modern autoregressive generative models. However, it is widely
known to suffer from codebook collapse and inefficient code utilization. To address
this issue, we propose FlowVQ, a simple yet effective alternative to standard
vector quantization by formulating neural quantization as a joint modeling and
clustering process. Specifically, we learn the latent space using a lightweight
flow matching model alongside training, corresponding to a probability flow ODE
that transports an often fixed well-behaved source distribution while preserving
probability mass. Based on this, we define the codebook C1 by transporting even-
quantile partitioned prior codes C0 from the source space through the learned flow,
and obtaining the Voronoi centroids under the induced distribution, leading to a
structured discretization. We evaluated on image tokenization and generation, as
well as on two tasks requiring high-precision tokenization, demonstrating scalability
and improved expressivity of the codebook with full utilization. Code is available
at: GitHub repository.

Introduction
Vector Quantized Variational AutoEncoder (VQ-VAE), proposed by Van Den Oord et al. [1], [2]
demonstrates effective discrete latent representation learning by incorporating Vector Quantization
(VQ) into reconstruction. It learns a finite set of codebook vectors and tokenizes continuous data
into discrete sequences by mapping to the nearest vectors, enabling large autoregressive models
for continuous data generation while leveraging the design, training, and scaling principles behind
large language models. By balancing fidelity, expressiveness, and computational efficiency, VQ-VAE
remains an essential building block of modern generative models that are adopted across diverse
domains, including state-of-the-art models for image generation and video-based world modeling.
With codebook collapse and unscalable model capacity remaining unresolved, existing works have
explored numerous ways to mitigate such issues. Early improvements [3–5] involved various training
techniques such as random restart, random replacement, and k-means initialization. Williams et al.
[6] and Takida et al. [7, 8] proposed stochastic quantization, Zhu et al. [9] and Chang et al. [10]
proposed adding an additional projector layer or bridging network after a frozen codebook. With an
altered latent space, model performance can be compromised, leading to degenerate reconstruction
and representation learning. Recently, Lookup-Free Quantization (LFQ) [11–13] methods proposed
an alternative to VQ based on direct scalar quantization with fixed hypercube geometry, rounding
values in each dimension to the nearest point, and showed scalable codebook capacity with improved
image generation using large vocabulary sizes beyond 214.
To circumvent codebook collapse, we propose that an ideal codebook should jointly model and
cluster the latent space into even partitions alongside reconstruction training. The codebook vectors
correspond to the Voronoi centers of these partitions, naturally allocating denser vectors to high-

A flow matching model [14] learns a continuous transport mapping samples from a prior distribution,
such as a Gaussian, to an arbitrary data distribution. This transport corresponds to a Probability
Flow ODE (PF-ODE) [15, 16], which preserves probability mass and transports prior quantiles to
data quantiles. Based on this, we define the prior codebook uniformly in the well-behaved source
space, and co-train the flow model with VQ-VAE. The codebook vectors are then obtained as the
pushforward of the prior codebook and move toward the Voronoi centers under the estimated density.
Experiments show that a lightweight flow model introduces minor overhead relative to VQ-VAE
while maintaining full code utilization and improving performance.
In summary, our contributions are as follows:
1. We propose FlowVQ, a novel VQ technique that converts codebook learning into a quantile
transport problem, where code utilization emerges as a geometric property of the latent
space, enabling balanced usage and avoiding codebook collapse.
2. We show that FlowVQ consistently achieves full codebook utilization across a range of tasks,
improving reconstruction and generation quality within a unified tokenizer framework.

Related Works
VQ-VAE & Generation in the Latent Space
Proposed by [1], the VQ-VAE combines vector
quantization with a reconstruction objective and achieves effective neural tokenization. It enables
autoregressive generation on continuous data, such as high-fidelity image generation, by predicting
the sequence of tokens in the latent space using a PixelCNN [1, 17] and, more recently transformer
models. VQ-GAN [18] further couples the training with a discriminative loss, addressing the issue of
blurry reconstruction from traditional VAE greatly improving generation fidelity. It has since been
adopted as a standard framework. Recently, advancements on residual [19], multi-head [20], and
multi-scale [21] tokenization were proposed and demonstrate solid improvements [19, 21, 22]. As a
general tokenization technique, VQ-VAE based tokenizers are widely adopted in diverse generative
tasks today, especially in tasks where direct generation can be prohibitively expensive such as
image [12, 21, 23], audio [3, 24], and video [25, 26], or in fields where structural representations are
often unavailable, such as AI for protein and material generation [27–29].
Mitigating Codebook Collapse
To address the codebook collapse, which leads to unscalable
codebook capacity, an active line of work has been proposed to mitigate it. Early work addressed
it with various tricks such as random restart or random replacement [3, 5]. Another line of work
explored differentiable relaxation [4, 30, 31], most notably using the Gumbel–Softmax [32] . However,
these approaches generally underperform and produces lower-quality reconstruction [6]. Takida et
2


al. [7, 8] proposed improving the mode-dropping behavior by switching to stochastic quantization
but suffer from similar unrealistic reconstructions. Zhu et al. [9] and Chang et al. [10] addressed the
under-utilization issue by adding intermediate projection layer(s) between a frozen codebook and the
decoder. In a parallel track, Mentzer et al. [11], Yu et al. [12], and Weber et al. [13] proposed LFQ,
which rounds each dimension to the nearest point in a hypercube, and scales the codebook capacity
with its size using direct scalar quantization. These methods demonstrate competitive or improved
image generation performance, especially with large vocabulary sizes beyond 214 to 218.
The majority of the existing works mitigate this issue without fundamentally solving it. To our
knowledge, the closest work is OptVQ [22], which introduces global information via optimal transport
between batch latents and the codebook. However, its cost matrix requires per-batch normalization
for numerical stability, which distorts the transport cost inconsistently across batches. Moreover, the
resulting doubly stochastic assignment is converted to a hard assignment, which does not guarantee
that every code is selected, especially under stronger entropy regularization. Full utilization thus
depends on the entropy weight, which trades off quantization accuracy against utilization and may be
sub-optimal for a given task as observed in our experiments.

The codebook structure closely follows the geometry of the latent manifold and adapts to its density,

3.3
Self-Correction toward Voronoi Centroids
Up to this point, a set of approximately uniform points in the latent space is obtained through Eq (7)
as the codebook C1, which only fulfills the first two key properties of faithful latent modeling and
approximately even partitioning. To minimize the approximation error arising from the quasi-random

toward the Voronoi centroids as the Euclidean centers of the transported quantiles, aiming to achieve
the final desired property of the codebook. This process can be viewed as transporting each quantile
cell through a set of its uniformly spread samples.

The results are summarized in Table 1. We can spot the codebook collapse of the
original VQ immediately when |C| increases from 1024 to 10240 with a fixed latent dimension. The

number of active codes decreases from 1024 × 45.6% = 467 to 10240 × 2.73% = 280, leading to
degenerate overall performance. The entropy regularization drastically improves the utilization rate
of LFQ, but does not guarantee full utilization when balancing multiple objectives of minimizing
quantization error and maximally filling the hypercube simultaneously. The same conflict applies to
OptVQ, which was observed to learn fast in early steps, but plateaus much earlier than methods that
are solely driven by the quantization objective, such as VQGAN and FlowVQ here. Nevertheless,
we note that VQ-GAN serves as a strong baseline due to its flexibility, which leads to high-quality
alignment between the codebook and an evolving latent space.
We observe that FlowVQ achieves significant improvements in both image quality, with rFID = 1.40,
reconstruction error MAE of 0.036, and codebook quality with full utilization across all settings. The
performance consistently improves with increasing |C|. Explicit optimizing the mutual information
further improves the model slightly, though we note the performance is very close between the two.
Both variants achieve the highest entropy (13.97, near the maximum log216384 = 14), indicating the
most balanced usage across codes, which validates the effectiveness of the proposed method.

Time Analysis
One potential concern for the proposed method is that the pushforward operation
can be time-consuming. We measure the average training time per 1000 steps (one codebook update
cycle) for all models in robotics VLA model Table 6 in Appendix B. FlowVQ takes on average 618s
compared to 535s for VQ-GAN, increased by 15.6% with the difference mostly due to the codebook
update, which takes 82.8s on average. This time is expected to further decrease with more GPU
memory. Meanwhile, as the prior vectors are pre-defined in the fixed source space, the update is
fully deterministic and easily supports distributed execution with minimal communication overhead.
We implement the distributed update. It takes 3s per codebook update on 32 NVIDIA A100 GPUs
for |C| = 16384 for large scale training such as ImageNet, thus comparable speed to the original
VQ-GAN.

4.2
High Precision Tokenization
To examine the potential of an adaptive and fully utilized codebook for FlowVQ as a general tokenizer,
we would like to extend the evaluation beyond the image domain. One natural task to consider is
high-precision tokenization, especially where structural representation is missing and generation
can greatly benefit from the discretized representation learning. Data containing geometric, motion,
and spatial state information usually resides in continuous space, and small numerical differences
can lead to significantly different physical states or interaction outcomes. Tokenization needs to
allocate sufficient resolution to ensure that distinct physical configurations do not collapse into
indistinguishable tokens. The current common practice uses k-means initialized VQ-VAE [27–29].
We consider two representative tasks here: crystalline material tokenization in Sec 4.2.1 and robot
action tokenization in Sec 4.2.2.
8


4.2.1
Crystalline Material Tokenization
We evaluate tokenization techniques for crystalline materials based on their ability to faithfully
represent crystal structures using an atom-based encoder–decoder transformer. Crystals exhibit
periodic atomic arrangements in 3D space and are commonly represented by a unit cell containing a
set of atoms. Each atom is described by a tuple (A, X, L), where A is its one-hot atom type vector,
X is its 3D fractional coordinates, and L is the lattice information. These components are then
concatenated into a single vector before being fed into the model.
We evaluate on MP-20 [46] with |C| = 8192 and d = 8. As VQ-VAE and OptVQ benefit from an
aligned initialization [27–29], and to isolate the effect of the quantizer, we initialize their codebooks
with k-means centroids of the training-set latents from the same autoencoder at epoch W = 500, when
FlowVQ quantization begins. All methods also start from the same encoder and decoder weights. For
evaluation, we follow prior work [47] by comparing each reconstructed material with its ground-truth
material using StructureMatcher from PyMatgen [48] and reporting the resulting match rate. We do
not report codebook utilization rate for this task because MP-20 is a relatively small dataset, and
the evaluation distribution does not fully cover the training distribution, which is reflected as only
7,408 of the 8,192 codes derived from the training data k-means clusters were actively used on the
evaluation set. This number can further vary with the latent geometry evolving during training. We
analyze the number of actively utilized codes instead.

Conclusion
To summarize, we introduced FlowVQ, a vector quantization technique that reformulates codebook
learning in classic VQ-VAE as a quantile-based transport process, where balanced code utilization
is governed by the geometry of the latent space rather than a byproduct of auxiliary optimization
objectives. By constructing the codebook via deterministic pushforwards from a uniform prior
and refining them through self-correction, FlowVQ achieves stable and balanced code usage while
avoiding codebook collapse. We conducted experiments across a range of tokenization and generation
tasks, demonstrating consistently improved reconstruction and generation quality. We hope the
proposed method will serve as a useful tool, especially in fields where features are naturally continuous
or hybrid, and benefit from learned representations.
Limitations and Future Work
In addition to the slight increase in training time, FlowVQ primarily
focuses on addressing codebook collapse, while another important limitation of VQ-VAE lies in the
reliance on the biased STE for backpropagation through discrete quantization. Recent works have
shown that this gradient mismatch can be alleviated through simple rotation tricks [59], but more
effective alternatives remain largely unexplored. Another direction worth exploring is to investigate
FlowVQ together with recent architectures on flexible-length [60, 61] or flexible-resolution [62]
embedding learning, which may enable more adaptive token allocation to regions with higher semantic
or structural complexity, leading to further improvements.

Material Tokenization
We follow [63] for the data processing, model design and training for crystalline material tokenization
using atom only features without using atom neighbor information. Each material is represented by
its minimum repeating unit, containing the lattice information as 3 lattice base vectors L = [l1, l2, l3],

We used a transformer-based encoder-decoder architecture, where the encoder contains 6 and decoder
contains 2 transformer blocks. The hidden dimension is 512 and each transformer block uses 8
attention heads. We set the starting epoch W to 500, and update the codebook at every M = 5 epochs.
The model is trained using Adam optimizer, with constant learning rate 1e −4 and batch size 128.
The codebook size K = 8192 and vector size d = 8. The loss function is defined as follows where
Lcodebook is enabled only when online correction is used, and α = 0.01 is chosen for both VQ-VAE
and FlowVQ to keep all loss terms on comparable scales:
L(θ, γ) = Lrecon + LFM + α(Lcodebook + βLcommitment)
For baseline models, we performed k-means clustering on the training data latent vectors of the
autoencoder at epoch W using FAISS [65]1, and used the resulting cluster centroids to initialize
VQ-VAE and Opt-VQ codebook vectors. To ensure consistency with the initial latent space, and fair
comparison from the same starting point, we also initialized the encoder and decoder weights using
those of the autoencoder. For LFQ, we used the implementation from vector-quantize-pytorch
library2.

Action Tokenization
The action tokenization is evaluated on the DROID dataset [55], which contains 76k successful
demonstration trajectories. We split the original trajectories into 80% train, 10% validation and 10%
test set, then further processed them into non-overlapping chunks of 50 steps. The codebook size
K is set to 2048 for fair comparison with existing tokenizers, and vector size d = 8. The codebook
construction starts at step W = 1000th, and is update at every 250th steps using batch size 512.
The model contains 8 transformer blocks for both encoder and decoder, using 8 attention heads and
hidden dimension as 512. Similarly, we train the model using a constant learning rate 1e−4 and Adam
optimizer.
For the baseline FAST+ tokenizer, we use the weight downloaded from its original HuggingFace
repository3.
