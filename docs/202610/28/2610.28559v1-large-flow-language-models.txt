Abstract. Continuous flow language models offer a path to parallel generation with tools for control and acceleration unavailable to discrete diffusion, yet effective techniques for building them at the billion-parameter scale remain less established. We present Lafa (La rge f low l anguage model) ,a fast, high-performing flow language model scaled to 8B parameters in a data-efficient manner. Lafa is a blockwise flow model with causal attention on the prefix and bidirectional, asynchronous flow within each block. This design enables efficient adaptation of pretrained autoregressive (AR) models and fast inference via prefix caching and blockwise parallel decoding. We train Lafa at 0.5B, 7B, and 8B scales with fewer than 30B tokens, more efficient than previous diffusion models, and evaluate it on instruction following, math, and coding, where the learned flow matches or surpasses the quality of AR and can be distilled into flow maps for one- and few-step generation. Notably, flow training preserves or even improves pretrained AR capabilities via a multi-token prediction effect, which we leverage in a self-speculative decoding scheme that verifies flow-based drafts autoregressively within a single model. This yields 2.54 × throughput of an AR model at comparable quality, outperforming multi-token prediction and discrete diffusion methods. Our findings show that continuous flows can serve as a scalable foundation for language modeling.

Today’s large language models are predominantly trained as autoregressive (AR) models that predict the next token from the preceding text [1–3]. The parallelism of the transformer architecture and teacher forcing [4, 5] allows these models to be trained efficiently at the scale of billions of parameters and trillions of data tokens. However, their generation is inher-ently sequential, producing one token per forward pass, which makes inference latency a bottleneck. To accelerate inference, prior work has explored approaches to parallel generation, namely multi-token prediction (MTP) [6–8], discrete diffusion models [9–11], and continuous flow language models [12–14]. Distinguished from the other approaches, flow language mod-els perform parallel generation by integrating a continuous flow from noise to token representations [12]. By operating in a continuous space, they can leverage powerful techniques de-veloped for controllable and accelerated generation of images and video, many of which are unavailable to discrete meth-ods [13, 14]. Together, effective techniques for building performant flow language models at the billion-parameter scale remain less established. In this paper, we present Lafa (La rge f low l anguage model) , a fast and high-performing flow language model scaled to 8B parameters in a data-efficient manner. Motivated by the left-to-right directionality of text [19], we design Lafa as a blockwise flow model, with causal attention on the prefix, and bidirectional but left-to-right-biased asynchronous flow within each block. This brings its architecture and inductive bias close to AR models, enabling efficient training by adapting pretrained models. Further, this allows fast inference via prefix caching and parallel flow decoding, which can be further distilled into flow maps for one- and few-step generation. We train Lafa with blockwise denoising for the flow, along with AR prediction on the prefix, and find that flow training preserves or even enhances pretrained AR capabilities through a multi-token prediction effect. We leverage this in a self-speculative decoding scheme that verifies flow-based drafts autoregressively. With these techniques, we train Lafa across 0.5B, 7B, and 8B scales with fewer than 30B tokens, cheaper than comparable adaptation of discrete diffusion models that use up to 500B tokens [20, 21]. Across benchmarks spanning instruction following, reasoning, and coding, Lafa attains a quality–speed frontier that surpasses similarly sized AR, MTP, and discrete diffusion language models, as shown in Figure 1. An overview of the approach is in Figure 2. Our main contributions are as follows: 

• We introduce Lafa, a large flow language model that learns blockwise asynchronous flow, enabling efficient training by adapting pretrained AR models and fast inference through prefix caching and parallel flows. 

• We identify key design choices for scalability, including the architecture and asynchronous flow formulation, training and flow map distillation objectives, and inference schemes including self-speculative decoding. 

• We scale Lafa to 7–8B parameters with <30B tokens and show that its learned flow matches or surpasses the quality of AR and is distillable into flow maps, and its learning improves pretrained AR capabilities. 

• By jointly leveraging the flow and AR capabilities for self-speculative decoding, Lafa attains 2 .54 × the throughput of a strong AR baseline at matched quality, outperforming existing parallel generation methods. Our findings show that continuous flows offer a practical, scalable foundation for parallel language modeling. 22 Large flow language models

As our formulation keeps a causal structure, we learn the blockwise flow by adapting a pretrained AR model 

f into a block denoiser. The challenge is parallel denoising of continuous, noisy embeddings while retaining AR prediction and prefix caching. Our adaptation combines tokenwise time conditioning, block-bidirectional attention, and constrained updates to pretrained embeddings with joint AR and denoising training. 

Tokenwise time conditioning. We feed f the embedded prefix c followed by the noisy block x, and condition each position on its corruption level through adaptive layer normalization (adaLN) [31]. Prefix positions always receive time 1, indicating the absence of noise, while each block position i ∈ [B] receives its tokenwise time τ i(t). This lets a single forward pass handle both the clean prefix and asynchronous denoising within the block. We initialize adaLN to the identity, so that adding time conditioning initially leaves the pretrained computation unchanged. We denote the resulting model by ft(x; c). Block-bidirectional attention. We make attention bidirectional within the noisy block, allowing partially denoised later tokens to inform earlier ones (Figure 3). Block positions also attend to the entire clean prefix, whose attention remains causal and excludes the noisy block [32, 33]. Together with conditioning prefix positions on time 1, this makes their keys and values independent of the block state ( x, t ), allowing reuse across denoising steps and subsequent blocks. Unconstrained updates, however, can inflate embedding norms relative to the fixed noise scale σ, making denoising easier by reducing the effective corruption [12, 13]. Learning tokenwise time. We learn the tokenwise time τ : [0 , 1] →

[0 , 1] B from the denoising loss during training. Inspired by the loss-aware time warping of CDCD [12], we construct each τ i as the inverse CDF of the normalized denoising loss across noise levels, estimated online for position i (details in Appendix B.1). This slows progress where the loss is high, so uniform sampling and discretization of base time t allocate more training samples and integration steps to difficult noise levels. The learned schedules generally satisfy τ i(t) > τ j (t) for i < j (Figure 5), with earlier positions denoised ahead of later ones, even though this left-to-right bias is not manually imposed. 

Self-conditioning. Following prior work [14, 15, 36], we use self-conditioning [37] to improve sample quality. With a fixed probability during training, we obtain a denoising prediction without gradients, feed it back as an additional input, and compute the loss on a second forward pass (details in Appendix B.2). During flow decoding, each Euler step reuses the previous step’s prediction for conditioning, requiring no added forward. 

Flow map distillation. Flow decoding requires multiple denoiser evaluations per block. To reduce this cost, we distill the learned flow into a flow map Xs,t that transports a state directly from time s to t [38]. For this, we make a novel extension of simplex-valued two-time denoisers [13] to asynchronous flows over vocabulary embeddings. Specifically, δs,t (x; c) ∈ (∆ |V |− 1)B predicts token probabilities, which we map through E before interpolating with the noisy state x:

Xs,t (x; c)i := 1 − τ i(t)1 − τ i(s) xi + τ i(t) − τ i(s)1 − τ i(s) δs,t (x; c)iE, i ∈ [B], 0 ≤ s < t ≤ 1. (7) This retains categorical predictions for distillation while transporting states in embedding space. We initialize the student ˆδs,t from the frozen teacher ˆDs, adding an identity-initialized adaLN for the destination time 

t. Following semigroup-based distillation [13, 36], we match the student’s diagonal predictions at s = t to the teacher ˆDs and its off-diagonal predictions at s̸ = t to a stop-gradient target formed by composing two shorter student transitions. We use KL losses for both terms and additionally distill the teacher’s prefix AR predictions. ## 3.1 Flow and flow map decoding 

We first evaluate flow generation with block sizes B ∈ { 4, 8, 16 }, varying the number of Euler integration steps in (8). We also test whether flow map distillation improves few-step generation. Figure 6 summarizes the results (per-benchmark scores in Appendix C.1). Across scales and block sizes, flow accuracy generally improves with more integration steps, demonstrating effective iterative refinement. With sufficient steps, flow decoding with block size four approaches AR SFT accuracy at 0.5B and surpasses it at 7B. At a fixed number of steps, larger blocks generally trade accuracy for greater parallelism. For flow map decoding, we distill the models with block size four and observe improved accuracy in the one- and two-step regimes. The gains are largest at 7B, where the two-step student approaches the four-step teacher; the student also outperforms the teacher at four steps and reaches similar accuracy at eight steps. Nevertheless, a quality gap remains relative to flow decoding with more integration steps. ## 3.2 Autoregressive decoding 

We next examine how joint flow training affects the retained AR capability (Section 2.2), evaluating Lafa and baselines under single-token decoding. As shown in Table 1, Lafa improves average accuracy over matched AR SFT across all three model scales (per-benchmark results in Appendix C.1). It also outperforms the matched multi-token and block-parallel baselines on average at 0.5B and 7B. At 8B, Lafa achieves similar accuracy to Efficient-DLM evaluated with single-token unmasking, while using at least 18 × fewer training tokens. These results show that learning the flow can also improve AR prediction, consistent with the benefits of multi-token training [6, 8, 34]. We therefore use this retained AR predictor to verify flow-based drafts for self-speculative decoding (Section 3.3). 

## 3.3 Self-speculative decoding 

We now evaluate self-speculative decoding, which verifies one-step flow drafts with the retained AR predictor (Section 2.3). We measure both parallelism, in tokens committed per forward pass (TPF), and end-to-end wall-clock throughput. In this section, we review existing representative approaches for parallel language modeling. 

Block-autoregressive language models. Block-autoregressive models reduce sequential generation by producing blocks of tokens conditioned on preceding blocks. Approaches to parallel block generation include multi-token prediction (MTP) heads or modules [6, 8], and predictors that take masked or noisy blocks as input [23, 33, 46]. Many parallel decoders predict tokens independently within an update, which can degrade quality when many tokens are committed at once [13]. Some methods mitigate this through AR verification or confidence-based acceptance of a draft prefix [8, 33, 41]. Some recent methods learn deterministic noise-to-token maps that can capture joint dependencies without independent output sampling [47, 57]. 

Discrete diffusion language models. Discrete diffusion models generate text by reversing a discrete corruption process, such as masking or random token replacement, through iterative denoising [58–61]. As with other parallel decoders, independent sampling within each update can limit quality in few-step generation [62, 63]. These models have been scaled to billions of parameters [11, 20, 21, 45], with generation performed over entire sequences or individual blocks. Block diffusion [23] combines iterative denoising within each block with autoregressive generation and prefix caching across blocks. 

Continuous diffusion and flow language models. Continuous diffusion and flow models generate text through noisy continuous representations, including token embeddings, simplex representations, and binary encodings [12, 18, 28, 37, 64–67]. Continuous flows transport joint sequence representations and admit flow maps that can be learned for few-step generation [13, 38]. Recent work has demonstrated competitive generation quality and improved few-step decoding [13–17, 36, 68], while a few recent efforts have explored billion-parameter scales [69–72]. Related approaches also study autoregression and diffusion over autoencoded continuous representations [73–76]. Realizing the benefits of continuous flows at large and practical scale calls for data-efficient training and fast inference. Lafa addresses both by aligning blockwise asynchronous flows with the causal structure of pretrained AR models. This enables efficient adaptation and prefix caching, while retained AR prediction supports self-speculative generation. 

# 5 Conclusion 

We introduced Lafa, a blockwise flow language model scaled to 8B parameters by adapting pretrained AR models with fewer than 30B training tokens. Lafa combines causal generation across blocks with learned asynchronous flows within each block, preserving prefix caching while enabling parallel generation. With sufficient integration steps, flow decoding approaches or surpasses AR SFT accuracy, and flow map distillation improves few-step generation. Joint training also improves AR accuracy over matched AR SFT. This retained AR predictor verifies one-step flow drafts within the same model, enabling self-speculative decoding. With this approach, Lafa 7B achieves 2 .54 × the throughput of the 7B AR SFT baseline at comparable average accuracy. These results demonstrate a practical path to scaling continuous flows for language modeling through data-efficient AR adaptation. Improving flow map distillation, particularly its incorporation of self-conditioning and prefix guidance, remains a promising direction for further accelerating flow generation.

## C.3 Confidence decoding 

As an alternative to self-speculative decoding, we follow MARS [33] and accept the longest left-to-right prefix of a one-step flow draft whose token confidence exceeds a threshold λ (Appendix B.3), and apply the same rule to block-autoregressive baselines. We implement it with the fused decoding of Algorithm 4, where ˆy1: a is instead the longest prefix of ˆ y with confidences ( pi)ˆyi ≥ λ (where we always set a ≥ 1). Since a is 26 known before the forward pass, only the block z(a) is needed instead of all B blocks. To apply this strategy, Dream and LLaDA use their native global entropy or confidence ordering over masked positions, whereas Fast-dLLM v2 applies the threshold within the active sub-block. In Figure 10, we show an accuracy–TPF tradeoff measured by sweeping over confidence thresholds. At 0.5B, Lafa with block size four attains the highest peak accuracy among all methods, including AR SFT, and its accuracy remains at or above that of pure AR at high thresholds. At 7B, Lafa exceeds AR SFT at high thresholds, while MARS, which is trained for confidence decoding, degrades more gracefully as the threshold is lowered. At 8B, Lafa remains above AR SFT across the tested range and on par with Efficient-DLM. Since AR verification avoids the degradation at lower thresholds without tuning a threshold, we use self-speculative decoding as our default in the main text. 27
