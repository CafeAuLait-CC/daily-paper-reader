Abstract
On-policy distillation (OPD) enables flow-matching
models to acquire complementary capabilities from special-
ized teachers through supervision on student-generated tra-
jectories. However, different evaluation dimensions (e.g.,
aesthetics and OCR accuracy) and tasks (e.g., image gener-
ation and editing) emphasize different aspects of the denois-
ing process, suggesting that their effective supervision may
occur at different stages. Existing methods typically select
supervision states from predefined timestep distributions,
which may overlook these phase-dependent differences with
unnecessary computation overhead.
This motivates two
critical timing decisions: when to adaptively query teachers
for effective supervision based on the evolving generative
process, and when to skip the remaining rollout to avoid
redundant computation, i.e., timing matters twice. In this
paper, we introduce PhaseOPD, a phase-aware query-and-
skip framework that identifies generative phases online from
revisions of predicted-clean latents, using existing student
velocity predictions without additional model evaluations.
For each task-assigned teacher, PhaseOPD selects a super-
vision state based on its phase preference and skips the un-
used rollout suffix once all query decisions are finalized,
preserving the single-query velocity-distillation objective.
On SD3.5-M, PhaseOPD executes only 5.56 of 16 rollout
positions per local batch (65.2% fewer), reducing training
time by 41.3% relative to DanceOPD while improving Aes-
thetic, GenEval, and OCR scores.

These teachers then provide supervision on the student’s
own generation trajectories, integrating diverse generative
capabilities into a unified student model.
Although OPD provides an effective approach to multi-
objective optimization, existing OPD paradigms do not ad-
equately characterize how the value of each task-specific
teacher’s supervision changes across generation stages. Be-
cause each teacher is trained for a distinct objective, the
key question is which part of the student’s trajectory pro-
vides the most effective supervision for that teacher and its
corresponding task—not which teacher should be assigned
to a given stage. This raises two connected questions: for
each teacher-task pair, at what stage should the student be
queried to obtain the most useful supervision? Once the rel-
evant query for each task has been completed, when can the
remaining rollout be terminated to avoid redundant compu-
tation? When query timing is not specified for each teacher-
task pair, it remains unclear whether a chosen supervision
stage is well suited to that teacher’s objective. This matters
because denoising follows a stage-dependent pattern: early
stages focus on constructing global semantics, intermediate
stages gradually shift toward local content generation, and
later stages place greater emphasis on refining details and
visual aesthetics. These evolving responsibilities mean that
the utility of a given teacher’s guidance depends on whether
the student’s state at a particular stage is suitable for that
teacher’s corresponding objective. Thus, supervision tim-
ing should be determined for each teacher-task pair, rather
than by assigning teachers to stages in isolation. Beyond ef-
fectively scheduling teacher supervision, avoiding unneces-
sary computational overhead is another important challenge
for multi-teacher OPD. Obtaining teacher supervision re-
quires long and continuous rollouts of student trajectories,
which introduces considerable computational costs. More
importantly, once all necessary teacher queries have been
completed, or once the denoising process is close to conver-
gence and the latent representation has become nearly sta-
tionary, continuing the remaining rollout provides little ad-
ditional information for distillation. It may even introduce
optimization conflicts due to a mismatch between teacher
supervision and generation dynamics. Therefore, skipping
redundant trajectory supervision while preserving its effec-
tiveness is crucial to improving OPD training efficiency.

Building on these insights, we propose PhaseOPD, a
dual timing-adaptive policy distillation framework that uses
the student’s generation dynamics to schedule each task-
specific teacher at the stages where its supervision is
most useful. Unlike existing methods that focus primar-
ily on task-level teacher selection without jointly optimiz-
ing teacher-query timing and trajectory length, PhaseOPD
identifies a suitable query interval for each teacher-task
pair from the student’s evolving trajectory. For example, it
schedules structure-oriented supervision during global lay-
out and primary structure formation, supervision for objec-
tives involving both structure and detail during the transi-
tion toward semantic content and salient details, and visual-
quality supervision during later texture and appearance re-
finement. The task-specific teacher defines what is super-
vised, while the trajectory dynamics determine when its su-
pervision is applied. This constitutes the first level of adap-
tivity in PhaseOPD. In addition, PhaseOPD uses generation
dynamics and the marginal utility of task-specific supervi-
sion to detect diminishing returns. It terminates the remain-
ing rollout once no further teacher query is needed, thereby
reducing computational overhead. This constitutes the sec-
ond level of adaptivity in PhaseOPD and follows Occam’s
razor: introduce supervision only when necessary.
In summary, our main contributions are threefold: (1)
We revisit teacher organization in multi-teacher on-policy
distillation by introducing a generation-dynamics dimen-
sion orthogonal to the task dimension. We further iden-
tify the mismatch between teacher supervision and denois-
ing dynamics, a problem that task-level OPD cannot ad-
equately characterize. (2) We propose PhaseOPD, which
uses the student’s dynamics to identify, for each task-
specific teacher, the generation intervals where its super-
vision is most valuable and schedules queries accordingly.
This reduces cross-stage interference and unnecessary over-
constraint. (3) We introduce a dynamic truncation strategy
that immediately terminates the subsequent rollout once the
supervision signals determine the required teacher-query
positions, avoiding redundant computation.
2. Motivation
Mainstream OPD paradigms overlook that different special-
ized teacher capabilities should align with the characteris-
tics of different denoising stages. This oversight can cre-
ate temporal conflicts between teacher supervision and de-
noising dynamics. If a teacher specializing in local details
intervenes before the global structure stabilizes, its super-
vision may prematurely steer the model toward local op-
timization objectives and disrupt primary structure forma-
tion. Meanwhile, because local details rely on an estab-
lished global structure as their underlying carrier, the
absence of a stable structural foundation also prevents
detail-oriented supervision from fully realizing its value.
Conversely, once the primary structure has converged and
the model has entered the local refinement stage, continued
strong structural supervision may unnecessarily constrain
subsequent detail enhancement. More generally, even if a
teacher’s objective is reasonable, applying its supervision
at an incompatible generation stage may still produce low-
value or even competing gradients.
This issue resembles course scheduling in education. A
student’s learning outcome depends not only on what is

taught, but also on whether the course sequence matches
the student’s current developmental stage. The visualization

cializing in semantic structure achieve the best results when
queried early in the generation stage.
In contrast, Opti-
cal Character Recognition (OCR) teachers focusing on tex-
tual content and teachers specializing in aesthetic quality
yield greater supervisory benefits during the middle and late
stages, respectively.
3. Method
This section presents PhaseOPD’s overall framework,
extending task-level teacher organization to generation-
dynamics-aware teacher coordination. We first introduce
an adaptive generation-stage identification module that an-
alyzes the student model’s own generation trajectory to de-
termine the generative responsibility of its current state.
Based on the identified stage, PhaseOPD employs a dy-
namic teacher-routing mechanism that assigns teachers with
different capabilities to the generation stages where their su-
pervision is most effective, thereby reducing cross-stage su-
pervision conflicts. Finally, we propose a dynamic rollout
truncation mechanism to eliminate redundant computation.

3.2. Online Generative Phase Perception
Existing OPD methods select teachers primarily accord-
ing to task categories. However, a teacher’s supervisory
value varies across states and generation stages. To allo-
cate teacher supervision on demand following the inherent
generation dynamics, PhaseOPD detects stage transitions
during the student model’s on-policy rollout and adaptively
determines when each teacher should provide supervision.

3.3. Teacher-Conditioned Phase Routing
Building on the three generation stages identified in
Sec.3.2 , we establish a correspondence between generation
stages and supervision objectives, enabling each specialized
teacher to contribute during the stage in which its supervi-
sion is most valuable.
Stage I: Global Structure Formation. Since the overall
structure has not yet fully formed at this stage, local details
lack a stable structural foundation. This stage is therefore
better suited to supervision targeting global structural con-
sistency, such as GenEval. We set the teacher query posi-
tion to the sampling position immediately preceding the left
boundary, namely kL −1. This choice is motivated by the
observation that states too close to the pure-noise endpoint
lack well-defined structure. Selecting the last sampling po-
sition before the left boundary thus allows structure-related
supervision to be applied once structural information has
begun to emerge, but before local details become dominant.
Stage II: Local Content Generation. In Stage II, the pri-
mary structure provides a relatively stable spatial founda-
tion for local content, while local semantics and details con-
tinue to emerge and evolve. This stage is therefore well
suited to specialized teachers that focus on local content ac-
curacy, such as OCR teachers. We randomly sample a su-
pervision point within Stage II to cover varying degrees of
local content formation, avoid excessive reliance on a single
generation position, and improve the adaptability of local
content supervision to variations in states within this stage.
Stage III: Visual Aesthetic Enhancement. In the final stage
of generation, the image approaches convergence, with the
primary structure and local details remaining largely un-
changed. This stability provides a foundation for aesthetic
optimization, making this stage well suited to specialized
aesthetic teachers, such as Aesthetic. In Stage III, we set the
teacher query position to the first sampling position after the
right boundary, namely kR + 1. This choice lets aesthetic
supervision act on a state where the primary structure and
local content are already stable, while still leaving room for
further refinement.
Once the supervision position for the current sample is
determined, the teacher provides supervision only at the
corresponding student-visited state, preserving the basic
formulation of on-policy distillation while reducing cross-
stage supervision mismatch and redundant trajectory com-
putation. The unified query rule can be written as:

4. Experiments
This chapter evaluates PhaseOPD around three questions:
(1) whether staged querying can improve both generation
quality and training cost with the primary teacher group; (2)
whether these gains generalize across teacher combinations,
student backbones, and task types; and (3) what roles adap-
tive triggering and early termination play, and why online
signals can identify effective querying stages. We present
the main results, generalization experiments, component
ablations, and mechanistic analyses in that order. Detailed
settings and additional results are provided in Appendix B.

4.2. Main Results
Tab. 1 summarizes overall performance across multiple ca-
pabilities and training cost. Although DanceOPD and Dif-
fusionOPD can integrate knowledge from multiple teachers,

These re-
sults suggest that adaptively identifying and accommodat-
ing diffusion models’ generation dynamics through tar-
geted teacher supervision can improve multi-teacher knowl-
edge fusion while avoiding unused rollout computation.
Consistent improvements on independent diagnostic met-
rics—ImageReward and VQAScore in Tab. 1—further in-
dicate that these gains generalize beyond the training ob-
jectives, rather than merely reflecting overfitting to teacher
rewards. Fig. 5 further presents representative results for
the primary teacher group. We select Aesthetic, GenEval,
and OCR prompts from a fixed sampling list. All methods
within each row use the same prompt, initial noise, and in-
ference configuration, so differences in visual quality, com-
positional generation, and text rendering directly reflect the
three corresponding metrics in Tab. 1.
We further compare the cost required by different meth-
ods to reach the same visual metric scores. Fig. 3 shows
the learning curves of the three methods under the same
wall-clock budget. Our method reaches competitive qual-
ity levels earlier and maintains stable performance across
multiple capabilities throughout subsequent training, indi-
cating that its advantages do not stem from a longer training
budget or a fortuitously selected final checkpoint. Together
with the final results in Tab. 1, these findings show that our
method consistently achieves a more favorable quality–time
trade-off throughout training. The respective contributions
of staged querying and early termination are further exam-

Phase-aware querying can be directly integrated into

cific distillation loss or implementation and can serve as a
general-purpose component for different OPD methods.

4.5. Ablation Studies
Our method comprises adaptive phase querying (AT) and
early skipping (ES). To examine their respective roles, we
remove AT, ES, and both components in Tab. 4. Remov-
ing ES yields Aesthetic performance close to that of the
full model, with slight decreases in GenEval and OCR and
a substantial increase in training time. This suggests that
ES effectively reduces rollout computation while preserv-
ing generation quality. Removing only AT lowers all three
quality metrics and increases training time, indicating that
adaptive phase querying helps balance effective supervision
with computational efficiency. When we remove both AT
and ES, GenEval improves, but Aesthetic and OCR decline
substantially, revealing trade-offs among capabilities. The
full model achieves the best Aesthetic and OCR scores and
the second-best GenEval score, while offering the highest
training efficiency. These results support the complemen-
tary roles of AT and ES: AT selects supervision points ac-
cording to the generation phase, while ES eliminates sub-
sequent computation once the necessary queries have been
completed. Together, they achieve a better balance between
generation quality and training efficiency.

4.7. User Preference Study
To evaluate the generated results from a human percep-
tual perspective, we conduct a double-blind user preference
study comparing our method against DanceOPD and Diffu-
sionOPD in pairwise comparisons. Each pair of candidate
images is generated using the same text prompt, random
seed, and inference configuration and presented in a ran-
domized order. Participants are unaware of which method
produced each image and are asked to select the better result
based on overall visual quality, text alignment, and content
completeness, or to indicate a tie. As shown in Fig. 10,
our method receives higher user preference rates against
both multi-teacher distillation baselines. These findings are
consistent with the automatic evaluations, indicating that
the improvements from phase-aware querying translate into
perceptible gains in generation quality rather than merely
numerical improvements on specific evaluation metrics.
5. Conclusion
We presented PhaseOPD, which connects teacher super-
vision timing with early termination of student rollouts.
Causal signals from predicted-clean latent revisions identify
teacher-specific query states, allowing single-query distilla-
tion to proceed without generating the remaining trajectory.
Experiments show fewer rollout steps, lower training cost,
the strongest GenEval score in our comparison, and faster
first-hour gains on GenEval and OCR. The results support
jointly designing where to learn and how far to roll out as a
practical route to efficient multi-capability post-training.

A. Related Work
A.1. Post-training generative models.
Diffusion models [6] and flow matching [10] learn itera-
tive transformations between noise and data; rectified-flow
transformers scale this approach to high-resolution text-to-
image synthesis [4]. Post-training methods align these mod-
els with downstream objectives. DDPO treats denoising as
a sequential decision process and applies policy gradients to
image rewards [3]; Diffusion-DPO instead learns from pair-
wise preferences [15]. Flow-GRPO extends online policy
optimization to flow matching and reduces the number of
training denoising steps [11]. PhaseOPD integrates expert
capabilities and reduces rollout computation by identifying
supervision states online.
A.2. On-policy capability transfer.
Training on student-generated sequences is a central prin-
ciple of on-policy language-model distillation [1]. Diffu-
sionOPD transfers task-specific teachers along student tra-
jectories using a continuous-state distillation formulation
[8]. Flow-OPD combines specialized teachers, task rout-
ing, and dense trajectory supervision for flow models [5].
DanceOPD routes each sample to one generative field and
uses velocity regression at a low-noise student state [20].
We retain task-specific teachers and single-query regres-
sion.
Our contribution concerns how student dynamics
determine the query state and the minimum rollout prefix
needed to select it; we do not claim a new regression loss or
single-query distillation principle.
A.3. Stage specialization and efficient sampling.
eDiff-I assigns separately trained denoisers to different syn-
thesis stages [2], demonstrating the usefulness of time-
dependent specialization. PhaseOPD instead schedules su-
pervision from capability-specific teachers using the evolv-
ing student state, producing one student for inference. Pro-
gressive distillation reduces the number of steps required
to generate a completed sample [14]. Our early stopping
serves a different purpose: it removes the unneeded suffix of
a training trajectory once its supervision state is known. It
leaves the inference procedure unchanged and can be stud-
ied independently of methods that accelerate completed-
image sampling.
A.4. On-Policy Distillation
On-policy distillation supervises students on self-generated
states, reducing training–generation mismatch [1]. For vi-
sual generation, Flow-OPD combines task routing with
dense trajectory supervision [5], while DiffusionOPD de-
rives a continuous-state distillation objective [8]. Flow3D-
OPD extends multi-teacher transfer to 3D geometry [13],
and DanceOPD uses one low-noise velocity query per sam-
ple [20].
These methods leave a complementary timing
question: which state best supports each teacher’s objective,
and how much rollout is needed to select it? PhaseOPD
uses student dynamics to determine teacher-specific query
positions and skips the remaining rollout once all query de-
cisions are finalized.

B.1. Detailed Setup
Implementation Details & Training Data.
We use
SD3.5-Medium as the base model for multi-teacher distil-

r = 32 and scaling factor α = 64. We use frozen task-

and 4.5 for the three teachers, respectively. Tasks are sam-
pled with equal probability, and all distillation loss weights
are set to 1. The main experiments are run on four H20
GPUs for 3,000 updates with a learning rate of 10−4.
Metrics.
We evaluate image aesthetic quality using Aes-

and OCR, respectively.

PickScore, SigLIP, ImageReward, and VQAScore as aux-

Baselines.

teacher models exhibit different sensitivities to the query-

The GenEval teacher exhibits a clear preference for

not consistently provide more effective teacher supervision.
These distinct temporal response profiles provide controlled
experimental evidence for determining query timing based
