ABSTRACT 

Discrete flow matching (DFM) offers a promising approach to graph generation; however, generating high-quality graphs with few steps remains challenging due to the strong dependencies between nodes and edges. Although DFM decouples training from sampling, enabling flexible sampling strategies without retraining, existing graph DFM samplers often rely on task-specific heuristics, substantially expanding the hyperparameter space. Moreover, graph DFM models still require sampling budgets comparable to those of diffusion-based approaches, leaving their potential for accelerating graph generation only partially realized. In this work, we introduce H-Glow, a hazard-guided framework for accelerating graph generation built on the decomposition of Continuous-Time Markov chain (CTMC) generators into hazards and conditional jump distributions. H-Glow leverages these quantities to plan graph edits and dynamically adjust the sampling time to graph activity. H-Glow can be adapted to pretrained graph DFM models, substan-tially improving the generation quality with fewer steps. We further introduce a novel graph DFM model based on the conditional generator matching framework, which directly predicts hazards and conditional jumps, and show that its training objective decomposes exactly into hazard matching and hazard-weighted jump prediction. Experiments on molecular and synthetic graph benchmarks demon-strate improved generation quality with substantially fewer sampling steps. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Graph generative models have the potential to accelerate materials design (Sanchez-Lengeling & Aspuru-Guzik, 2018) and drug discovery (Zhang et al., 2025) by designing new molecular structures. Discrete-state graph diffusion models (Vignac et al., 2022) offer a promising approach for progres-sively refining categorical node and edge attributes through iterative denoising updates. However, their denoising process is defined on a fixed temporal grid, and sampling is therefore tied to the discrete time points used during training. Continuous-time graph diffusion models overcome this restriction by learning to denoise at arbitrary time points, allowing flexible time discretization during inference (Campbell et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2024; Siraudin et al., 2024). More recently, discrete flow matching (DFM) has achieved state-of-the-art performance on molecular and synthetic graph benchmarks (Qin et al., 2025). Despite the sampling acceleration achieved by flow-based models in domains such as images (Geng et al., 2026) and text generation (Sevriugov et al., 2026), graph flow models remain computationally costly and often require as many sampling steps as diffusion-based methods, leaving their potential for efficient generation only partially realized. The structural complexity of graphs poses distinct challenges for efficient sampling compared with image or text generation, as node and edge attributes are strongly interdependent. For example, a single incorrect bond can violate valency constraints and invalidate a molecule. Existing sam-plers update multiple nodes and edges in parallel without accounting for potential conflicts between updates, leading to violations of structural or chemical constraints. The approximation errors be-come more pronounced as the sampling budget decreases. To address this, prior work exploits the training-sampling decoupling of DFM and employs specialized sampling-time interventions to im-1arXiv:2610.33599v1 [cs.LG] 24 Oct 2026 Preprint 

Figure 1: Overview of H-Glow. prove generation quality, including target guidance, additional stochasticity, and tailored sampling-time discretizations (Campbell et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2026). However, these interventions expand the hyperparameter space and often require extensive tuning for each task and sampling budget during generation. We present H-Glow , a novel framework for graph generation built on the decomposition of CTMC generators into hazards and conditional jump distributions, as presented in Fig. 1. H-Glow decom-poses the generative process into three key components: (i) hazard-guided planning that prioritizes graph edits and coordinates node and edge updates; (ii) conditional jump distributions that guide the refinement of selected graph components; and (iii) a hazard-guided clock that dynamically adjusts the sampling-time distortion to the graph’s predicted activity. Our H-Glow sampler substantially ac-celerates graph generation and serves as a drop-in replacement for pretrained models with accessible CTMC generators. We further introduce the H-Glow model, a structured DFM model for graph gen-eration trained using the generator-matching framework (Holderrieth et al., 2025). H-Glow adopts a factorized CTMC parameterization to learn node and edge hazards, as well as conditional jump distributions (Li et al., 2026), thereby directly supervising the quantities that guide the sampling. We summarize our contributions as follows: • Hazard-guided graph sampling. We introduce H-Glow, a novel sampler that effectively lever-ages the model’s predicted hazards and jump distributions to prioritize graph edits and dynam-ically adapt sampling-time distortions to graph activity. Moreover, H-Glow serves as a drop-in sampler that accelerates pretrained models with accessible CTMC generators, without retrain-ing, auxiliary networks, or complex sampling interventions. • Structured graph generator learning framework. We introduce a graph DFM model that directly learns node and edge hazards, as well as conditional jump distributions, rather than re-lying on a denoiser network. Under the CTMC factorization, we derive an exact decomposition of the generator-matching objective into hazard matching and hazard-weighted jump prediction. • Empirical evaluation. H-Glow achieves high validity and strong FCD performance on molecu-lar benchmarks using substantially fewer sampling steps. Our ablations further demonstrate that the hazard-guided clock outperforms prior time discretizations and that our sampler surpasses existing samplers with fewer function evaluations.

Sampling complexity. Graph DFM models often rely on sophisticated sampling procedures, in-cluding predictor-corrector updates (Campbell et al., 2022), target guidance and additional stochas-ticity (Campbell et al., 2024), as well as carefully designed sampling-time distortions (Qin et al., 2025). In particular, Luo et al. (2026); Qin et al. (2025) design non-uniform time discretizations that concentrate computation in specific regions of the generation trajectory where graph validity is most sensitive. While effective, these techniques introduce additional hyperparameters and design choices, requiring substantial inference-time tuning for each task and sampling budget. 

Sampling efficiency. DFM unlocks flexible sampling strategies by decoupling model training from sampling. However, existing graph DFM methods still require a large number of function evalu-ations (NFE), often comparable to that of diffusion-based approaches. In practice, DFM methods simulate the process in Eq. (11) using finite-step approximations such as τ -leaping (Gillespie, 2001), which hold transition rates fixed over a finite interval and apply the resulting component updates si-multaneously. These fixed rates neglect interactions between updates within the interval, thereby introducing approximation errors when multiple graph components change. These errors are more pronounced in the low-step regime, where larger intervals tend to produce more simultaneous up-dates. Accurately coordinating node and edge transitions is therefore crucial for maintaining sample quality at low NFE.

7 RELATED WORK 

Discrete diffusion and flow matching for graphs. Discrete-state graph diffusion models rely on categorical corruption toward simple priors, such as uniform or empirical marginal distributions over node and edge attributes (Austin et al., 2021; Vignac et al., 2022), with recent extensions introducing re-noising and learned critics to identify corrupted nodes and edges (Boget, 2025) to improve sample quality. Recent graph diffusion approaches build on the continuous-time formulations based on 9Preprint CTMCs, which allow flexible sampling-time discretizations (Campbell et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2024; Siraudin et al., 2024); however, their reverse dynamics remain tied to the forward noising process. In contrast, discrete flow matching (DFM) (Campbell et al., 2024; Gat et al., 2024) decouples training from the choice of sampling dynamics. Recent graph adaptations (Qin et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2026) leverage the training-sampling decoupling and improve performance by optimizing rate matrices and time distortions during inference. While diffusion- and flow-based graph models typically learn a denoiser network, Li et al. (2026) recently proposed a diffusion model that directly parameterizes the network to predict hazards and conditional jumps. We adapt this parameterization within the generator-matching framework (Holderrieth et al., 2025) and propose a structured generator-learning framework for graphs. 

Improved sampling. Continuous-time approaches adopt finite-time approximators to simulate the CTMC trajectory, including independent Euler updates and τ -leaping (Campbell et al., 2022; Gat et al., 2024), where additional predictor-corrector steps can be used for further refinement. Liu et al. (2025) propose decomposing the generation into planning and denoising, and introduce a latent variable that indicates whether a token is corrupted. In contrast, H-Glow decomposes the generative process by directly exploiting the decomposition of CTMC generators into hazards and conditional jumps to guide sampling without an auxiliary planner network. In masked diffusion language models, iterative refinement is achieved by remasking previously decoded tokens, thereby enabling additional computation to improve sample quality Wang et al. (2026). 

8 CONCLUSION 

We present H-Glow, a hazard-guided framework for accelerating graph generation. By factorizing generators into hazards and conditional jump distributions, H-Glow leverages the event structure of CTMC generators to coordinate graph edits. Under the same decomposition, we propose a novel DFM model for graph generation. We limit our experiments to accelerate pretrained DFM models; however, H-Glow can be readily applied to discrete diffusion models with accessible generators, which we leave for future work. Also, H-Glow benefits from the empirical prior over node and edge attributes, where initialized edges are dominated by the no-bond category, unlocking accelerated molecular generation within a few NFEs. To accommodate graphs that require extensive updates, we provide an extension of H-Glow that enables parallel updates per step. QM9. We evaluate H-Glow on QM9 (Ramakrishnan et al., 2014). Following prior work (Vignac et al., 2022), we kekulize the molecules using the RDKit library and remove the hydrogen atoms. We use the same data split as Vignac et al. (2022) and train on 100K molecules. For the evaluation, we sample 10K molecules and report four metrics. The Validity is measured by sanitizing the sampled molecules and converting them to SMILES strings using the RDKit library. We also report the Relaxed Validity , which accounts for charged molecules. Uniqueness is measured across valid molecules, and it captures the proportion of non-isomorphic graphs within the generated samples. We also report the Fr´ echet ChemNet Distance (FCD) metric, which embeds the generated samples and the test data using the ChemNet neural network and compares the resulting distributions using the Wasserstein-2 distance. 

MOSES. We evaluate H-Glow on the MOSES (Polykovskiy et al., 2020) benchmark. We sample 25K samples and report the Validity, as well as the Uniqueness. We further report the Novelty ,which measures how many of these samples are non-isomorphic to any graph in the training set. We further include the Filters metric, which indicates the percentage of generated samples that pass the filters applied when constructing the dataset. In addition, we report Scaffold similarity (Scaf) ,which compares the frequencies of the Bemis-Murcko scaffolds in the generated set and a reference set, as well as the Similarity to the nearest neighbor (SNN) , which computes the average Tanimoto similarity between the molecular fingerprints of a generated set and the fingerprints of the molecules of a reference set 

Guacamol. We evaluate H-Glow on the Guacamol (Brown et al., 2019) benchmark. We note that, as Guacamol includes molecules with charges, the generated graphs are converted to charged molecules based on the relaxed validity criterion used by Jo et al. (2022) before being translated to the corresponding SMILES strings. We sample 10K samples following prior baselines and report the Validity, as well as the percentage of Valid and Unique (V.U.) samples, and Valid, Unique, and Novel (V.U.N.) samples. The Gua-caMol benchmark computes two scores, the FCD score and the KL divergences (KL) between the distributions of a set of physicochemical descriptors in the training set and the generated set. 

Synthetic graphs. We evaluate the generation performance of H-Glow using the synthetic bench-marks Planar (Martinkus et al., 2022) and Tree (Bergmeister et al., 2024). Planar graphs are con-nected planar graphs that can be drawn on a plane without any edges crossing, while tree graphs are connected acyclic graphs. We follow prior work (Vignac et al., 2022; Qin et al., 2025) and report V.U.N, which is the percentage of valid, unique, and novel graphs, as well as the Ratio metric. The ratio is computed by first calculating the MMD distances between the training and test sets for the same graph statistics. The final Ratio metric is then computed by dividing the average MMD distance between the generated sets and test sets by the average MMD distance between the training and test sets.
