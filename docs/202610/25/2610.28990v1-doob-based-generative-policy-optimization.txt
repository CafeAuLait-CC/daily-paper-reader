Diffusion and flow models provide expressive policy classes for robotic and sequential decision-making systems. However, their action likelihoods are generally intractable, limiting the direct use of widely adopted policy optimization methods such as PPO and GRPO. We propose Doob Pol-icy Optimization (DoobPO), a framework that leverages Doob’s h-transform for likelihood-free reinforcement learning fine-tuning of generative policies. DoobPO separates policy improvement from generative policy learning. It first learns a lightweight ratio model that represents the desired policy update, and then distills the resulting ratio-tilted target policy into the generative policy via conditional drift matching. This formulation applies uniformly to both diffusion and flow policies and supports a broad family of policy improvement rules, including PPO and GRPO. We prove that the practical conditional drift matching objective is gradient-equivalent to the ideal Doob drift matching objective. We further show that the induced diffusion process solves an endpoint-constrained stochastic optimal control problem. This establishes that it achieves the minimum-energy modification required to realize the desired policy tilt. Across MuJoCo continuous-control benchmarks, DoobPO achieves strong performance against competitive diffusion- and flow-policy baselines, showing a principled and practical route to RL fine-tuning for generative policies.

# 1 Introduction 

Generative policies based on diffusion models [Song and Ermon, 2019, Song et al., 2020, Ho et al., 2020] and flow matching models [Lipman et al., 2022, Liu et al., 2022] have emerged as a powerful paradigm for policy learning. Diffusion policies generate actions through iterative denoising, while flow policies generate actions by integrating a learned velocity field, often providing a deterministic and more efficient alternative. Due to their flexibility and accuracy in modeling high-dimensional action distributions, generative policies have achieved strong empirical gains over deterministic or unimodal policies in challenging continuous-control settings [Ke et al., 2024, Chi et al., 2025, Huang et al., 2024, Black et al., 2024, Intelligence et al., 2025]. A natural way to further improve generative policies is through reinforcement learning fine-tuning. However, a central challenge is that the exact likelihoods of generative policies are generally in-tractable, since each action is produced only after simulating an entire denoising or flow trajectory. This prevents the direct use of many stable and widely adopted policy optimization methods, such as PPO [Schulman et al., 2017] and GRPO [Shao et al., 2024]. Several recent works have at-tempted to bypass this likelihood challenge [Psenka et al., 2023, Ding et al., 2024, Ma et al., 2025, Ren et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2025, McAllister et al., 2025]. However, these methods often suffer from biased estimation, limited data efficiency, substantial computational overhead, or restriction to a particular generative policy class or policy update rule. Consequently, the full potential of RL for diffusion and flow policies remains underexplored. To address this problem, we propose Doob Policy Optimization (DoobPO) , a likelihood-free framework that separates policy improvement from generative policy learning. The first stage represents the desired policy improvement through a target policy ratio. The second stage distills the resulting ratio-tilted target into the generative policy. This decomposition avoids evaluating the likelihood of the generative policy itself. The policy-improvement stage is agnostic to the underlying generative model and accommodates a broad family of updates, including regularized Policy Mirror Descent [Zhan et al., 2023], Soft Actor-Critic [Haarnoja et al., 2018], PPO [Schul-man et al., 2017], and GRPO [Shao et al., 2024]. The generative stage uses Doob’s h-transform to characterize the ratio-tilted target process and learns this process through Doob’s Drift Matching. The same construction applies to both diffusion and flow policies. 

Contributions. Our contributions are three-fold: • Method. We propose Doob Policy Optimization (DoobPO) , a unified framework for RL fine-tuning of generative policies without evaluating exact action likelihoods (Section 3). DoobPO follows a two-stage design: it first obtains the target policy ratio and then transfers the resulting policy improvement into the generative policy through a tractable conditional drift matching objective. The framework supports both diffusion and flow policies (Sec-tions 3.1 and 3.2). We summarize the procedures in Algorithms 1 and 2. Instantiating DoobPO with different policy updates yields DoobPMD , DoobPPO , and DoobGRPO . We further show that several existing methods arise as special cases under particular choices of the policy update and generative policy class (Section 3.3). We summarize these instantia-tions in Table 1. 1• Theory. We characterize the ratio-tilted target policy through Doob’s h-transform (Lemma 3.1). This characterization leads to Doob’s Drift Matching as a principled way to realize the desired policy update. For tractability, we introduce a conditional drift matching objective and prove that its gradient is proportional to that of the ideal Doob’s Drift Match-ing objective for both diffusion and flow policies (Theorems 3.1 and 3.2). From the Stochas-tic Optimal Control perspective (Section 3.4), we further show that the Doob-transformed diffusion solves the corresponding endpoint-constrained control problem (Proposition 3.1). Hence, DoobPO learns the minimum-energy modification of the pretrained diffusion pro-cess that realizes the terminal tilt (Remark 3.2). • Experiments. We evaluate DoobPO on MuJoCo continuous-control benchmarks with both diffusion and flow policies (Section 4). For diffusion policies, DoobPO achieves the strongest overall performance against competitive diffusion-policy RL baselines, with lead-ing results on most tasks (Section 4.2). For flow policies, DoobGRPO achieves the best performance on 9 out of 10 tasks under the same interaction budget and delivers large gains on challenging locomotion environments (Section 4.3). These results establish DoobPO as a strong and practical framework for RL fine-tuning across both diffusion and flow policies.

# 3 Likelihood-Free Generative Policy Optimization 

We introduce Doob Policy Optimization (DoobPO) , a general framework for RL fine-tuning of diffusion policies (Section 3.1) and flow policies (Section 3.2) without evaluating exact ac-tion likelihoods. DoobPO supports a broad family of trust-region policy gradient methods, while Section 3.3 shows how both existing and proposed algorithms arise under the same framework. 

## 3.1 Diffusion Policy Optimization with DoobPO 

In this section, we introduce DoobPO for diffusion policies. We start from a base diffusion model with score network sϕ(x, t ), which induces a diffusion policy πϕ. Our goal is to optimize the policy objective (2.1) over parameters θ, starting from the pretrained initialization θ = ϕ.

Ratio Learning. We first learn a probability ratio that captures the policy improvement signal in-4duced by trust-region policy-gradient updates, including regularized Policy Mirror Descent [Zhan et al., 2023], Soft Actor-Critic [Haarnoja et al., 2018], PPO [Schulman et al., 2017], and GRPO [Shao et al., 2024]. Specifically, we introduce a lightweight ratio network rβ (o, a ) to approximate the intractable likelihood ratio rθ(o, a ) = πθ(a|o)/π old (a|o). We train rβ on trajectories collected under the behavior policy πold by optimizing a clipped reinforcement learning objective JRL . For example, under PPO, we use JRL = JPPO following (2.3), with rθ replaced by rβ . Intuitively, 

rβ (o, a ) > 1 upweights an action relative to πold , while rβ (o, a ) < 1 downweights it. The clip-ping term enforces a conservative trust-region-style update. In practice, we parameterize the ratio model as rβ (o, a ) = exp( erβ (o, a )) to ensure rβ (o, a ) > 0.

Doob’s Drift Matching. We now distill the policy improvement information encoded by the learned ratio rβ into the score model. We consider a diffusion policy, where the denoising SDE (2.5) is conditioned on the environment state o, and the score estimator takes the form sϕ(x, t, o ).This score model induces the behavior policy πold (·| o) as the terminal distribution of the denoising process. Given the learned ratio rβ (o, a ), we would like to update the policy toward the ratio-tilted distri-bution π(·| o) ∝ rβ (o, ·)πold (·| o). We realize this tilt by modifying the denoising dynamics with an additional drift term. We characterize this modification via Doob’s h-transform.

## 3.3 Unified Framework and Instantiations 

DoobPO is a unified framework with two design choices. The first is the generative policy class. The second is the policy update method that specifies the target policy improvement. Different choices along these two dimensions induce a broad family of diffusion/flow matching RL algo-rithms. Under this view, several prior methods arise as special cases of DoobPO. In particular, they cor-respond to policy updates that admit closed-form solutions. In these cases, the ratio-learning stage becomes unnecessary, and DoobPO reduces to a single reweighted drift matching step. # 5 Discussion and Conclusion 

In this work, we introduce Doob Policy Optimization (DoobPO) , a unified framework for RL fine-tuning of diffusion and flow policies. The framework supports a broad family of policy updates and avoids exact action-likelihood evaluation by separating policy improvement from generative policy learning. Theoretically, we justify DoobPO from two directions. First, we characterize the ratio-tilted diffusion process through Doob’s h-transform (Lemma 3.1). We then show that, for each fixed state, the gradient of the conditional drift matching objective is propor-tional to that of the ideal objective for both diffusion and flow policies (Theorems 3.1 and 3.2). Second, under the stated assumptions, the population minimizer of conditional drift matching in-duces a diffusion process that solves the corresponding endpoint-constrained stochastic optimal control problem (Proposition 3.1). The resulting process is the minimum-energy modification of the reference diffusion that realizes the prescribed tilted endpoint marginals. This provides a control-theoretic interpretation of how the desired policy update is transferred into generative dynamics. Experimentally, we evaluate DoobPO on MuJoCo benchmarks with both diffusion and flow policies and observe superior overall performance against strong baselines (Sections 4.2 and 4.3). More broadly, DoobPO provides a common interface between policy optimization and genera-tive model training. Policy updates with analytic ratios and updates represented by learned ratio models are accommodated within the same conditional drift matching framework (Section 3.3). This separates the choice of the policy update rule from the choice of the generative policy class. Nevertheless, our study mainly focuses on continuous generation, and the empirical evaluation is limited to MuJoCo continuous-control benchmarks and Robomimic manipulation tasks. The theoretical results characterize population objectives under the stated assumptions. An important direction for future work is to quantify how ratio estimation error and approximate drift matching affect the realized policy update. We also plan to extend the framework to discrete generation and evaluate its applicability to more complex policy learning settings. 14 Appendix

# B Related Works 

Diffusion Model and Diffusion Policy. Diffusion models [Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015, Ho et al., 2020] are probabilistic generative models that progressively transform noise into samples from a target distribution. They are closely connected to score-based generative modeling [Song and Er-mon, 2019, Song et al., 2021], which offers a complementary view based on learning the gradient field of the data distribution and unifies diffusion-based generation under the stochastic differen-tial equation framework. Owing to their strong expressiveness for complex, high-dimensional, and multimodal distributions, diffusion models have become increasingly attractive for control 16 and decision-making. In particular, diffusion policies parameterize action distributions with dif-fusion models and generate actions through iterative denoising, yielding richer policy classes than standard unimodal Gaussian actors. Prior work has shown strong empirical performance of dif-fusion policies in visuomotor control and robotic manipulation [Chi et al., 2025], as well as in goal-conditioned imitation learning and language- or play-conditioned skill learning [Reuss et al., 2023, Chen et al., 2023]. More recently, diffusion policies have been extended to online reinforcement learning. Meth-ods such as DPPO and related diffusion-policy RL approaches show that diffusion-based policy classes can be optimized effectively beyond pure imitation learning [Ren et al., 2024, Ma et al., 2025]. Our work builds on this line of research, but focuses on RL fine-tuning of diffusion policies in the likelihood-intractable setting. We compare against five recent diffusion-policy RL baselines: DIPO [Yang et al., 2023], DPPO [Ren et al., 2024], DPMD [Ma et al., 2025], DACER [Wang et al., 2024], and SDAC [Ma et al., 2025]. These baselines cover both off-policy and on-policy diffusion RL methods: DIPO, DPMD, DACER, and SDAC are off-policy, whereas DPPO is on-policy. DIPO adopts a two-stage strategy that first updates state-action particles using value gradients and then distills them into a diffu-sion policy. DPPO formulates policy optimization over a diffusion-augmented MDP and applies Proximal Policy Optimization to the resulting process. DPMD studies efficient online RL for dif-fusion policies through a policy-mirror-descent-style update with an analytic target ratio. DACER directly backpropagates value gradients through the reverse diffusion process and introduces en-tropy regularization to improve exploration. SDAC extends diffusion-policy optimization to the soft-actor-critic regime with an analytic-ratio-based update. Our work generalizes the analytic-ratio ideas underlying DPMD and SDAC into a unified likelihood-free framework, extending them from diffusion policies to flow matching policies and to a broader class of policy optimization methods, including PPO [Schulman et al., 2017] and GRPO [Shao et al., 2024]. 

Flow Matching Model and Flow Policy. Flow matching [Lipman et al., 2022, Liu et al., 2022] provides an alternative to diffusion models by learning a deterministic ODE transport between a noise prior and the data distribution. Compared to diffusion models, flow matching avoids sim-ulating stochastic dynamics during training and offers faster sampling through fewer integration steps. These properties have made flow matching models increasingly popular in decision-making applications. π0 [Black et al., 2024] and its successor π∗ 

> 0.5

[Intelligence et al., 2025] adopt flow matching as the generative backbone of vision-language-action models for general robot manipu-lation, demonstrating that flow policies can scale to diverse real-world tasks. However, the like-lihood of a flow policy is as intractable as that of a diffusion policy, since it requires solving an ODE and computing the associated log-determinant of the Jacobian. This intractability poses the same challenge for online RL fine-tuning: standard policy gradient methods that rely on likeli-hood ratios cannot be directly applied. Flow Q-Learning [Park et al., 2025] is an early attempt that sidesteps this issue by using Q-function guidance to steer the flow sampling process, analogous to Q-guided diffusion methods. For policy gradient approaches, several workarounds have been pro-17 posed. FPO [McAllister et al., 2025] is a PPO-style method that replaces explicit policy ratios with an implicit surrogate derived from the flow matching training loss, avoiding likelihood computa-tion entirely. ReinFlow [Zhang et al., 2025] converts a deterministic flow policy into a stochastic short-horizon Markov process via learned noise injection, enabling tractable stepwise likelihood computation for policy gradient updates. Flow-SDE [Chen et al., 2025] instead replaces the de-terministic flow ODE with an analytically constructed stochastic differential equation, yielding explicit transition probabilities suitable for policy gradient estimation. All these methods require some form of likelihood evaluation or surrogate at the denoising steps; in contrast, our approach bypasses this requirement entirely by learning a lightweight ratio network that directly estimates the probability ratio needed for policy optimization.

## G.2 Unbiased Ratio Regularizer 

The ratio regularizer Eo∼D 

h Ea′∼πold (·| o)[rβ (o, a ′)] − 12i

contains a quadratic moment term, whose naive Monte Carlo estimate is biased. To obtain an unbiased estimator, we use double sam-pling. For each sampled state o, two independent action batches are drawn from πold (·| o), yielding two conditionally independent estimates bg1(o) and bg2(o) of g(o) := Ea′∼πold (·| o)[rβ (o, a ′)] − 1. We then replace g(o)2 with bg1(o)bg2(o), which satisfies E[bg1(o)bg2(o)|o] = g(o)2.

## G.3 Ratio Regularizer in the Off-Policy Setting 

In the on-policy variant, the ratio centering regularizer enforces Ea′∼πold (·| o)[rβ (o, a ′)] ≈ 1 by sampling actions from the frozen rollout policy, which corresponds exactly to the normalization constraint of the density ratio. In the off-policy variant with a replay buffer, there is no single well-defined behavior policy associated with each stored transition, since the buffer aggregates experience from many past policy versions. We therefore estimate the centering constraint by sampling a′ from the current actor at the same state o. This ensures that the learned ratio re-mains well-calibrated within the support of the evolving policy, effectively regularizing rβ against degenerate solutions regardless of the age of the replay sample. Empirically, this regularizer is applied with a small coefficient ( λ = 0 .01 ) and we observe that it is sufficient to stabilize ratio learning throughout training. 28 G.4 Computation and Memory Cost
