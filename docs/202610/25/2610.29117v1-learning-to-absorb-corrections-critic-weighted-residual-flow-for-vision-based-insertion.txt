# Learning to Absorb Corrections: Critic-Weighted Residual Flow for Vision-Based Insertion 

Yiqing Yuan 1, Zhi Li 2, Jinrong Yang 2, Mingshan Sun 2,Mei Yu Zhi 2, Yong Zhao 2, Liangliang Ren 2, and Hui Cheng 1,∗

Abstract — Vision-based insertion requires correcting align-ment errors while retaining useful behavior learned from demonstrations. We present ACT–Flow, an online learning framework that couples an adaptive Action Chunking Trans-former (ACT) with a conditional residual flow policy. Follow-ing anchor-guided warmup, ACT absorbs successful executed actions through behavioral cloning, while residual targets are recomputed relative to the updated prior. A chunk-level critic learns from successful and failed interactions and evaluates final composed actions. Its estimates weight generated residual candidates for flow matching, complemented by supervision from successful real executions. Candidate scoring is confined to training; deployment uses RGB observations and samples one residual per action chunk. We evaluate the framework on power-plug, USB, and key insertion, together with cable routing and needle threading. Results show a five-task mean success rate of 66% at 20,000 post-initialization interaction steps, exceeding the strongest baseline by 18 percentage points at this checkpoint. Evaluations at matched checkpoints with the residual disabled show ACT’s mean success increasing from 16% to 76%. Component ablations further support the complementary roles of behavior absorption, value weighting, and successful-action supervision. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Vision-based insertion requires a robot to correct align-ment errors during execution. Inserting a power plug, USB connector, or key can fail when small position or orientation errors persist, or when the target moves after the initial approach. Demonstration-trained policies, including Action Chunking Transformer (ACT) [1] and Diffusion Policy [2], provide effective action priors. However, limited demon-strations cannot cover every state encountered during de-ployment, and deviations from the training distribution can compound [3]. This motivates a policy that can both correct execution errors and incorporate successful corrections into its learned behavior. Existing methods obtain corrective guidance from demon-strations or human intervention. DOME [4], MILES [5], and AutoSERL [6] use demonstrations to guide visual alignment, recovery, or automatic intervention. HIL-SERL [7] incorpo-rates human corrections into reinforcement learning (RL), while CR-DAgger [8] learns residual policies from compliant corrections. Demonstration-guidance mechanisms depend on available reference behavior, while human corrections require operator effort. Beyond obtaining guidance, a further ques-tion is how the robot should represent corrections and retain the useful behavior they produce.

Residual adaptation provides a natural representation: a base policy supplies nominal behavior and an additional pol-icy learns corrections [8]–[11]. FlowDAgger [12] similarly steers a frozen generative policy through its latent variables. A frozen prior provides a stable basis for adaptation, but successful corrections remain in the additional module rather than becoming part of the prior itself. The central question is: how can a robot learn effective insertion corrections from interaction and absorb successful corrected behavior into an improving action prior? 

Joint prior–residual learning raises two issues. Updat-ing the prior changes the residual needed to reproduce a successful action, making historical labels inconsistent. Critic estimates rank corrections but cannot replace observed successful actions as supervision. Learning must combine these signals while tracking the evolving prior. Retaining successful executed actions provides a stable reference: their decomposition can change as the prior improves, while their role as observed targets is preserved. We present ACT–Flow, an online framework for vision-based insertion that coordinates an adaptive ACT prior with a conditional residual flow policy. Following anchor-guided demonstration and correction warmup, successful executed actions update ACT through behavioral cloning (BC), while residual targets are recomputed against the updated ACT to preserve consistent supervision. To combine value guidance with observed successful behavior, residual learning couples critic-weighted flow matching (FM) [13] on generated can-didates with FM on successful executions. The critic learns from both outcomes and scores composed actions. We evaluate three insertion tasks—power-plug, USB, and arXiv:2610.29117v1 [cs.RO] 21 Oct 2026 key insertion—plus cable routing and needle threading. Comparisons and ablations examine adaptation, behavior absorption, and residual supervision. Our contributions are:  

> •

An online learning framework for vision-based insertion connecting an ACT prior and residual flow policy through anchor-guided initialization, online correction, and successful-experience feedback.  

> •

A behavior-absorption mechanism that updates ACT from successful actions and relabels residual targets under the current prior, keeping correction supervision consistent as the base policy changes.  

> •

Critic-weighted residual flow learning that combines value-weighted fitting of generated corrections with FM supervision from successful executed actions.  

> •

Comparisons on three real-world insertion tasks and two additional manipulation tasks, supported by component ablations and diagnostics of base-policy improvement, residual contribution, and critic ranking quality. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Demonstrations and Corrective Interaction 

DAgger [3] aggregates corrective supervision, while CR-DAgger [8] collects compliant corrections. MILES [5] aug-ments a reference demonstration through self-supervised recovery, and HD-Space [14] organizes collection within overlapping atomic state–action spaces. Simulation can also supply demonstrations: Li et al. [15] use appearance-noise annealing to shape optimization for sim-to-real conveyor ma-nipulation. SERL [16] supports practical real-world RL; HIL-SERL [7] incorporates human corrections, while AWAC [17] and RLPD [18] exploit prior data for online learning. AutoSERL [6] automates demonstration-guided intervention and recovery, and Focus-Then-Contact (FTC) [10] combines residual RL with affordance guidance and timed human intervention. Visual task conditioning offers a complementary route to robust imitation. Vision-instructed pre-training (VIP) [19] uses future images and sparse point flows to specify targets; SAM2Grasp [20] combines object prompts with temporal tracking to disambiguate grasp trajectories. These approaches clarify task targets, while our focus is learning and absorbing corrections during interaction. In our setting, corrective experience must support both prior improvement and residual learning. We use HD-Space’s collection principle for warmup, successful actions to update ACT, and both outcomes to train the residual-learning critic. 

B. Residual Adaptation and Prior Updating 

Residual RL [9] adds learned corrections to a nominal controller. Policy Decorator [21] refines a frozen imitation policy and evaluates composed actions. CR-DAgger [8] learns residual motions and forces from compliant supervi-sion; FTC [10] and HiL-ResRL [11] combine frozen priors with human-guided residual RL. FlowDAgger [12] steers a frozen generative policy through latent-space corrections. In contrast, RL-100 directly fine-tunes an imitation-pretrained diffusion policy through offline and online RL [22]. A frozen prior provides a stable residual reference but does not absorb corrections. Updating the prior permits behavior absorption, yet changes the residual required to reproduce a previously successful action. We retain executed actions as stable targets, update ACT from successful experience, and recompute residual targets against the current ACT. 

C. Value-Guided Generative Policy Learning 

Advantage-weighted regression [23] uses value-derived weights for supervised policy fitting. FM [13] provides a generative regression objective; reward-weighted fine-tuning [24] favors high-reward samples while regularizing deviation from a reference model. Flow Q-Learning [25] trains a separate one-step actor with value optimization and distillation from a behavior-cloned flow policy. PACT [26] calibrates credit assignment using intervention-derived pref-erences, while Max-Q [27] uses chunk-value regression to select between policy and replay actions for imitation. Under an evolving prior, critic rankings provide value guidance but cannot replace successful-action targets. We combine critic-weighted FM on generated candidates with successful-execution FM, both referenced to the current ACT. III. P ROBLEM FORMULATION

A. Anchor Warmup and Behavior Absorption 

Anchor warmup initializes the policy; online updates then alternate behavior absorption and residual relabeling.

E XPERIMENTS 

We evaluate ACT–Flow on five real-world tasks, including three insertion tasks, to examine online learning efficiency, successful-behavior absorption, and the value of critic-guided residual learning. Comparisons assess overall performance, while ablations and paired diagnostics explain the contribu-tion of each component. 

A. Environmental Setup 

Platform and tasks: Figure 3 shows the six-axis arm, gripper, task-specific flanges, and RealSense D405 close-view camera. Work and ring lights illuminate the table and contact region. Collection controllers serve the baselines; ACT–Flow uses anchor-guided warmup (Sec. IV-A). Power-plug, USB, and key insertion require connector– socket or key–keyway alignment. Cable routing places a de-formable cable through clips; needle threading passes flexible thread through an eye. Task completion defines success. 

Initialization and training: The comparison protocol allocates 30 episodes to initialization and defines online step zero after initialization training. Our warmup collection follows HD-Space [14] and the staged procedure in Sec. IV-A. Anchor poses are manually specified relative to the socket or cable fixtures before warmup and remain fixed in their respective local frames. The learning curves then cover up to 

120 k real online environment steps. We use chunk horizon 

H = 4 , flow integration budget K = 10 , N = 8 training candidates, λ = 1 , two critic heads, γ = 0 .99 , and target update rate 0.005 . Initial values are τ = 1 , β = 1 , and equal anchor/online-success sampling for ACT. Candidate generation and scoring use model computation; only actual robot execution contributes online environment steps. 

Baselines: We compare with five methods. MILES [5] augments a demonstration with self-supervised recovery tra-jectories. AutoSERL [6] constructs automatic intervention and recovery from demonstration-derived guidance. HIL-SERL [7] incorporates human corrections into real-world RL. CR-DAgger [8] learns compliant residual corrections from human supervision. FTC [10] combines affordance-guided residual RL with timed human intervention. All methods are evaluated on the same tasks. Online environment steps are counted after initialization. 

Evaluation metrics: At each checkpoint, we evaluate 100 episodes per task per training seed, with success judged by humans. Main learning curves report means and standard deviations (SDs) of success rate (SR) over three training seeds. Lines connect evaluated checkpoints. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We presented ACT–Flow for vision-based insertion. Suc-cessful executions improve ACT, while residual relabel-ing maintains supervision under the evolving prior. Critic-weighted candidate fitting and successful-action FM jointly learn corrections. Five real-world tasks demonstrate efficient early adaptation and behavior absorption, with residuals retaining additional benefits. The resulting policy both learns corrective behavior and incorporates successful experience into its action prior. The framework requires tasks that admit subtask decom-position with feasible anchor demonstrations and correction warmup.
