ABSTRACT
Motion Diffusion Models (MDMs) can generate diverse and visually appealing
human motion; yet, the resulting trajectories frequently violate physical constraints,
limiting their downstream use in simulation and control. We present PhysibleMDM,
a framework that promotes physical feasibility of MDM output by optimizing at
inference time the diffusion noise without retraining the generative model. We
introduce a set of surrogate feasibility constraints that can be evaluated without full
trajectory simulation. These constraints combine kinematic checks (e.g., ground
penetration, slip, and self-collision) with dynamic feasibility tests computed via
an inverse-dynamics inner loop formulated as a cone program. We solve the
resulting black-box noise optimization problem using the Separable Covariance
Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (Sep-CMA-ES), thereby avoiding reliance
on computationally expensive and discontinuous gradients. An autoregressive
scheme then chains optimized segments to synthesize long motion sequences.
Across 100 motion styles on an SMPL humanoid, PhysibleMDM reduces mean
ground penetration, self-collision penetration, and ground slip by 75%, 92%, and
74%, respectively, relative to unoptimized diffusion output. Across 11 sequences
for a custom bipedal robot, it achieves median reductions of 89.5% in per-sequence
mean acceleration-reconstruction error and 79.5% in the mean inverse-dynamics
residual.

INTRODUCTION
Motion Diffusion Models (MDMs) (Tevet et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2023; Rempe
et al., 2026) currently achieve state-of-the-art performance in synthesizing human-like motion for
physics-based characters and robots. These generators are typically trained on large corpora of
human motion capture data and can be conditioned on various signals, including text, style, goals,
and semantic tasks (Dabral et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024a; Huang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026).
The generated motion commonly serves as a reference for Reinforcement Learning (RL)-based
trackers (Peng et al., 2018; Luo et al., 2023; Serifi et al., 2024b; Liao et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2026).
Errors can arise at multiple stages of this pipeline. In particular, generated motions may exhibit
physical artifacts that hinder their execution. Kinematic artifacts include joint-limit violations (Lee
et al., 2025), self-collisions (Khazoom et al., 2022), ground penetration (Yuan et al., 2023), undesired
ground slip (Pan et al., 2025), and foot placement in non-traversable regions (Nguyen et al., 2016).
Dynamic artifacts include motions requiring joint torques beyond actuator limits (Herzog et al., 2014)
or contact forces outside friction cones (Grandia et al., 2021), as well as trajectories inconsistent with
the dynamics of unactuated coordinates, such as the floating base of a legged robot (Herzog et al.,
2014; Grizzle et al., 2014). These artifacts degrade downstream tracking performance (Ara´ujo et al.,
2026; Zhang et al., 2026), as infeasible reference motions cannot be reliably tracked.

Several methods mitigate these artifacts before or during training, for example, by learning from
motions tracked in physics simulation (Wu et al., 2025; Watanabe et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025), or
fine-tuning the generator with physics-informed objectives (Serifi et al., 2024a; Zhang et al., 2026).
While effective in practice, these approaches can be resource-intensive, particularly when generative
models and tracking policies are retrained across refinement iterations, and they do not inherently
guarantee that all samples from the final diffusion model satisfy the desired physical constraints.
Inference-time optimization incorporates explicit objectives and constraints during generation without
retraining the diffusion model. Common approaches steer intermediate denoising states through
gradient guidance (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Karunratanakul et al., 2023) or projection onto feasible
sets (Yuan et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2025; Watanabe et al., 2026). Noise optimization offers an alter-
native in which objectives and constraints are evaluated on fully denoised motions (Karunratanakul
et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025). Gradient-based noise optimization requires costly backpropagation
through both the diffusion sampler and the physical-feasibility evaluation. Contact-rich motion
further introduces non-smooth objectives and constraints for which gradients may be poorly informa-
tive (Antonova et al., 2023; Paulus et al., 2026). Sampling-based optimization has shown promise
for optimal control of contact-rich legged locomotion (Xue et al., 2025; Alvarez-Padilla et al., 2025;
Schramm et al., 2026). Recent work also applies sampling-based optimization to diffusion noise
for image generation and protein design (Kim et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2026), but does not address
the kinematic and dynamic feasibility requirements of robotic motion. Efficient gradient-free opti-
mization of initial and posterior diffusion noise for these requirements, while retaining characteristic
motion style, remains unexplored.
To address this gap, we propose PhysibleMDM, a gradient-free method that jointly optimizes
initial and posterior diffusion noise at inference time. Parallel forward passes through the frozen
model generate a population of motions for evaluating task objectives, kinematic constraints, and
constrained inverse-dynamics residuals. These evaluations guide Separable Covariance Matrix
Adaptation Evolution Strategy (Sep-CMA-ES) (Ros & Hansen, 2008), whose diagonal covariance
supports high-dimensional search, in iteratively refining the noise population. Noise standardization
regularizes noise toward its training-time statistics to help limit deviations from the learned motion
distribution. An autoregressive scheme chains optimized segments into long motion sequences.
In summary, the contributions of this paper are:
• A physical-feasibility formulation for MDMs that combines kinematic constraints with con-
strained inverse-dynamics residuals, enabling inference-time refinement without a tracking
controller or full trajectory simulation.
• A scalable sampling-based method for high-dimensional noise optimization in MDMs
(realized via Sep-CMA-ES), evaluating non-smooth objectives and constraints on fully
denoised motions without costly gradient computation.
• A diffusion-noise optimization strategy, applicable to any pretrained MDM without
architecture-specific changes, that combines joint initial and posterior noise optimization
with noise standardization, balancing optimization flexibility against deviations from the
model’s learned distribution and supporting motion-style retention in MDMs.

RELATED WORK
Inference-Time Optimization
Guidance and projection steer diffusion sampling by modifying
intermediate denoising states or their clean-sample estimates (Karunratanakul et al., 2023; Christopher
et al., 2024; Bodmer et al., 2026). Noise optimization instead evaluates objectives on fully denoised
samples and adjusts the stochastic inputs, using gradients (Karunratanakul et al., 2024; Zhao et al.,
2025) or sampling-based updates (Kim et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2026). The latter support non-
differentiable objectives without backpropagating through the denoising process. Relative to these
sampling-based methods, our approach jointly optimizes initial latent and posterior noise with Sep-
CMA-ES, whose diagonal covariance yields linear internal time and memory scaling with the number
of noise variables (Ros & Hansen, 2008), making it suitable for this high-dimensional search.
Physical Feasibility of Generated Motion
Existing methods incorporate physics at different stages
of motion generation. Before training, motion data can be refined through kinematic-constraint-aware
retargeting (Yang et al., 2026) or collected through simulated tracking, with the latter sometimes
repeated as the generator and tracker improve over successive training rounds (Watanabe et al., 2025;
Xu et al., 2025). Physical objectives can also guide learning directly through dynamics-consistency
losses (Serifi et al., 2024a; Xing et al., 2026) or preference-based post-training (Zhang et al., 2026).
Other approaches introduce physics during generation: PhysDiff projects intermediate denoising
states through simulated execution (Yuan et al., 2023), while CLoSD alternates motion planning and
execution (Tevet et al., 2025). Alongside this choice of when to incorporate physics, methods differ in
what they predict. Whereas most motion diffusion models generate kinematic state trajectories, joint
state–action models also generate controls, learning both from trajectories collected with tracking
controllers (Huang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). However, incorporating physics before or during
training does not ensure feasibility of individual generated samples, particularly under distribution
shift. Predicting controls alongside states does not remove this limitation, since the predicted states
may differ from the motion produced by executing those controls. Furthermore, approaches that use
simulated tracking depend on a trained tracking controller. To address these limitations, our method
evaluates kinematic constraints and constrained inverse-dynamics residuals on each fully generated
motion and optimizes diffusion noise at inference time, requiring neither generator retraining nor
tracking-controller rollouts.

OVERVIEW
PhysibleMDM refines generated motion at inference time by optimizing diffusion noise under
task objectives and physical-feasibility penalties (Fig. 1). Starting from standard-normal noise, we
standardize each candidate, generate a complete motion through the frozen MDM, and evaluate its
kinematic violations and constrained inverse-dynamics residuals. Batched Sep-CMA-ES (Ros &
Hansen, 2008) updates the candidate population over a fixed iteration budget. We then commit the
first part of the best motion and repeat autoregressively, conditioning on the preceding optimized
motion and encouraging consistency between overlapping predictions.

DIFFUSION NOISE OPTIMIZATION
We jointly optimize initial and posterior diffusion noise to provide additional control over the denois-
ing trajectory, increasing flexibility to reduce constraint violations and improve task performance. To
help preserve motion style, we standardize the noise produced by the optimizer to have zero mean
and unit variance, matching the corresponding moments of the model’s standard-normal sampling
distribution. This regularizes the search to help limit deviations from the learned motion distribution.

6.3
DYNAMIC FEASIBILITY CONSTRAINTS
Dynamic feasibility requires existence of joint torques τ and contact forces λ that realize the
prescribed motion under three conditions. Underactuated dynamics: the motion must satisfy the
equations of motion for the unactuated coordinates, including the floating base; joint torques cannot
directly compensate for an imbalance in these equations. Actuation limits: joint torques must stay
within actuator bounds. Contact limits: normal forces must be nonnegative (λ⊥≥0), and tangential
forces must lie within the friction cone for the assumed sticking mode; no-slip kinematics are checked
separately. Admissible torques and contact forces alone therefore do not establish that the prescribed
acceleration is realizable.

EXPERIMENTS
8.1
EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
We evaluate the Skinned Multi-Person Linear (SMPL) humanoid (Loper et al., 2015) and a cus-
tom bipedal robot using MDMs based on Conditional Autoregressive Motion Diffusion Model
(CAMDM) (Chen et al., 2024a), trained on retargeted 100STYLE motions (Mason et al., 2022).
Generation is conditioned on past motion, a commanded root trajectory, and a style label, with eight
6


denoising steps and autoregressive execution of optimized segments. We evaluate task tracking
and physical feasibility from decoded configurations, forward kinematics, and smoothed motion
derivatives; evaluation in the dynamic setting additionally uses constrained ID. Joint initial- and
posterior-noise optimization uses 78,840 scalar optimization parameters per motion horizon for
SMPL and 103,680 for the bipedal robot in the experimental implementation (Appendix E.2). Full
implementation details appear in Appendices B–F.
8.2
BASELINES
We compare inference-time refinements of a frozen generator without requiring a pretrained track-
ing controller. Unoptimized generation establishes the starting quality, and Noise Best-of-N tests
independent sampling against adaptive search. Motion GD and Motion Sep-CMA-ES directly refine
generated trajectories, motivated by constraint-aware retargeting (Yang et al., 2026). Guided Diffu-
sion represents objective guidance during denoising (Karunratanakul et al., 2023), while Noise GD
represents gradient-based diffusion-noise optimization (Karunratanakul et al., 2024). We include
Noise ZeNO (Kim et al., 2026) as a prior sampling-based noise-optimization method, adapting its
zeroth-order updates to joint initial and posterior noise for kinematic feasibility. We adapt these
technique families to our common objectives and constraints. Two component ablations, Lat. Noise
Sep-CMA-ES and Noise Sep-CMA-ES (NNS), test posterior-noise optimization and noise standard-
ization, respectively. The latent-only variant disables posterior-noise optimization, holding sampled
posterior noise fixed across candidates and iterations within each horizon. NNS denotes no noise
standardization: the normalization in Eq. (3) is omitted (implementations in Appendix E.5).
8.3
PHYSICAL FEASIBILITY OPTIMIZATION SETTINGS
We consider two feasibility optimization settings: kinematic feasibility optimization, which penal-
izes contact-pattern deviations, ground slip, and self-collision; and dynamic feasibility optimization,
which penalizes acceleration-reconstruction error, ground slip, and self-collision. Both settings also
include velocity tracking and autoregressive motion consistency objectives. The kinematic setting is
comparatively inexpensive but requires contact-pattern preservation to discourage floating, whereas
the dynamic setting evaluates whether accelerations can be supported under actuator bounds and
contact-force cones, at the additional cost of dynamics evaluation and inner ID solves. Within each
setting, methods share objective & constraint weights (budgets and weights: Appendix F.3; active
terms: Tab. 4).
8.4
EVALUATION METRICS
We measure ground penetration, slip, self-collision, acceleration-reconstruction error, and the ID
force residual, alongside task tracking, autoregressive consistency, and runtime per iteration and
motion horizon. Smoothness is measured by Log Dimensionless Jerk (LDJ) (Balasubramanian et al.,
2015), which normalizes jerk energy separately for root translation, root rotation, and joints before
averaging and taking the negative logarithm; higher is smoother. For SMPL, a pretrained style
classifier provides style accuracy and overall/per-style Fr´echet Inception Distance (FID) (Chen et al.,
2024a).
Framewise metrics pool committed frames over time and, where applicable, styles. Tables report

TRAINING RL CONTROLLER ON OPTIMIZED VS. UNOPTIMIZED MOTION
DeepMimic-style policies (Peng et al., 2018) trained on the optimized bipedal two-foot jump reduce
body position, body orientation, and joint position tracking errors by 16.4%, 11.9%, and 7.4%, respec-
tively, relative to training on the unoptimized reference. Root position and orientation errors increase
by 39.6% and 41.3%, revealing a tradeoff between root-relative body tracking and accumulated global
root-pose drift. Appendices F.7 and H.4 detail the setup and results, respectively.
9
CONCLUSION
Physible refines frozen motion diffusion models through batched, gradient-free optimization of initial
and posterior noise, combining kinematic penalties with constrained inverse-dynamics evaluation.
Across 100 motion styles on SMPL, it reduces mean ground penetration, self-collision penetration, and
slip by 75%, 92%, and 74% relative to unoptimized generation. Across 11 bipedal robot sequences,
median reductions in per-sequence mean acceleration-reconstruction error and inverse-dynamics
residual are 89.5% and 79.5%, respectively, supporting inference-time noise optimization as a means
of improving physical feasibility without retraining the generator or requiring tracking-controller
rollouts.
9


Limitations & Future Work
Our soft penalties do not guarantee strict physical feasibility, and
optimized noise can drift from the learned motion distribution and degrade style. Noise standard-
ization favors style preservation in this tradeoff at the cost of optimization expressivity, a restriction
partly alleviated by jointly optimizing initial and posterior noise rather than initial latent noise alone.
Evaluation is limited to bipedal embodiments with assumed dynamics and sticking contacts, while re-
peated feasibility evaluations and inverse-dynamics solves can be costly for real-time use. Appendix I
expands on limitations and future work.

A
EXTENDED RELATED WORK
A.1
MOTION DIFFUSION MODELS
Diffusion models (Ho et al., 2020) serve as strong generative priors for humanoid motion, enabling
high-quality synthesis under rich conditioning (Tevet et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024a; Dabral et al.,
2023) and interactive control via guided diffusion (Karunratanakul et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2025;
Wu et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2026). Motion diffusion models commonly generate kinematic trajectories
for a downstream tracking controller (Serifi et al., 2024a; Yuan et al., 2023; Truong et al., 2024;
Watanabe et al., 2025; Tevet et al., 2025). Diffusion policies instead generate action sequences (Chi
et al., 2025), while joint state–action diffusion models predict both motion and control, allowing
objectives on predicted states to guide action generation (Huang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). While
these methods generate visually compelling humanoid motions, ensuring strict kinematic validity and
contact-rich dynamical feasibility remains a core challenge. Our method optimizes the stochastic
inputs of a kinematic motion generator and evaluates physical feasibility before downstream tracking.
A.2
INFERENCE-TIME OPTIMIZATION OF DIFFUSION MODELS
A major practical advantage of diffusion models is that sampling can be modified at inference time to
satisfy additional objectives or constraints without retraining the base model. A common approach is
gradient-based guidance, which biases the denoising process using gradients of an auxiliary signal.
Building on classifier guidance (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021), prior work in robotics has explored
constraint-aware guidance for kinematic constraints (Ma et al., 2025; Cho et al., 2026) as well as
Lyapunov-based objectives to ensure safety and stability (Cheng et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025).
Gradient-based guidance requires differentiable objectives, while sampling-based guidance can also
incorporate non-differentiable constraint evaluations (Hui et al., 2026). Both modify the intermediate
sampling dynamics and can bias samples away from the model distribution.
Alternatively, projection methods enforce constraints by mapping intermediate denoising states
or their clean-sample estimates onto feasible sets (Christopher et al., 2024; Zampini et al., 2025).
Related strategies have also been explored in flow-matching frameworks, including exact linear spatial
constraints for motion control (Li et al., 2026; Utkarsh et al., 2025; Watanabe et al., 2026). While
these approaches can be effective when well-defined projection operators exist, their success depends
on the informativeness of intermediate sample estimates and the degree to which the projection
conflicts with the learned prior.
More recently, noise optimization has emerged, which directly optimizes the initial diffusion noise
and, for stochastic samplers, noise injected along the sampling trajectory, to improve the generated
output under a given objective (Chen et al., 2024b; Karunratanakul et al., 2024; Eyring et al., 2024;
Uehara et al., 2025; Ben-Hamu et al., 2024; Pu & Kong, 2026). This approach evaluates objectives
on generated outputs and can regularize the noise to limit deviations from the prior. Gradient-based
approaches update the noise by backpropagating through the generator (Ben-Hamu et al., 2024;
Karunratanakul et al., 2024; Pu & Kong, 2026; Zhao et al., 2025). Recent work introduces sampling-
based noise updates, including evolutionary search, using only objective evaluations (Kim et al.,
2026; Tan et al., 2026). For example, ZeNO uses a path-integral update of the latent noise, whereas
our Sep-CMA-ES strategy adapts a diagonal search covariance and step size using ranked candidates
for both latent and posterior noise.
Guidance and projection also require choosing how strongly constraints influence each diffusion step:
intermediate trajectories may give incomplete feasibility information, while strong corrections can
conflict with the learned prior and compromise motion style. Noise optimization instead evaluates
fully denoised outputs and permits regularization of noise statistics (Ben-Hamu et al., 2024; Karun-
ratanakul et al., 2024; Su & Kasai, 2025; Eyring et al., 2025). Gradient-based noise updates require
backpropagation through both the denoising graph and the objective and constraint evaluations. This
can be expensive, and contact decisions introduce non-smoothness that can make gradients poorly
informative (Antonova et al., 2023; Paulus et al., 2026).
Efficient gradient-free optimization of high-dimensional (motion) diffusion model noise for non-
smooth objectives and constraints remains underexplored. We address this gap by jointly optimizing
initial and posterior noise with Sep-CMA-ES.

A.3
PHYSICAL FEASIBILITY OF GENERATED ROBOTIC CHARACTER MOTION
Physically infeasible references hinder downstream motion tracking and can distort the style and
semantics of generated motion (Ara´ujo et al., 2026). Approaches to improve feasibility generally
focus on (i) enhancing the training data and (ii) enforcing constraints at inference-time during motion
generation.
Physics-Informed Training
A common strategy is to retarget motion capture data to new mor-
phologies while enforcing kinematic and contact constraints via constraint-aware optimization (Luo
et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023; Ze et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2025;
Dhedin et al., 2026). SimDiff (Watanabe et al., 2025) constructs simulator-augmented motion data
using a pretrained tracking policy and trains adapters around a frozen diffusion backbone, enabling
inference without repeated simulator calls. PARC (Xu et al., 2025) instead iteratively trains a motion
generator and tracking controller: the generator proposes motions, the tracker executes them in
simulation, and successful executions augment the dataset for subsequent training rounds. Both
use physics-based execution to improve the training pipeline. Joint state–action diffusion models,
such as Diffuse-CLoC and UniPhys (Huang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025), similarly use tracking
controllers to collect simulated state trajectories and associated actions for training. They learn to
predict motion and control together. InterPhys trains motion generation for consistency with predicted
torques and contact parameters supervised by offline dynamics fitting (Xing et al., 2026). SafeFlow
distills samples guided by kinematic penalties on joint-limit violations, self-collisions, and joint
and center-of-mass velocities and accelerations (Cho et al., 2026). Preference-based post-training
methods such as PhysMoDPO (Zhang et al., 2026) also rely on a physics-based tracking controller.
Physics-based corrections can introduce style drift (Zhang et al., 2026), and incorporating physics into
the training pipeline does not guarantee feasible generation at inference time. For joint state–action
models, this limitation also manifests as potential disagreement between predicted states and the mo-
tion produced by executing the generated actions, particularly under out-of-distribution conditioning.
Our refinement evaluates explicit constraints on generated motion at inference time without training
additional generator components or requiring a tracking controller.
Motion Diffusion Model Optimization
Finally, physical feasibility of MDMs can be enforced
at inference time by modifying diffusion sampling. Gradient-based guidance steers sampling using
differentiable penalties for kinematic constraints (Cho et al., 2026). PhysDiff (Yuan et al., 2023)
injects physics through intermittent simulator projection: at selected denoising steps, the partially
denoised motion is tracked in simulation and denoising resumes from the resulting closed-loop
trajectory. CLoSD (Tevet et al., 2025) adopts a related approach in an autoregressive setting, where
short motion segments are repeatedly planned and executed. The motion actually executed by the
simulated character becomes the prefix for the next diffusion plan, closing the loop through the
tracking controller; our autoregressive procedure instead conditions on the preceding optimized
motion. While both PhysDiff and CLoSD use physics-based tracking to project kinematic into
dynamically consistent motions, PhysDiff applies projection within the diffusion trajectory, whereas
CLoSD applies it between successive planning steps.
Improving both kinematic and contact-rich dynamical feasibility under non-smooth constraints while
preserving motion quality, style, and diversity remains challenging, particularly without retraining
the generator or relying on a physics-based tracking controller. Our approach combines gradient-free
noise optimization with kinematic penalties and constrained inverse-dynamics evaluations on fully
generated motions.

B
MOTION DIFFUSION MODEL: DATA, TRAINING, AND GENERATION
B.1
MOTION DATA AND PREPROCESSING
We train on the 100STYLE dataset (Mason et al., 2022), which contains locomotion sequences
performed in 100 distinct styles. The human motion capture is retargeted to the SMPL skeleton and
subsequently to the bipedal robot.
This pipeline follows SMooDi (Zhong et al., 2024), using Rokoko (Rokoko Electronics, 2026) to
map the source motion capture onto the SMPL skeleton and obtain retargeted motions for all 100
styles. SMPL’s human-like proportions allow this mapping to be performed kinematically.
The bipedal robot differs substantially from the human body in size, proportions, and kinematic
structure. We therefore retarget the SMPL motions using ReActor (M¨uller et al., 2026). The simulated
retargeting controller tracks scaled SMPL motion. A fixed correspondence maps the pelvis, hips,
knees, feet, shoulders, elbows, neck, and head between the two embodiments. The reference motion
is scaled by 0.5; the controller tracks root pose, root velocity, and corresponding body orientations,
with auxiliary root forces and torques penalized in the retargeting reward. The resulting robot-motion
files provide training data for the bipedal robot. This retargeting stage is distinct from the downstream
tracking comparison in Appendix F.7.
The motion loader resamples the SMPL and bipedal robot clips to 30 Hz and 50 Hz, respectively. It
constructs every contiguous window of P + F frames with stride one and omits clips shorter than
this window. The past and future parts are expressed in the heading frame associated with the history.
Position features retain metric units; each three-component position occupies a six-entry feature
block with three zero-padding entries. Root, joint, and body orientations use the 6D representation
described below. Style labels are obtained from the motion-file style identifiers.
B.2
MOTION REPRESENTATION, ARCHITECTURE, AND CONDITIONING
Our MDM builds on the CAMDM transformer architecture (Chen et al., 2024a) with latent width
256 (4 layers, 4 heads, feed-forward size 1024, dropout 0.2) and predicts motions over a horizon of
F = 20 frames at 30 Hz for the SMPL humanoid and F = 30 at 50 Hz for the bipedal robot. It is
trained on the 100STYLE dataset (Mason et al., 2022). The model is conditioned on (i) the past-motion
window Mpst,t, with P = 10 frames for SMPL and P = 15 for the bipedal robot (expressed in
the current heading frame Ht), (ii) a desired root pose sequence Cfut,t in the same frame derived
from velocity commands (local x–y translational velocity and yaw rate), (iii) a discrete motion-style
label ℓsyl, and (iv) scalar attention biases bpst, bsyl that modulate the influence of past-motion and
style conditioning. We collect these inputs as ct = (Mpst,t, Cfut,t, ℓsyl, bpst, bsyl). Conditioning is
encoded in a tokenized representation: at each denoising step, we embed a timestep token, a global
style/condition token, and per-frame tokens for the commanded root trajectory and the past-motion
context, concatenate them with the per-frame future-motion tokens to be denoised, add positional
encodings, and process the resulting sequence with a Transformer encoder; the scalar conditioning
weights enter as learned attention-mask biases on the corresponding token blocks, and Classifier-Free
Guidance (CFG) is implemented by randomly masking the past-motion condition during training
(mask probability 0.15).
Orientations use the continuous 6D rotation representation (Zhou et al., 2019). The conversion from
generated motion features to physical robot configurations is described in Appendix C.1.
The two models share the transformer dimensions above. SMPL uses J = 23 and nb = 24, whereas
the bipedal robot uses J = 20 and nb = 21. Consequently, nenc = 438 for SMPL and nenc = 384
for the bipedal robot. Although the bipedal robot has revolute joints, the denoiser represents each
joint orientation with six features.

NUMERICAL IMPLEMENTATION OF BATCHED INVERSE DYNAMICS
D.1
SOLVER FORMULATION AND NUMERICAL CONDITIONING
The differentiable floating-base dynamics in Eq. (16) are implemented in PyTorch using a custom
version of adam (Nori et al., 2015; L’Erario et al., 2026) that supports spherical joints. Each
spherical joint has four quaternion position coordinates and three angular-velocity coordinates; the
implementation accounts for these different position and velocity dimensions in the rigid-body
dynamics.
In the ID formulation, we assume that Cℓ= Cstick
ℓ
, Cslide
ℓ
= ∅. Equation (26) has a convex quadratic
objective and second-order cone constraints (friction cones). We solve it as an inner-loop Second-
Order Cone Program (SOCP) with Clarabel (Goulart & Chen, 2024), using DiffCP (Agrawal et al.,
2019b) and the differentiable CVXPYlayers PyTorch interface (Agrawal et al., 2019a).
Motion generation, forward kinematics, and dynamics tensors use single precision. The CVXPYlayers
problem data are converted to double precision for the CPU solve, and the returned solution is
converted back to the dynamics tensor precision and device. Acceleration reconstruction uses a linear
solve with B.
D.2
BATCHING AND COMPUTATIONAL EFFICIENCY
At each diffusion noise optimization step, we group the SOCPs instances (i.e., per individual in the
population and per motion-plan time step) by the tuple of active contact-point identities. For each
group, we compile a dedicated CVXPY problem and then solve it for all samples in the batch in
parallel on the CPU. This approach avoids recompilation as stances change, while also sidestepping
the numerical issues that can arise from “Big-M” reformulations of disjunctive contact constraints.
The CVXPY layer is cached for each active-contact tuple and reused when that tuple recurs. All
candidate/frame instances with the same active-contact tuple are stacked into one layer call. The CPU
backend uses eight forward workers by default, with an environment-variable override available.
D.3
DIFFERENTIATION FOR GRADIENT-BASED BASELINES
Gradient-based methods differentiate the scalar batch-mean loss through the optimized motion or
noise, rotation decoding, forward kinematics, derivative estimation, and, when active, the CVXPYlay-
ers/DiffCP inverse-dynamics solve. Denoiser parameters remain fixed. Hard contact masks and the
grouping of active contacts are discrete selections; derivatives follow the selected problem and do not
differentiate a change of contact set. Absolute values, clamping, vector norms, and separating-axis
minima use the piecewise derivatives provided by PyTorch.

E.5
INFERENCE-TIME OPTIMIZATION BASELINES AND COMPARISON BUDGETS
We compare inference-time methods that improve the physical feasibility of generated motion while
keeping the MDM fixed. Within each experimental setting, the optimization methods use the same
task objectives and feasibility constraints. Approaches that require retraining or finetuning the motion
model (Xu et al., 2025; Watanabe et al., 2025), or a pretrained tracking controller for closed-loop
simulator rollouts (Yuan et al., 2023; Watanabe et al., 2025; Tevet et al., 2025), fall outside this
comparison and are, therefore, out-of-scope for this paper. Appendix F.3 specifies the experimental
settings.
E.5.1
BASELINE METHODS
Unoptimized Generation
We generate a batch of stochastic motions from the trained MDM and
select the sample whose aggregated loss is closest to the batch median as a representative sample by
loss. For an even batch size, the median is the average of the two middle losses.
Motion-Space Optimization
We directly post-process the generated motion sequence, analogous in
spirit to constraint-aware, physics-informed retargeting of motion capture to robot embodiments (Luo
et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023; Ze et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2025;
Dhedin et al., 2026). Motion GD updates the generated batch of motions to minimize the aggregated
loss and returns the lowest-loss motion after its final update. Motion Sep-CMA-ES initializes its
search mean with the lowest-loss generated motion, optimizes in motion space, and returns and
evaluates the final search mean.

We sample successive populations of diffusion noise tensors from the nominal

Noise GD
We optimize the batch of diffusion noise tensors by differentiating through the sampler,

Noise ZeNO
ZeNO (Kim et al., 2026) optimizes a frozen generator’s initial noise using only
rewards evaluated on generated outputs. Its update is derived from path-integral control with an
Ornstein–Uhlenbeck reference process whose stationary distribution is standard Gaussian. Centering
rewards and linearizing their exponential weights under a small-variance approximation yields a
control estimate that favors perturbations producing above-average rewards. Each iteration evaluates
perturbed noise candidates, combines this estimate with an independent Gaussian perturbation, and
projects the updated state onto a sphere.
We adapt this strategy, originally evaluated for image generation and protein design, to the physical-
feasibility constraint formulation and task objectives of Physible, extending the optimization variables
to both initial and posterior diffusion noise. In the kinematic setting, the reward is the negative of our
shared loss, combining contact-pattern preservation, ground slip, and self-collision penalties with
velocity tracking and autoregressive motion consistency under the same weights as the other methods.

STYLE CLASSIFIER AND FID EVALUATION
Following Chen et al. (2024a), we evaluate whether generated motions preserve the commanded style
using a style classifier trained on 15-timestep motion chunks (0.5 s) from the 100STYLE dataset
retargeted to the SMPL humanoid (Loper et al., 2015). We segment each generated sequence (with
a fixed style command) into overlapping 15-timestep windows, run the classifier, and report: (i)
window-level style accuracy and (ii) overall FID pooled across styles and per-style FID computed
from the classifier activations against the corresponding reference data.
35


The style classifier embeds each frame’s complete encoded motion into 128 features and prepends a
learned register token. After positional encoding, a four-layer transformer encoder with four heads,
feed-forward width 1024, GELU activation, and dropout 0.2 produces the register representation.
This representation, before the final GELU and linear classification head, supplies the features for
FID. The head predicts one of the 100 styles.
Classifier inputs are expressed relative to the root position and heading of the window’s first frame.
Its training script uses 15-frame windows at stride ten, cross-entropy loss, and Adam with learning
rate 10−3. Evaluation uses every sliding 15-frame window at stride one. For a 1500-frame sequence
of one style this gives 1486 evaluated windows. The generated features are compared with the stored
per-style reference means and covariances; the final style accuracy and FID are evaluated over the
full committed sequence.

We benchmark the runtime of each optimization method in two ways: (i) time per iteration (e.g., a

and (ii) total wall-clock time to optimize one motion horizon. For the total-time comparison, we use
the same number of iterations for all sampling-based methods (e.g., best-of-n, Sep-CMA-ES) and

The per-iteration timers bracket the optimizer’s sampling/generation, objective and constraint evalua-

G.1
QUANTITATIVE COMPARISON WITH BASELINES
Tab. 1 and Tab. 2 compare the proposed diffusion-noise optimization approach against the baseline
methods for the SMPL humanoid and the bipedal robot, respectively.
Overall, the proposed method best addresses the multi-objective problem, consistently reducing
kinematic constraint violations and objective costs relative to the unoptimized motion while preserving
the motion style. In contrast, the baselines—despite using identical loss/fitness weights—tend to
improve only a subset of constraints and objectives while degrading others. Gradient-based methods
(e.g., GD, Guided Diffusion) particularly struggle with the contact pattern preservation constraint
due to its discontinuous gradients. Sampling-based methods handle this constraint more robustly,
with Sep-CMA-ES performing best; however, simple Best-of-N sampling is insufficient in our
high-dimensional search space (78,840 variables for SMPL and 103,680 for the bipedal robot).
While direct motion optimization can be effective, it often introduces visible jitter, which is reflected in
lower LDJ values. Guided Diffusion is computationally efficient because it avoids iterative inference
through (or backpropagation through) the full MDM and instead only differentiates the loss terms, but
its effectiveness is limited—likely due to discontinuous gradients and the small number of denoising
steps. Somewhat surprisingly, GD-based noise optimization—despite its popularity (Karunratanakul
et al., 2024; Eyring et al., 2025)—performs poorly in our setting: it is computationally expensive
(requiring backpropagation through both the objectives and the entire MDM at each iteration), is
sensitive to discontinuous gradient information, and often yields severe ground penetration and
degraded motion quality and style.
Under the same physical-feasibility constraint formulation and loss weights, Physible achieves lower
mean ground penetration (0.676 vs. 0.939 mm), self-collision penetration (0.0152 vs. 0.0455 mm),
and ground slip (0.0484 vs. 0.0792 m s−1) than Noise ZeNO on SMPL, although ZeNO retains
slightly higher style accuracy (91.71% vs. 87.73%; Tab. 1). On the bipedal robot, Physible reduces
mean ground penetration, self-collision penetration, and slip by 40%, 85%, and 61% relative to Noise
ZeNO, respectively (Tab. 2).

G.3.2
NOISE STANDARDIZATION
As shown in Tab. 1, omitting Eq. (3) yields a style accuracy of 76.01 %, overall FID of 22.00,
and mean per-style FID of 74.21. With standardization, these values improve to 87.73 %, 14.41,
and 43.95, respectively: an accuracy gain of 11.72 percentage points and a 40.8% reduction in
mean per-style FID. Standardization also improves LDJ from −7.29 to −6.59 and decreases mean
motion-consistency error from 1.92 × 10−4 to 1.52 × 10−4.
These gains come with higher mean ground penetration (0.676 vs. 0.541 mm), self-collision pen-
etration (0.0152 vs. 0.009 22 mm), slip (0.0484 vs. 0.0401 m s−1), and velocity-tracking error
(0.0392 vs. 0.0334). Thus, the corrected NNS results support a tradeoff between feasibility and style
preservation, with residual violations in both methods.
G.3.3
CONTACT PATTERN PRESERVATION
One aspect of kinematic feasibility is avoiding (excessive) ground penetrations, which are physically
infeasible. A natural question is why we favor the Contact Pattern Preservation constraint (Sec. 6.2)
over directly minimizing the ground penetration distance. The key reason is to prevent floating charac-
ters, i.e., motions that exhibit extended time intervals without ground contact.1 When we co-optimize
dynamical feasibility, floating is implicitly discouraged because, without contacts, the accelerations
encoded in the motion cannot be supported due to the absence of contact forces. In contrast, under
purely kinematic feasibility optimization, this implicit safeguard is absent: optimizing only a ground
penetration constraint can yield “seemingly” valid solutions that simply avoid penetration by lifting
the character off the ground for long durations. We therefore employ the contact pattern preservation
constraint, which enforces (i) contact—while limiting penetration—during phases where contact
should occur (thereby preventing floating), and (ii) separation during phases where contact should
not occur.
To substantiate this reasoning, we perform an ablation study that compares the contact behavior
obtained when using the Ground Penetration constraint versus the proposed Contact Pattern Preser-
vation constraint. The three conditions use the settings in Appendix F.2.
Figure 4 compares unoptimized motion and the two losses for the SMPL humanoid in the neutral
walking style, using matched foot close-ups, signed ground clearance, and intended versus observed
foot contacts. Over the 240-frame (8 s) clips, the ground penetration distance loss reduces mean
penetration from 3.826 mm to 0.002 mm, but leaves all checked contact points above ground in
91.7% of frames, with a longest contact loss of 2.20 s. Contact pattern preservation reduces mean
penetration to 1.166 mm, while limiting frames without contact to 4.2% and the longest contact loss
to 0.13 s (unoptimized: 3.3% and 0.07 s). Thus, it reduces penetration while avoiding the prolonged
floating induced by the ground penetration distance loss, although some penetrations still exceed the
5 mm allowance. Figs. 5 and 6 provide the corresponding contact-point ground separation plots.
1Short no-contact phases can be feasible or even desired (e.g., jumping or running). However, if a motion
lacks ground contact for long periods, it will generally not be dynamically feasible.

SUPPLEMENTARY DYNAMIC FEASIBILITY RESULTS
This appendix compares methods, evaluates results across motion styles and contact conditions, and
measures variability across optimization seeds. It then analyzes selected motions and downstream RL
tracking. Experimental settings and metric definitions are given in Appendix F.
H.1
QUANTITATIVE EVALUATION
H.1.1
COMPARISON WITH BASELINES FOR TWO-FOOT-JUMP
The quantitative results in Tab. 3 show that most optimization methods reduce the dynamical infeasi-
bility metric relative to the unoptimized motion, but with different trade-offs. Noise GD obtains the
lowest acceleration-reconstruction error and ID residual (0.013 and 1.94, respectively), but requires
by far the longest wall-clock time per horizon and reduces LDJ from −7.71 to −10.03. Direct Motion
Sep-CMA-ES also strongly reduces the two dynamical metrics, but has the lowest LDJ (−11.67)
among the compared methods. In contrast, Physible reduces acceleration reconstruction from 0.454
to 0.081 and the ID residual from 84.4 to 26.5, while attaining the highest LDJ among the optimized
methods (−8.51). Guided Diffusion is comparatively fast in total horizon time, but fails in this setting,
increasing both dynamical errors and reducing LDJ to −9.91. Physible trades larger remaining resid-
uals for smoother motion and lower computational cost than Noise GD. Appendix H.3.1 examines
the associated changes in motion style and contact behavior.

H.3
MOTION CASE STUDIES FOR QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS
The following cases pair motion behavior with time-resolved diagnostics to allow for a qualitative
analysis of the optimized motion. Acceleration, torque, and friction diagnostics use the nominal
inverse-dynamics solution. Signed acceleration residuals are plotted as planned minus reconstructed
acceleration, corresponding to −eacc. Virtual-wrench diagnostics use the separate relaxed solve in
Eq. (32). Across the method comparisons, lower dynamic residuals can coincide with degraded style,
reduced smoothness, or loss of ground contact; the diagnostics are therefore interpreted together.
H.3.1
TWO-FOOT JUMP
Comparison against Baselines
We compare optimization methods on a 250-frame (5 s) two-foot-
jump sequence of the bipedal robot. Figure 25 shows unoptimized generation, Motion Sep-CMA-ES,
Guided Diffusion, Noise GD, and Physible, corresponding to the methods evaluated in Tab. 3. Motion
snapshots and time histories of ground clearance, vertical base acceleration error, and joint jerk
illustrate the tradeoffs between dynamic consistency, contact behavior, and motion quality. Motion
Sep-CMA-ES introduces substantial jitter, reflected in rapidly varying joint accelerations and torques
(Figs. 31–33 and Figs. 35–36), while Guided Diffusion increases vertical base acceleration errors and
produces both unrealistic floating and ground penetration. Noise GD reduces acceleration error but
leaves the character floating throughout the sequence, suggesting that optimization can settle on a
physically implausible solution despite achieving a small residual. The ground-separation and slip
traces show that its low slip coincides with loss of contact (Figs. 37 and 38). Physible achieves a
more balanced tradeoff: it reduces vertical base acceleration error while retaining the characteristic
jumping motion, repeated ground contacts, and comparatively smooth trajectories.
The full-sequence diagnostics compare unoptimized generation, Motion Sep-CMA-ES, Noise GD,
and Physible. Guided Diffusion is omitted from these plots because its much larger errors would
obscure the differences among the remaining methods.
Unoptimized vs. Optimized
Physible improves dynamic consistency while retaining the character-
istic jump trajectory and support phases. In Fig. 26, the rendered poses and side-view root trajectories
show that the optimized motion follows a similar jump arc to the unoptimized reference. The smaller
virtual root forces and acceleration-residual arrows illustrate the reduction in nonphysical residual
support and acceleration mismatch, particularly around takeoff and landing. The same improvement
is visible over the full sequence in the virtual-wrench and root-acceleration-residual traces (Figs. 27
and 28). The planned-versus-feasible acceleration curves in Fig. 26 and Figs. 29–33 show closer
agreement after optimization for the root and joints while preserving the temporal structure of the
jump.
These reductions in dynamic mismatch accompany repeated ground contacts during the support
phases, as shown by the motion snapshots in Fig. 26 and the ground-separation traces in Fig. 37. The
slip and contact-force plots provide complementary views of the ground interaction (Figs. 38 and 34),
with contact forces remaining within the friction cones. Residual acceleration mismatch remains, but
Physible retains the intended jumping behavior and support phases without an explicit contact-pattern
objective.

H.3.2
LEFT HOP: UNOPTIMIZED VS. PHYSIBLE
The left-hop comparison covers 150 frames (3 s) on the bipedal robot and compares unoptimized
generation with Physible under asymmetric support. In Tab. 9, mean acceleration-reconstruction
error decreases from 0.511 to 0.009, and the mean ID residual decreases from 106.43 to 1.91,
corresponding to reductions of approximately 98% in both metrics. This qualitative analysis reveal
how these improvements arise and which aspects of the original motion change during refinement.
A prominent source of infeasibility in the unoptimized motion is the vertical root acceleration.
During repeated intervals, the nominal inverse-dynamics solve reconstructs an acceleration close
to free fall while the planned acceleration remains much higher (Fig. 41). The resulting vertical
acceleration residual repeatedly reaches approximately 10 m s−2 or more (Fig. 40). Consistently,
the separate relaxed solve requires a large virtual upward force, often of order 100 N, together with
substantial corrective moments (Fig. 39). After optimization, the planned and reconstructed root
accelerations nearly coincide, and the virtual force and moment traces remain close to zero over most
of the sequence, apart from short transients. The joint-acceleration residuals also decrease markedly
(Fig. 42); the planned-versus-feasible joint plots retain oscillatory motion while showing changes in
its amplitude and timing (Figs. 43–45).
During sustained toe support, the reconstructed tangential force remains well below its friction limit,
and the joint torques remain within the displayed actuator bounds (Fig. 46 and Figs. 47–48). The
optimized left hip-roll and ankle-pitch torques become more sustained, consistent with the longer
support intervals.

H.3.3
UPHILL AND DOWNHILL WALKING: UNOPTIMIZED VS. PHYSIBLE
We compare unoptimized and Physible-optimized bipedal robot motions in the neutral style for uphill
(+25◦) and downhill (−25◦) scenarios in Fig. 51. Each sequence spans 3 s at 50 Hz, with friction
coefficient µc,p = 1.0. The fixed command and experiment settings are given in Appendix F.2. The
renderings and root pitch use a slope-aligned coordinate frame: x points along the direction of travel
and z is normal to the surface, so the horizontal ground plane in the renderings represents the slope.
We evaluate the mismatch between planned and feasible reconstructed root accelerations using the
Euclidean norms of the three-dimensional linear and angular residuals, ∥∆a∥2 and ∥∆α∥2. Their
root-mean-square values over each sequence decrease from 5.95 to 3.13 m s−2 for uphill motion
(47%) and from 5.00 to 0.82 m s−2 for downhill motion (84%). The corresponding angular mismatch
decreases from 3.01 to 0.32 rad s−2 (89%) and from 3.00 to 0.12 rad s−2 (96%), respectively.
Optimization produces opposite pitch adaptations in the two scenarios: root pitch shifts toward
positive values uphill and negative values downhill. The planned and reconstructed accelerations
agree more closely after optimization, especially downhill. The uphill sequence retains appreciable
linear mismatch, visible in both the along-slope and slope-normal components. These examples
therefore show improved dynamic consistency under the slope-specific gravity vectors.

H.4
DOWNSTREAM RL TRACKING
We evaluate downstream tracking of the unoptimized and Physible-optimized bipedal two-foot-jump
motions using the protocol in Appendix F.7. Relative to the policy trained on the unoptimized
motion, the policy trained on the optimized motion reduces body position, body orientation, and
joint position tracking error by 16.4%, 11.9%, and 7.4%, respectively, indicating that the increased
dynamical consistency makes the reference easier to track. In contrast, global root position and
orientation error increase by 39.6% and 41.3%. We attribute this to the step-wise nature of our
dynamic feasibility optimization: accelerations are corrected locally in time rather than through the
resulting integrated trajectory, so small residual inconsistencies accumulate as drift in the global root
motion, while the more local and repetitive joint and body motion is largely unaffected. Consistent
with this interpretation, the RL reward tracks the motion-frame root pose together with root-relative
body motion, but it does not explicitly penalize accumulated global root-pose drift. The tracking
results therefore show that reducing local dynamic residuals can improve body and joint tracking
without improving every aspect of the complete trajectory.

DETAILED LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Our evaluation is currently limited to bipedal robotic embodiments. Future work should study addi-
tional robot topologies and embodiments (e.g., manipulators, quadrupeds, and mobile manipulators).
The method further relies on accurate knowledge of robot dynamics, inertial parameters, contact
geometry, and contact characteristics (e.g., friction). In practice, these quantities are uncertain and
time-varying, and perception errors can directly affect contact reasoning. A natural next step is to
incorporate uncertainty into the optimization (e.g., robust/risk-sensitive feasibility) and to evaluate
how performance degrades under dynamics/contact mismatch.
Moreover, we currently assume that all ground contacts are sticking. While this simplifies ID
and feasibility evaluation, it rules out behaviors that deliberately leverage controlled slipping (e.g.,
moonwalk-like styles). Future work could therefore co-optimize contact-mode decisions jointly with
the motion.
Relatedly, we infer the enforced contact pattern for the contact pattern preservation constraint
via a majority vote over a batch of unbiased denoised motion sequences. Although effective in
practice, this choice is restrictive, as it discourages substantial changes to the initial contact pattern
during optimization. Future work should investigate alternatives that simultaneously mitigate ground
penetration and “floating” artifacts in kinematic feasibility optimization without overly constraining
contact-pattern changes.
More broadly, the proposed optimization does not guarantee that the injected/optimized noise
remains within the diffusion model’s training distribution. Therefore, a common failure mode of the
optimization is a style collapse where objectives and constraints might be satisfied, but the optimized
motion does not adhere to the commanded motion style anymore. Beyond noise normalization, future
work should investigate additional distribution-preserving mechanisms to better keep optimized
motions in-distribution while still improving feasibility.
Furthermore, the approach requires trading off multiple (sometimes competing) objectives and
constraints, which can make it challenging to identify suitable weights in the fitness function. In
addition, achieving strict satisfaction of physical constraints would require many iterations and can
push the optimization into regions where the diffusion model drifts out-of-distribution, degrading
motion style. Future work should therefore explore enforcing physical feasibility constraints earlier
in the pipeline—including during motion retargeting and in the construction/selection of the training
dataset—so that the unoptimized diffusion output is closer to physical feasibility and requires smaller
inference-time corrections.
Building on the previous point, a particularly tricky issue is ground penetration. Physically feasible
motion (e.g., non-floating behavior, low tangential slip velocities near the ground, and dynamically
feasible accelerations) relies on active contacts for constraint enforcement. While, in principle,
one would like exactly zero penetration—ensuring both contact and no interpenetration—this is in
practice generally infeasible from an optimization standpoint. We therefore introduce a contact-
pattern preservation constraint that permits a limited amount of penetration. However, the appropriate
penetration threshold is not obvious: setting it too low can substantially increase problem difficulty
and slow convergence. Future work should develop more synergistic approaches that better resolve
this physical-feasibility “tension”.
From a practical standpoint, repeated horizon-wide feasibility evaluations (including inner-loop
solves of the ID) can be computationally expensive for long horizons or real-time use. Promising
directions include warm-starting (possibly with a learned modification of the diffusion noise (Eyring
et al., 2025)), multi-resolution optimization (Antonova et al., 2023), and amortized surrogates for
(parts of) the feasibility evaluation to reduce wall-clock time without sacrificing reliability.
