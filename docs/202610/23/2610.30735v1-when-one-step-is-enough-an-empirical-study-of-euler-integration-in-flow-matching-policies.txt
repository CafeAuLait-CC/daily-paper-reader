# When One Step Is Enough: An Empirical Study of Euler Integration in Flow-Matching Policies 

Shivakanth Sujit ∗1, Luca Nunziante ∗1, Rousslan Fernand Julien Dossa 1, Kai Arulkumaran 11Araya Inc., Tokyo 

<sujit shivakanth, luca nunziante, dossa, kai arulkumaran>@araya.org 

onestepflow.github.io 

Abstract: Flow matching is now the dominant approach for vision-language ac-tion (VLA) robot policies, thanks to its expressiveness and simple training objec-tive. However, inference for standard flow matching requires iteratively solving an ODE, limiting the control frequency at deployment. While several works have proposed novel methods to allow for single-shot action generation, we ask whether such additional objectives are actually necessary. We find that across six flow matching VLAs, one-step action generation largely matches multi-step sampling at scale: multi-step integration confers little to no consistent advantage at infer-ence. We empirically find that this holds as long as models are sufficiently trained and have enough capacity. Practically, the implication is that for large VLAs, the single-step policy comes essentially for free from the multi-step model, enabling low-latency deployment without retraining, distillation, or auxiliary objectives. 

Keywords: Vision-language action models, Flow matching, Policy evaluation

To generate action samples, these models learn a vector field that drives noise samples, taken from a source distribution, to a target distribution. Of these, flow matching is now the dominant

Many of the latest robot foundation models, in the form of vision-language-action (VLA) models, combine a large, pretrained vision-language model (VLM) backbone [6, 7, 8, 9], providing existing image and text understanding, with a flow matching action head [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. In flow matching policies, a velocity field is learned and generating actions requires integrating the learned velocity field, typically with an Euler integrator that evaluates the network N times. Higher N results in lower discretization error at the cost of increased latency, thus limiting control frequency, and subsequently hurting fluid motion in dynamic or contact-rich tasks. Recent works mitigate these issues by distilling the multi-step policy into a single-step proxy [4], introducing distribution-shaping objectives that are expensive for large-scale VLAs [16], or augmenting the flow matching objective to encourage straight-line flows that need fewer integration steps [17, 18, 19, 20]. This multi-step formulation also necessitates backpropagation-through-time (BPTT) for gradient-based optimization of reinforcement learning (RL) objectives, which is costly and prone to unstable gradients. Due to the simplicity of its formulation, in practice VLAs are trained with the rectified flow matching objective) [21], where the target velocity along each training pair is constant, producing linear inter-polation paths between source and target samples. It is not the case in general that the learned trajec-tories are straight, as intersecting linear paths produce curved learned trajectories, which in image generation degrades single-step sample quality [21]. However, for robot control, given the current observation and state, the nature of the conditional target distribution may differ substantially from the image generation setting. Prior work [22] finds that, for control policies, the performance advan-tage of generative models over regression ones mainly comes from stochasticity injection and the dense supervision signal during training, rather than from modeling multimodality, which they find largely absent in robotics datasets (including the ones understood to be multimodal like Push T ), likely due to their high-dimensional observation space. Concurrent work [23] reaches a similar conclusion for VLAs, but studies a single architecture, focusing on training hyperparameters on LIBERO [24] and its variants, with a small real-world experiment. In contrast, we study six VLAs across multiple benchmarks, run a large-scale real-world evaluation, and analyse the smoothness and diversity of the generated trajectories. We ask whether large-scale VLAs trained with the rectified flow objective and no additional regu-larization can be integrated in a single step at inference without loss of performance. We answer this question with what is, to our knowledge, the first paired one-step vs multi-step ablation of flow matching VLAs, covering six VLAs across multiple benchmarks. We evaluate six flow matching VLA models spanning 450 M to 4.7B parameters (SmolVLA [10], FLOWER [12], X-VLA [11], 

π0.5 [13], LAP [14], XR-0 [15]) on four simulation benchmarks (LIBERO [24], LIBERO+ [25], CALVIN [26], RoboTwin 2.0 [27]) and a real world Franka suite [28]. All comparisons use matched seeds and real world paired testing. We compare success rates through the two one-sided t-tests (TOST) equivalence test [29], analyse the difference in smoothness and diversity of the generated trajectories and also report a latency comparison. Results are summarised in Fig. 1. Our findings are as follows: • Step-equivalence is a factor of model scale, VLM pretraining and training maturity: 

The four largest VLAs show strong robustness to single-step inference across simulation benchmarks. π0.5 and XR-0 are statistically equivalent within 3 percentage points (pp) on every simulation benchmark for which a public checkpoint was available. X-VLA and LAP are equivalent on all but one benchmark each (Sec. 4.1). Initialization ablations fur-ther suggest that pretrained visual-language representations favour step-equivalence, even without robot-action pretraining, and the difference between one-step and multi-step in-ference fades with longer fine-tuning (Sec. 4.5). Similarly, for the real world, multi-step inference confers no consistent advantage in terms of success rate or trajectory smoothness (Sec. 4.1 and 4.3). 2• Longer task horizons expose greater sensitivity to inference steps: The two smaller models (FLOWER, SmolVLA) retain a performance gap that is larger on long-horizon tasks while their short-horizon performance is more robust to the number of integration steps (Sec. 4.1). Importantly, additional Euler steps do not always help: the direction of the performance gap varies across the smaller models (Sec. 4.1 and 4.5). • Practical payoff: For most current flow matching VLAs, multi-step inference does not improve performance over one step, enabling 1.6-4.8× lower latency at deployment (Sec. 4.2). Taken together, these results reframe the conventional wisdom around inference in VLAs. While a growing body of work proposes auxiliary objectives explicitly to recover the single-step regime, we find that for the dominant class of present day flow matching VLAs, the single-step regime is already accessible without any retraining, architectural changes, or additional regularization. 

2 Background and Related Work 

2.1 Flow matching models 

Flow matching [30] is a generative modelling framework that learns a time-dependent velocity field 

vθ (xt, t ) defining a continuous-time transport from a source distribution p0 to a target data distribu-tion p1. The transport is described by the ordinary differential equation (ODE) 

dx t

dt = vθ (xt, t ), x0 ∼ p0, t ∈ [0 , 1] (1) that converts x0 ∼ p0 to x1 ∼ p1. In rectified flow matching [21], the driving velocity field is set to follow the direction of the linear path from x0 to x1, i.e., vθ (xt, t ) = x1 − x0, resulting in 

xt = (1 − t) x0 + t x 1. The rectified flow matching objective regresses the predicted velocity onto this target: 

LFM (θ) = Et, x 0, x 1 vθ (xt, t ) − (x1 − x0) 22, with t ∼ ρ (2) where ρ is a distribution in [0 , 1] . As discussed in [21], the minimiser of Eq. 2 is v⋆(x, t ) = E[x1 −

x0|xt = x] and it is straight iff the interpolation paths do not cross. To generate a sample from the target distribution, the learned velocity field is integrated from t = 0 

to t = 1 . The simplest and most used integrator for flow matching is the explicit Euler scheme with 

N uniform steps of size h = 1 /N :

xt+h = xt + h · vθ (xt, t ) t = 0 , 1/N, . . . , (N − 1) /N . (3) The choice of N trades inference cost against discretization error, and the choice of a single Euler step ( N = 1 ) is exact only if the flow is straight. The reference N reported by published VLAs ranges from 4 to 10 (Tab. 1). In this work, we ask whether using N = 1 leads to a drop in perfor-mance for robot action generation. 

2.2 Accelerating flow and diffusion inference 

The cost of iterative sampling has motivated a broad line of work on reducing the number of inte-gration steps required at inference time. Consistency models [31], shortcut models [19], and Mean-Flow [20] augment the training objective to recover the multi-step solution from a single forward pass. The motivation for doing so is well-documented in image generation, where the quality of the generated image shows that vanilla flow and diffusion models degrade sharply when collapsed into a few Euler steps [32], which further motivates work on improved ODE solvers and sampling schedules [33, 34]. The few-step methods that have been applied to robotics, MeanFlow [35] and ManiFlow [18], were evaluated on small-scale policies where empirically vanilla flow matching policies with single-step inference perform substantially worse than with multi-step inference. Recent work [22] shows that the choice of the model architecture is a major component of policy performance. This motivates 3asking whether the one-step degradation actually remains with large flow matching VLAs and, to the best of our knowledge, this has not been thoroughly investigated. 

3 Experimental Design 

Our central question is whether the multi-step Euler integration is buying meaningful improvements in task success rates over a single Euler step. We answer this with a paired comparison protocol. For each (model, benchmark) pair, we take a single trained policy, and evaluate once with the reference number of integration steps reported by the original paper, and once with a single Euler step. To reduce stochasticity in the evaluation process, for every (model, benchmark) pair, the 1-step and reference-step rollouts have the same initial conditions [36]. In simulation this means matched initial seeds for each episode trial. For real world evaluation, trials are run as paired A/B tests. To further control for experimenter bias, the experimenter is blind to the variant being tested. Finally, in real world experiments we also seed the noise sampled from the policy before every rollout within the pair.

3.2 Benchmarks 

We evaluate seven models across four simulation benchmarks and one real world suite. 

Simulation Benchmarks: We test all the models on the LIBERO [24] benchmark. LIBERO has four task suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, LIBERO-10), each comprising 10 tasks. We report aggre-gate results over the Spatial, Object and Goal suites (denoted as SGO), and separate results for the long-horizon LIBERO-10 suite. For evaluation, we run 50 trials per task, totalling 2000 tri-als per model variant. To test our hypothesis against distribution shift, we also test the models on 

LIBERO+ [25], a perturbation-extended version of LIBERO with 10 030 task instances. We also evaluate on the CALVIN ABC →D [26] setting that tests for zero shot generalization. CALVIN is a long-horizon language-conditioned benchmark, and evaluation requires policies to complete a sequence of 5 tasks across 1000 instruction chains. We report SR@5 (full-chain success) and 4average chain length. Finally, we also include the RoboTwin 2.0 [27] benchmark, a 50 -task biman-ual manipulation benchmark with two difficulty regimes (”clean” and ”randomised”). We use the matched-seed paired protocol ( 100 episodes per task) from the original benchmark, yielding 5000 

paired episodes per regime per (model, number of steps) cell. Separately, we also examine how the different models behave on one task from D3IL [38], a benchmark that explicitly evaluates the model’s ability to learn multimodal behaviours. 

Real world Franka suite: This consists of four in-house kitchen-style tasks using a Franka Emika Panda arm (Fig. 2): spoon to drawer (longest-horizon, pick-and-place), open drawer (contact-rich), 

open oven (precise manipulation), and close oven (short horizon task). The training dataset is the same as the one used in [28]. We run 100 trials per task per (model, num steps), totalling 400 

episodes per model variant and a total of 4800 rollouts. Additional evaluation details are provided in the Appendix (Table D4).

3.3 Evaluation Protocol 

To claim a positive equivalence we test our hypothesis of step-invariance through the TOST proce-dure [29] (see Tab. 2). We conduct this test aggregating results across tasks within a benchmark: this violates the i.i.d. assumption for the samples of the compared distributions, but the effect is negligible and recommended practice in prior work [36]. For real world experiments, due to a more restricted evaluation budget than simulation, we report and discuss the confidence intervals (CIs) of the difference between the approaches, and conduct a McNemar analysis (see Tab. 3 and Sec. B.2). We also investigate the quality of the executed real world trajectories through a jerk analysis (see Fig. 3). For a per-task breakdown we refer to Sec. E. For TOST, we set a margin of practical equivalence of δ = 3 pp (percentage points) when comparing success rates, and δ = 0 .15 when comparing chain length for CALVIN 1 and a confidence level 

α = 0 .05 . To claim equivalence, TOST needs to reject two composite null hypotheses, hence the CIs will be computed at a confidence of 90% , i.e., 100(1 − 2α)% . We declare the two methods equivalent within the margin δ iff the CI for the difference in means of the two distributions is strictly contained in [−δ, δ ], we declare a relevant difference if it lies completely outside of it, and otherwise the test is inconclusive (more details in Sec. B).

4.2 Inference Latency 

On a single A100 GPU, collapsing to 1-step cuts latency by 1.6× to 4.8×, raising the achievable control frequency (see Tab. 1). Since only the action head re-runs per Euler step, models with a small action head relative to the VLM gain the least ( π0.5, 1.6×), while those where the action head is comparable gain the most (SmolVLA, 4.8×). For the larger models, the latency gains generally come at little cost to task success, with the exceptions noted above.

Beyond success rates, we ask whether one-step inference changes the smoothness of the executed motion. To quantify trajectory smoothness we use the per-episode root mean square (RMS) of the joint jerk: after recording the executed joint trajectory, we first smooth it with a Savitzky-Golay filter (polynomial of order 3, window size 11 ) and then compute the jerk via finite differences. Because failure modes can produce different jerk profiles, we only compare episodes where both approaches succeed. The main results show that multi-step Euler inference does not guarantee higher success rates for current flow matching VLAs. We now investigate two questions: (Q1) How do model scale and pretraining influence step-equivalence? (Q2) How does the gap evolve over the course of training? We focus on LIBERO because it contains both short-horizon SGO suites, and the longer-horizon LIBERO-10 suite, where most of the performance gaps appear. Our findings are visualised in Fig. 5. 

Q1: How do model scale and pretraining influence step-equivalence? We first consider the randomly initialised π0.5 (scratch; Fig. 5, third column). It favours one-step inference throughout training and has not reached step-equivalence by the final checkpoint, although its gap narrows sub-stantially (from −20 to −3 pp on SGO with a [−4.38 , −1.64] CI). In the second column of Fig. 5, we omit the from-scratch X-VLA variant because of its low absolute task performance: across eval-uated checkpoints and inference-step settings, success rates remain below 10% on LIBERO-10, 5% on Goal, and 20% on Spatial, and peak at 42.8% on Object. Despite its low absolute performance, from-scratch X-VLA favours one-step inference more often, including on pooled SGO at the fi-nal two checkpoints, where the 90% CIs for the performance gap lie entirely below zero. Thus, within our training budget, step-equivalence does not emerge from random initialization, though the converging trend for π0.5 suggests that longer training might close the gap. To identify which kind of pretraining matters, we compare different initializations for both π0.5 and X-VLA: a generic VLM (PaliGemma2 [8] or Florence [7], respectively) with a randomly initialised action expert, the VLA’s own VLM backbone (trained also on robot data) with a randomly initialised 8action expert, and the fully pretrained VLA. For both models, all three achieve step-equivalence on SGO and reach near-zero gaps by the final checkpoint, so robot-action pretraining is not required. However, pretrained vision-language features alone are not sufficient: the 211M DiT, which condi-tions on pretrained CLIP image and text encoders [37] but trains the remaining model from scratch, develops a multi-step advantage on LIBERO-10 that grows to 15 pp over training (first column), echoing our scale-dependent main results. 

Q2: How does the gap evolve over the course of training? π0.5 is step-equivalent on SGO from the earliest checkpoint. For X-VLA, early in training, additional Euler steps hurt performance, and this gap collapses by the final checkpoint. FLOWER is the clearest case where multi-step helps, but the benefit is localised to LIBERO-10 at early and intermediate checkpoints, while the short-horizon SGO gap remains near zero and step-equivalence is verified on both SGO and LIBERO-10 at the last checkpoint (Fig. 5, rightmost column). This last point resolves an apparent tension with Tab. 2 where FLOWER shows a residual gap on LIBERO while X-VLA, a model of similar scale ( 950 M), does not. The discrepancy is one of checkpoint selection, not scale. The authors of FLOWER train their model for up to 40 epochs but mention that they use early stopping and for LIBERO use the checkpoint at epoch 19. Our ablation sweep instead retains all checkpoints during training, and at the final checkpoint FLOWER’s step-count performance gap disappears, bringing it in line with X-VLA. The Tab. 2 gap therefore reflects an early-stopped checkpoint evaluated before step-invariance has fully emerged, rather than a property of FLOWER’s scale or architecture. Together, these refine the main claim: step-equivalence is favoured by larger models, generic VLM pretraining, and longer fine-tuning. When one of these properties is missing, tasks with longer horizons tend to show step-dependence. 

5 Limitations 

To aid reproducibility and avoid confounding step-invariance with our own training decisions, we deliberately used released checkpoints as-is rather than fine-tuning base models for all our evalu-ations in simulation. The downside is uneven coverage: not every model ships a checkpoint for every benchmark, so some (model, benchmark) cells could not be evaluated. A full grid would re-quire training each model on each benchmark, which we leave to future work. While the aggregate results for the real world tasks are close, we observed up to 13 pp difference between single- and multi-step regimes on single tasks. Real world evaluation could therefore benefit from an increased dataset size, matching the scale of the simulated benchmarks. Finally, we show the single-step pol-icy matches the multi-step reference in most cases and that single-step gradient computations are therefore cheap. However, we do not run RL experiments to validate that resulting policy gradient updates are stable and effective at VLA scale. 

6 Conclusion 

Across six flow matching VLAs from 450M to 4.7B parameters, plus a 211 M DiT, four simulation benchmarks and a real world Franka suite, we find the assumption that multi-step inference is nec-essary for task performance does not hold for well-trained large VLAs. Where multi-step helps, the benefit is localised to smaller models on long-horizon tasks. Our ablations trace step-invariance to a combination of large scale models, training maturity and the quality of the pretrained VLM features rather than to large-scale robot-action pretraining which we find to be not necessary. The key implication is deployment latency: N multiplies action-head compute at every replanning step, limiting control frequency. A single Euler step removes this overhead with negligible impact on success rate, enabling low-latency deployment of existing flow-matching VLA checkpoints with-out retraining, distillation, or auxiliary objectives. The same property makes single-backward-pass actor updates attractive for RL post-training without differentiating through the integration chain. Converting this into a concrete recipe is a promising direction for future work. 9Acknowledgments

B.2 McNemar’s test 

Since our real world evaluation setup is paired, i.e., the 1-step and reference-step approaches are tested under the same initial conditions and seeded policy noise, we can treat the binary outcome of each trial (success/failure) as paired observations and employ the two-sided McNemar test [42] to assess if the difference in performance is statistically significant. E Per-task testing 

Following the approach in [36], we use violin plots to visualise the uncertainty in the success rate estimation. The violin plots represent a Bayesian posterior probability under a uniform Beta prior given the observed success rate and total number of runs. We model the binary outcome 

yi ∼ Bernoulli (θ) and θ ∼ Beta (1 , 1) . Given N rollouts with binary outcomes Y = {y1, . . . , y N },assuming we recorded s successes in N trials, the posterior probability is 

p(θ|Y ) ∝ Beta (s + 1 , N − s + 1) , (E1) and the violin plots are made with points sampled from Eq. (E1). The comparison is carried out using the STEP framework [43], with the Bonferroni correction over the per-task family of hypotheses controlling the family-wise error rate at αglobal = 0 .05 , hence running task-level tests with αsplit = αglobal /K where K is the family size. We note that we use Bonferroni correction here instead of Holm correction as in Sec. B.2 since the STEP framework does not return a p-value like McNemar, but a decision at a given α. We use K = 10 for LIBERO and LIBERO+, K = 50 for RoboTwin, K = 4 for the Real World suite, while for CALVIN we do not apply Bonferroni correction. 20 In the violin plots, we use compact letter display to mark a statistically significant difference. Violins with different letters and marked with ⋆⋆ were separable at the given level of confidence αsplit after Bonferroni correction. Violins sharing the same letter but with a single ⋆, are separable at αglobal =0.05 but are not after Bonferroni correction. Violins that share the same letter and do not have a ⋆

are not separable.
