# ABSTRACT 

Flow matching learns a generative velocity field by regression, but under distribution drift a field must be deployed before the current data arrive. We study this predictive problem for a fixed, possibly nonconvex, neural class, with dynamic regret against the best represented field of each round. We propose DyProd-FM, which refits flow matching on dyadic historical prefixes and selects among the fits with a sleeping Prod master. Without knowing any drift budget, it attains regret eO(min {Nω +1, 1 + √T Ω}) in the relative velocity risk variation Ω and eO(min {Nμ + 1 , 1 + T 1/3V 2/3

Prior optimal dynamic-regret guarantees rely on a convex decision set with strongly convex or exp-concave losses, whereas our class may be nonconvex and no curvature in its parameters is assumed. The target-law rate rests on a second-order link between the two variations, which the base noise of flow matching provides. 

# 1 Introduction 

Flow matching constructs a generative model by regressing a velocity field along a path from a base distribution to a target distribution (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Albergo et al., 2025). Its statistical analysis usually fixes the target while observations accumulate. In a changing environment, this separation between training and deployment is insufficient: a field learned from a large historical sample may accurately solve an obsolete regression problem. Conversely, discarding history after every observation forfeits the statistical benefit of repeated sampling. A predictive theory must quantify both effects under the information available when a field is deployed. We study nonstationary stochastic flow matching with independent, fresh batches. A learner selects one represented field using past batches, then receives the current batch and scores every active candidate. Its benchmark is the best represented field for the current population, which may change every round. Learning rounds, along which the environment changes, are distinct from flow time, along which a selected field generates a sample. How should drift be measured? The relative velocity risk variation Ω measures how drift changes the comparison between represented fields. It is class-relative and ignores changes the class cannot see. The target-law variation 

Vμ = P 

> t

W2(μt+1 , μ t) measures drift of the generative target itself. It is class-free and estimable from samples. We determine the minimax rate under both measures, up to logarithmic factors, for a fixed class and fixed problem scales. Three difficulties arise. Historical batches are independent but heterogeneous, so one common comparator need not fit all past velocities. Historical fitting attains O(1 /n ) with n examples, but standard online selection adds order √T

regret and erases this gain. First-order conversion of target drift into risk drift discards squared-loss curvature and yields only pT V μ. We must resolve all three for a nonconvex class without parameter-curvature assumptions. Section 5 resolves them in turn through historical estimation (Section 5.1), fast selection (Section 5.2), and drift transfer followed by horizon partitions (Sections 5.3–5.5).

The fast historical rate uses the Bernstein-type variance control of squared-loss flow matching (Sahoo et al., 2026), in the approximate form implied by the regression identity, and our lower bounds build on the rare-mixture construction of the same work. What is new is the nonstationary part. (i) Histories are independent but not identically distributed, and their common comparator carries a drift-induced residual even when every round is realizable (Proposition 1). (ii) The same squared-loss structure bounds the second moment of the master’s score differences by velocity risks, so selecting among the fits costs a polylogarithmic factor in T per lifetime, rather than the square root of the lifetime’s length. (iii) Relative-risk oscillation gives first-order historical transfer in Ω, while base smoothing and approximate quadratic growth give second-order transfer in Vμ. (iv) The two-point constructions become block constructions for dynamic regret that keep every class constant fixed. 

# 2 Related Work 

Flow matching. Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023, 2024), rectified flow (Liu et al., 2023), and stochastic interpolants (Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden, 2023; Albergo et al., 2025) learn transport velocities by regression. Fixed-target theory relates velocity accuracy to sampling error (Benton et al., 2024) and bounds statistical errors and sample complexity (Zhou and Liu, 2025; Gaur et al., 2025). Sahoo et al. (2026) obtain order-optimal fixed-target rates from Bernstein-type variance control, with a rare-mixture lower bound. Nonuniform flow-time laws are standard in large-scale training (Esser et al., 2024). We ask instead how much excess current velocity error accumulates when deployment precedes each fresh batch and the target drifts. 

Dynamic regret. Tracking changing comparators is classical (Herbster and Warmuth, 1998; Zinkevich, 2003; Besbes et al., 2015). Our analysis builds on strongly adaptive interval guarantees (Daniely et al., 2015; Jun et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2018; Wei et al., 2016) and on Prod with sleeping experts (Gaillard et al., 2014). For n observations with total variation Cn under squared loss, Baby and Wang (2019) establish the minimax rate n1/3C2/3 

> n

, and Baby and Wang (2021, 2022) attain such rates with proper learners for exp-concave and strongly convex losses over convex decision sets. Our target-law rate has the same exponents for a nonconvex neural class, where the curvature comes from the flow-matching regression identity and base smoothing rather than from the loss geometry in parameter space. We also use fast-rate arguments (Bartlett et al., 2005; Srebro et al., 2010; Boucheron et al., 2013) and two-point lower bounds (Tsybakov, 2009; Sahoo et al., 2026). Appendix A gives the extended discussion.

# A Extended Related Work 

This appendix expands Section 2. It places each line of work relative to the three ingredients of this paper: drifting targets in flow matching, budget-free dynamic regret for a nonconvex class, and the two measures of nonstationarity. 

A.1 Flow Matching, Rectified Flow, and Stochastic Interpolants 

Lipman et al. (2023) introduce flow matching, which learns a transport velocity by regressing on conditional velocities along a prescribed probability path. Rectified flow (Liu et al., 2023) uses linear interpolation between base and target samples and favors nearly straight transport paths. Stochastic interpolants (Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden, 2023; Albergo et al., 2025) formulate flows and diffusions through interpolation maps, possibly with additional latent noise, and characterize the drift by the same kind of conditional-mean regression. The guide of Lipman et al. (2024) surveys paths, schedules, and implementations. All of these frameworks use the regression identity that underlies Equation (6). Assumption 2 covers the linear and sine–cosine paths, and the affine schedules of Assumption 5 include both. Interpolants with additional latent noise, nonaffine interpolations, and couplings other than independent pairing are outside the present target-law analysis. The flow-time law is also a modeling choice in practice. Esser et al. (2024) sample flow time from a logit-normal law when training rectified-flow transformers at scale, and such laws satisfy Assumption 5(c) (Appendix E.1.9). 

A.2 Statistical Theory of Flow Matching 

Benton et al. (2024) relate the sampling error of flow-matching methods to the accuracy of the learned velocity, under regularity of the learned flow. Zhou and Liu (2025) give an error analysis of flow matching for deep generative modeling with fixed targets, including bounded-support distributions and stronger score-regularity regimes. Gaur et al. (2025) study the sample complexity of flow matching and separate approximation, estimation, and optimization errors. These results motivate the error decomposition used here, with regret, approximation error, and fitting error kept separate. We retain an explicit approximate-global fitting interface (Assumption 4) rather than proving convergence of a generic neural optimizer. Sahoo et al. (2026) obtain order-optimal sample complexity for rectified flows. Their analysis rests on Bernstein-type variance control for the squared flow-matching loss, a localized Rademacher bound, and a rare-mixture lower bound. Our Assumptions 1–4 follow the same setting. The difference is the protocol. These works study one fixed target and i.i.d. samples, whereas we study a drifting target, predictive deployment before each fresh batch, heterogeneous historical pools, and dynamic regret against the best represented field of every round. Our question is therefore different: how much excess current velocity error accumulates when deployment precedes each fresh batch and the target changes? Appendix C.1.5 compares our direct historical estimation with the localized analysis. 

A.3 Nonstationary Online Learning and Dynamic Regret 

Tracking the best expert under switches (Herbster and Warmuth, 1998) and dynamic regret for online convex optimization against moving comparators (Zinkevich, 2003) are the classical starting points. Besbes et al. (2015) study non-stationary stochastic optimization under a budget on the temporal variation of the loss functions. Our relative velocity risk variation is a class-relative budget of the same functional type, measured by the half-oscillation of risk increments so that changes common to all represented fields are free. The target-law variation is different in kind: it is a budget on the targets themselves, not on the losses or on the comparators. Strongly adaptive methods control regret on every interval. Daniely et al. (2015) use geometric covering intervals, and Jun et al. (2017) improve strongly adaptive rates through coin betting. Zhang et al. (2018) show how interval guarantees yield dynamic regret by partitioning the horizon according to functional variation. Our dyadic lifetimes follow the geometric-covering idea, and our analysis also partitions the horizon only in the proof. Wei et al. (2016) track the best expert in non-stationary stochastic environments and show how statistical variance interacts with switching and variation budgets. This stochastic-expert view is close to our fresh-batch protocol, in which one batch scores every active candidate. We adapt these ingredients to stochastic population risks of data-dependent historical fits, and we select a single field at every round so that the deployed field stays in the represented class. 13 Predictive Flow Matching under Distribution Drift The master of DyProd-FM is the Prod algorithm with sleeping experts. Gaillard et al. (2014) prove second-order regret bounds in terms of excess losses for multiple-rate Prod and give the sleeping-expert reduction. With one rate, the selection probabilities are normalized weights (Appendix B.2.1). Our contribution at this step is the population analysis: the squared flow-matching loss bounds the second moment of the score differences by velocity risks, which turns the potential inequality into the fast master bound of Lemma C.5.

C.2.4 Resource Accounting and Computational Scope 

Let N = 2 ⌈log 2 T ⌉ denote the containing power of two. There are 2N − 1 < 4T binary-tree nodes, so Me < 4T .
