Abstract 

Conditional generation is a key capability to be supported by Large Tabular Models, enabling a number of applications. Accurate conditional generation has to contend with two key challenges: (i) Data comprise a mixture of numerical and categorical entries, the latter often with high cardinality and heavily unbalanced proportions; ( ii ) Conditional generation becomes increasingly difficult for rare sub-populations. The best models in this class use a hybrid of continuous and discrete diffusions. In this paper, we design a continuous diffusion model with strong generation quality. An important element is the use of cross-entropy loss for categoricals, for which we provide a first theoretical justification. We introduce metrics that focus on rare subpopulations. Further, we generalize conditional generation techniques to enable new types of conditioning. Finally, we show that our model (trained on labor data) can capture classical insights from econometrics. Code is available at https://github.com/granica-ai/DiffLTM .

# 1 Introduction 

The term Large Tabular Model (LTM) was coined by [VBVDS24] to denote generative models for tabular data with cross-dataset modeling capabilities, enabling a variety of tasks. Among the key challenges outlined in [VBVDS24], we highlight three: (i) Tabular data present a mixture of categorical and numerical data, and categoricals are often high-cardinality or heavily skewed. (ii ) Standard evaluation metrics (e.g., FID, precision-recall) are often hard to interpret or misleading in the tabular setting, and synthetic samples are hard to interpret. (iii ) Conditional generation is arguably the most important capability for such models, as it enables a number of applications: forecasting; scenario analysis; imputation, and so on. Accurate conditional generation is particularly important and challenging for infrequently occurring conditions. In this paper, we address these challenges. As a result, we design a generative tabular model with state of the art conditional generation behavior (Figure 1). We train the model on sizable census datasets (up to 7M rows), and compare it with alternative approaches using newly designed faithfulness metrics. In the rest of this introduction we summarize some of our innovations, contrasting them with earlier literature and providing pointers to the rest of the paper. 

Metrics. An important concern with tabular data is that humans do not have good intuition of when the generated data ‘look good.’ This is in sharp contrast with images or language models. We address this challenge in two ways. First, we define interpretable fidelity metrics that emphasize the accuracy on rare subpopulations (see Section 2.2). While in hindsight this is a natural idea, previous work uses metrics that are dominated by majority classes [SXH +25, MGF25]. 

Second, we argue that an LTM must correctly reproduce non-obvious statistical patterns in the target population. To implement this idea, we consider two major discoveries in econometrics: counterfactual analysis using synthetic controls and Mincer equation on returns to education. In a sense, checking such patterns is the analogue of the ‘6-fingers test’ for image generation. We demonstrate that our LTM reproduces these patterns (see Figure 2). 

Architecture. We develop a diffusion LTM (DiffLTM). Given the mixture of categorical and numerical data in tabular datasets, an important choice is whether to use discrete or continuous diffusion, or a hybrid. 1.1 Further related work 

The literature on generative models for tabular data is rapidly growing. We provide a few pointers beyond the ones mentioned above. Evaluation is well recognized to be an outstanding challenge because of the heterogeneity of tabular data [NEMP +25]. Metrics based on statistical accuracy [AVBSVDS22], downstream utility of synthetic data [XSCIV19], latent causal relationships [JSJ25] have been developed. A variety of techniques and ideas have been brought to bear to develop generative models for tabular data. Among others, generative adversarial networks [XSCIV19], graph neural networks [LQBvdS23], based on autoregressive models [BSL +22], latent space diffusions [ZZS +24], variational flow matching [GCEVDM25]. Finally, a small number of recent papers develop models for relational databases beyond the single-table setting [HXS +25, KLSG26, SSB26].

TabRep and TabCascade are close comparison points since they are based on continuous diffusions. DiffLTM outperforms them on most metrics and all datasets. All datasets considered in [SOQ +26, SXH +25] have less than 100K rows; and all datasets but one in [MGF26] have less than 102K rows, with one exception comprising 540K rows. In contrast our study focuses on moderate-size datasets with ∼ 5M rows.

We study conditional generation using DiffLTM with two approaches: Classifier-free guidance (CFG) [HS22], and Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) [DDFG01]. We extend CFG to range and linear conditions, and compare them with baselines in Figure 1.

Training. During CFG training [HS22], we need to sample a condition C. Selected categorical columns are always assigned equality conditions. For each selected numerical column, the equality condition is replaced by a range condition with probability 

prng . To construct a linear condition, we sample the number of participating numerical columns, mlin ,uniformly in {1, . . . , m num } and then select mlin numerical columns. With probability plin , we impose 10 a linear condition over these selected columns and remove any equality or range conditions previously assigned to them. Equality conditions are constructed by setting the conditioned value of each selected column i to its value xi in the corresponding training row. For numerical range conditions, we must ensure that the optimal conditional denoiser targets the appropriate truncated distribution, ptarget (x) ∝ p(x) 1{Li <xi < U i}. Specifically, we first sample an interval width ∆ ∼ LogUnif(∆ min , ∆max ), then sample the lower bound as Li ∼ Unif( xi − ∆, x i), and set Ui = Li + ∆. For a linear condition involving mlin numerical columns, we sample the corresponding weight vector as w ∼ N (0, I mlin ). We then construct the bounds

4.2 Inference-time methods 

We experiment with two inference-time methods: rejection sampling and sequential Monte Carlo (SMC). Rejection sampling is unbiased but highly inefficient for rare conditions, so we use it as a reference (Rejection in Figure 1). SMC is a class of methods widely used across statistics and signal processing to perform posterior sampling in sequential systems [DDFG01], which was recently used in conjunction with diffusion models [WTN +23]. At a high level, SMC entails initializing K reverse-diffusion trajectories and reweighting them in accordance with how likely each trajectory is to eventually produce a synthetic row satisfying the requested condition (see Algorithm 3 in Appendix G for details)—the main algorithmic question that then arises is how to approximate this probability. To this end, we make three approximations: ( i)columns are conditionally independent given the current trajectory state; ( ii ) categorical columns follow the distribution induced by the predicted denoiser logits; and ( iii ) numerical columns follow a Gaussian distribution centered at the denoiser prediction. We note that choosing logit mode enables assumption (ii ), adding to the previously outlined reasons supporting this choice. SMC enables conditioning on categorical predicates as well as arbitrary numerical conditions, a capability not offered by prior work such as Harpoon [SWHC26].

# 7 Discussion and limitations 

We presented the design of a generative model for tabular data, based on continuous diffusions. The model has state of the art capabilities in generation and conditional generation. An important methodological element is the use of learned embeddings and CE loss for categoricals, for which we provide a mathematical rationale. Finally, we trained our model on datasets that are one order of magnitude larger than previous published work, and demonstrated the ability to recover econometric patterns, providing complementary information to earlier validations. An important missing element is cross-datasets modeling. In preliminary work, we trained a cross-datasets version of our architecture, but defer this to a future report. An important limitation to our econometrics application is that we did not develop rigorous statistical guard-rails for our approach. We consider it an important avenue for future work.

# B Unconditional generation: Experiment details 

In this section, we provide a full description of the experiment setup leading to the results reported in Tables 1 and 2. We recall that the model obtained by the architecture/training choices of ( i) learnt embeddings, ( ii ) logits mode and ( iii ) CE loss is utilized in Table 1 to compare against baselines. 

B.1 Architecture parameters 

When used, the learned embedding matrix is implemented using PyTorch’s nn.Embedding . In all such experiments we set the embedding dimension to demb = 16. Numerical columns are transformed using a quantile transformation. The concatenated noisy representation rt is first linearly projected to a hidden width of dh = 1024, which is subsequently processed by a shared MLP comprising L = 5 fully connected hidden layers of width dh = 1024 and using SiLU activations. The diffusion time t is encoded using a 1024-dimensional sinusoidal embedding, which is further processed by two fully connected layers of width 1024 with SiLU activations. This time embedding is added to the projected input representation before it is passed through the shared MLP. On the ACSLabour dataset, the unconditional model contains approximately 9 .84M parameters, including the learned embedding tables. 

B.2 Training 

All the DiffLTM variants are trained for 2000 epochs with a batch size 8192 and AdamW ( β1 = 0 .9and β2 = 0 .999). We use an initial learning rate of 3 × 10 −4 with a cosine decay schedule. We use the noise schedule defined above with ( σmin , σ max , ρ ) = (0 .002 , 80 , 7). Unconditional and CFG-based conditional generation use 100 Euler steps. Following earlier work [SSDK +21], we use exponential moving average(EMA) of denoiser parameters with decay of 0 .997 for evaluation. The final checkpoint is selected using lowest validation loss. All experiments were run on a single node with 192 CPU cores and 8 NVIDIA A100 80 GB GPUs, using Ubuntu 22.04 and CUDA 13.0. We train each model using three different random seeds and use bfloat16 (bf16) precision for all training runs. Each individual run, including both training and evaluation, takes less than 12 GPU-hours. 25 B.3 Baselines implementation 

We benchmark DiffLTM against three recent state-of-the-art generative models for tabular data. Two of these use continuous diffusion (TabRep [SOQ +26] and TabCascade [MGF26]), while TabDiff [SXH +25] is based on mixed diffusion. To ensure a fair comparison, we use the official implementations released by the authors: 

• For TabRep, we use the flow-matching variant, TabRep-Flow, available at https://github.com/ jacobyhsi/TabRep .

• For TabCascade, we use the implementation available at https://github.com/muellermarkus/ tabcascade .

• For TabDiff, we use the implementation from https://github.com/minkaixu/tabdiff . We ob-serve that, with the default recommended learning rate, training becomes unstable after a few optimization steps, with gradient norms increasing sharply and failing to recover. We therefore use a lower learning rate of 3 × 10 −4 together with gradient-norm clipping at 1 .0, which yields stable training and substantially better performance. We retain the original denoiser architecture; in contrast, some prior comparisons modify TabDiff by removing its Transformer layers. Importantly, we use the exact same training, validation, and test splits as for DiffLTM. We train each model end-to-end using its official implementation and generate a synthetic dataset containing ntrain rows, matching the size of the real training set. Regardless of the generation method, all synthetic datasets are evaluated using the same standardized evaluation setup described in the subsequent Appendix B.4. We note that the default training budgets used in the official implementations are substantially smaller, potentially due to their evaluation on smaller datasets. To match the training budget, we therefore train all models for 2000 epochs with batch size of 8192 and select the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss. 

Training time comparison. To quantify the training-efficiency gains of DiffLTM over TabDiff, we conduct an isolated profiling experiment on the ACSLabour dataset. For both models, we measure the wall-clock time over 100 gradient steps after 20 warm-up steps, using a batch size of 8,192 on the same NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB GPU. Averaged over five runs, TabDiff requires 78 .03 ms per gradient step, compared with 34 .98 ms for DiffLTM. Using BF16, as in all of our experiments in this paper, further reduces the DiffLTM step time to 14 .40 ms, leading to a 5 .4× speedup over TabDiff. 

B.4 Metrics computation

ntest synthetic rows and compare them against the held-out real test set. The MLE metrics involve randomness in fitting the downstream prediction model; we account for this by repeating both TRTR and TSTR evaluations 10 times. More precisely, in each round we sample ntest 

rows without replacement from the real training set for TRTR and from the synthetic pool for TSTR, fit a fresh XGBoost model, and evaluate it on the full real test set. We separately average the 10 TRTR and 10 TSTR scores and compute their ratio to obtain the final MLE score. # E Reparametrization in synthetic control details 

As alluded to in Section 5 and illustrated in Figure 4, synthetic control can be very brittle, in particular showing sharp performance degradation when the target variable is non-linearly transformed. We now precisely describe the experimental setup underlying this illustration. 

E.1 Data distribution 

To study this question in a controlled setting, we construct a synthetic dataset with state S ∈ { 1, . . . , 11 },time period T ∈ { 0, . . . , 10 }, and exogenous noise U ∼ Unif[ −√3, √3]. The outcome is generated as 

Y = μS + μT + σU, 

where 

μT = 11 10 · · · 1

μS = 0 0.02 · · · 0.08 10 19 .92 19 .94 · · · 20 .0

σ = 0 .5.

We draw 1 ,200 independent rows for each ( S, T ) pair (by sampling 1 , 200 corresponding U i.i.d.), giving a 4-column table with columns ( Y, S, T, U ). State 6 is subject to an intervention during periods 

T ∈ { 8, 9, 10 }. The construction is chosen so that state 6, for which μ6 = 10, lies between two clustered groups of donor states. The task is to learn a DiffLTM-CFG model on this table of four columns and then conditionally generate Y for the unseen slices which is S = 6 and T ∈ { 8, 9, 10 }.To study robustness to reparameterization, we transform the outcome Y using a function f (Y ). The training data are then given by the table ( f (Y ), S, T, U ). The three experiments in Figure 4 correspond to three choices of f : (i) f (y) = y, (ii) f (y) = log y, and (iii) f (y) = y(max( y) − y). The third setting is included to evaluate robustness under a non-monotone reparameterization, where max( y) denotes the maximum value of Y in the training set. Figure 4 compares the median of the distribution generated by our model with the median of the observed data and the median of the distribution generated by synthetic control. 

E.2 Training and generation 

Along with learned embeddings and logit parameterization, we use cross-entropy (CE) loss for the cat-egorical features ( S and T ) and mean-squared error (MSE) loss for the numerical features ( Y and U ). Given the smaller dataset sizes, we use a smaller model for these experiments with embedding dimension 

demb = 4, hidden width dh = 256, and depth L = 1, resulting in approximately 350 ,000 trainable parame-ters. Unlike the other experiments, we standardize the numerical columns rather than applying a quantile transformation. The model is trained for 1 ,000 epochs with a batch size of 4096, and the final checkpoint 32 is selected based on the minimum EMA validation loss. Following prior work [PX23], we additionally use MLP blocks with adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN) [PSDV +18]. Specifically, the time embedding is used to predict per-layer scale and shift parameters, which are applied to the layer-normalized features before each linear layer. All other training and architectural details follow Appendix B. For each choice of f (Y ), we repeat the experiment across three training seeds. For each trained model, we condition on S = 6 and each T ∈ { 0, . . . , 10 }, generating 100 ,000 samples per ( S, T ) condition. In Figure 4, we report the mean of the generated medians across the three training seeds, with error bars denoting the corresponding standard error.
