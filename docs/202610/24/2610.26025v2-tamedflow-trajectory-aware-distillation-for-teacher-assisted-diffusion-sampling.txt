ABSTRACT 

Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) deliver high-fidelity image and video generation, but inference requires many sequential model evaluations. Two acceleration strate-gies are increasingly important: model compression makes each evaluation cheaper, while adaptive inference avoids selected teacher computation through reuse, fore-casting, or speculative execution. Their combination, however, is brittle: directly applying the evaluated adaptive methods to a compressed student can degrade quality, an observed failure mode we call the Combination Gap . We link this gap to a mismatch between pointwise distillation and short-horizon execution: a student trained at teacher states need not remain reliable when its own predictions are chained. We propose TamedFlow , which trains a compressed DiT for this deployed role through cross-architecture feature alignment, a near-identity Velocity Alignment Head, and randomized rollout supervision. At inference, a lightweight Learnable Trajectory Estimator routes completed drafts: accepted drafts bypass the teacher, while rejected drafts roll back and receive one teacher rescue step. Dis-tilling DiT-XL/2 into DiT-B/2, TamedFlow reaches FID 2.58 at 2.25 × measured speedup over DDIM-50 on ImageNet 256 × 256 . Ablations support trajectory su-pervision, and routing controls favor LTE over random decisions at nearly matched latency. Evaluations on ImageNet 512 × 512 , MS-COCO, and Latte/FaceForensics extend the empirical scope. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) have become a leading backbone for high-fidelity visual genera-tion (Peebles & Xie, 2023), yet iterative denoising imposes high inference latency. Each sample requires dozens of sequential forward passes, with costs scaling rapidly with model parameters, resolution, and sequence length. Existing acceleration strategies attack complementary aspects of this cost: step-reduction methods shorten the solver schedule (Song et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2023; Geng et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2025); parameter compression lowers per-step computation; and adaptive inference reuses or forecasts intermediate activations (Ma et al., 2024b; Selvaraju et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025a;b). Combining parameter compression with adaptive execution is intuitively appealing, promising cheaper evaluations while skipping redundant computation. All headline experiments below use fixed-step DDIM integration to evaluate compression and adaptive execution under a unified solver convention. In practice, this composition can break down. In our controlled diagnostic (Appendix Table 11), attaching the evaluated adaptive reuse methods directly to a compressed student degrades sample quality, inflating ImageNet 256 × 256 FID from 5.56 to between 10 .40 and 13 .18 . We term this failure mode the Combination Gap . Rather than applying internal reuse unchanged to the compact model, we let the student generate short trajectory drafts and reserve teacher intervention for drafts predicted to deviate. Chaining student predictions into drafts, however, introduces compounding integration drift. Propo-sition 3.1 characterizes this through an Euler rollout bound: spatial sensitivity can amplify local student–teacher velocity mismatch over sequential steps. To stabilize drafting against this ampli-fication, we introduce TamedFlow , combining trajectory-aware distillation with selective teacher rescue (Figure 1). During training, cross-architecture feature alignment (AlignNet) grounds student representations in teacher layers; a near-identity Velocity Alignment Head (VAH) absorbs residual velocity errors; and randomized K-step rollout supervision ( K ∼ U (1 , 5) ) penalizes both interme-diate mismatch and accumulated endpoint drift. A compact Learnable Trajectory Estimator (LTE, 4M parameters) scores completed drafts from pooled student tokens and timestep embeddings without teacher access. If confidence exceeds threshold τ , the draft is accepted, advancing 

K steps while bypassing the teacher. If rejected, the sampler rolls back to the pre-draft state and advances one step with teacher rescue. Varying τ selects among measured operating points between throughput and fidelity without sequential teacher verification. 2.58 at 2.25 × measured speedup ( 0.464 s vs. 1.044 s) over the DDIM-50 teacher. In summary, our contributions are: • Diagnostic and analysis of the Combination Gap. We document the failure of applying the evaluated adaptive reuse methods to a compressed DiT, and derive a DDIM rollout bound characterizing how sequential drafting can amplify local velocity errors. • Trajectory-stabilized speculative distillation. We introduce TamedFlow, uniting feature align-ment, residual velocity correction, and randomized rollout supervision to optimize a compressed DiT for autonomous multi-step drafting. • Learned routing and deployment evaluation. We propose an asymmetric execution policy via a 4M-parameter LTE to accept reliable drafts or trigger teacher rescue, evaluating quality–latency trade-offs across resolutions, text conditioning, and video alongside physical deployment costs. 22 RELATED WORK 

Prior work on DiT acceleration reduces the number of solver evaluations, the cost of each evaluation, or the teacher computation retained during adaptive execution. TamedFlow studies their interface: whether a compressed student can reliably replace short segments of a teacher trajectory. 2.1 STEP COUNT REDUCTION IN DIFFUSION AND FLOW MODELS 

Step reduction lowers the number of solver evaluations. Higher-order solvers improve numerical efficiency for a fixed model (Song et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2025), while distillation changes the model: progressive distillation shortens the schedule (Salimans & Ho, 2022), consistency models match predictions across noise levels (Song et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2023), MeanFlow and Model-Guidance alter the target or absorb guidance (Geng et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2025). Other formulations align distributions or scores, distill autoregressively, transfer trajectories, or train few-step students adversarially (Yin et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025; Sauer et al., 2024). These methods optimize a sparse standalone sampler deployed entirely on its own. TamedFlow instead retains a multi-step teacher as a reliable safeguard and uses the student to propose short trajectory segments whose acceptance is decided adaptively online. A related line studies trajectory geometry. Rectified flow and InstaFlow construct straighter paths (Liu et al., 2023); interpolant transformers unify diffusion and flow parametrizations (Ma et al., 2024a); and guided path sampling corrects trajectories during integration (Li et al., 2026). TCD and Hyper-SD learn consistency or trajectory segments for few-step sampling (Zheng et al., 2024; Ren et al., 2024). TamedFlow keeps the teacher path as the reference, trains a cross-architecture student for selectively accepted rollouts, and retains teacher rescue in the deployed sampler. 2.2 PER -S TEP COMPUTE ACCELERATION AND FEATURE CACHING 

Per-step methods keep the schedule while avoiding full recomputation. Feature-reuse methods cache activations across timesteps or blocks (Ma et al., 2024b; Chen et al., 2024; Selvaraju et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2025; 2026b; Wimbauer et al., 2024); forecasting methods extrapolate future features (Liu et al., 2025a;b; Cai et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2025); and learned schedulers select policies per sample (Zhao et al., 2026). These methods exploit local regularity in intermediate computations. Our same-student diagnostic shows that their evaluated configurations do not transfer directly to the compressed student, but does not isolate a universal caching failure mechanism. Most reuse methods leave the model unchanged and decide which internal computations can be skipped. TamedFlow instead trains the student under rollout supervision and learns whether an entire student-generated segment can advance without a teacher call. DisCa (Zou et al., 2026a) also studies incompatibility between step-distilled video DiTs and feature caches, but repairs the cache side with a predictor and Restricted MeanFlow schedule. TamedFlow acts on the draft student instead. 2.3 SPECULATIVE VERIFICATION IN GENERATIVE PARADIGMS 

Speculative decoding verifies proposed tokens with a target model (Leviathan et al., 2023); causal masking permits parallel verification and exact correction. Diffusion lacks this structure because each teacher state depends on the preceding prediction. Replaying a K-step draft exactly would require sequential teacher evaluations and remove much of the saving. SpecDiff (Pan et al., 2026) studies self-speculative diffusion under this constraint. TamedFlow replaces exact verification with learned trajectory estimation. The LTE scores student-internal features after a draft using rollout agreement as its training target. Accepted drafts avoid the teacher; rejected drafts return to the pre-draft state for one teacher step. Routing is therefore inexpensive and online, but approximate and not distribution-preserving by construction. 33 METHOD

Models and datasets. The primary setting distills a 675M DiT-XL/2 teacher Tϕ (Peebles & Xie, 2023) into a 130M DiT-B/2 student Bθ on class-conditional ImageNet at 256 × 256 . Quality–latency operating points. Table 1 compares complete systems across speedup regimes. Four-step distillation baselines attain aggressive 14 ×–15 × acceleration at the cost of severe FID degradation. TamedFlow instead targets moderate acceleration with tight quality retention: at 

τ = 0 .85 , it matches the DDIM-50 teacher within 0.02 FID ( 2.33 vs. 2.35 ) at 1.73 × speedup; at default τ = 0 .80 , it reaches FID 2.58 at 2.25 × speedup ( 0.464 s). In the 2.0× to 2.5× range under nearly matched latencies around 0.46 to 0.48 seconds, TamedFlow achieves better quality than FoCa at FID 2.65 and TaylorSeer at FID 2.73 , alongside a higher Inception Score. This represents a quality advantage at matched latency rather than a throughput claim from millisecond-scale differences. Although SpeCa reports lower backbone FLOPs of 4.76 T, its FID is 

0.20 worse, and backbone FLOPs omit runtime overheads like rollback, VAH, and LTE that are fully captured by measured latency. Thus, TamedFlow’s edge lies in its empirical Pareto trade-off for the deployed system, not a FLOPs-only claim.

Draft length K and threshold τ balance speedup against fidelity (Appendix Figure 10). Speedup peaks at K = 3 because longer drafts compound drift and increase rollback costs upon rejection. As shown in Figure 4, LTE confidence remains high in the mid-noise regime but drops near the plotted noise level 0.22, invoking more teacher rescues late in sampling. The distribution shows clear operational separation between accepted and rejected drafts at the chosen operating point τ = 0 .80 .In the reference trace at K = 3 and τ = 0 .80 , 14 of 22 routing events are accepted, allowing student rollouts to cover 42 of the 50 integration steps. In this work, we introduced TamedFlow, an acceleration framework that reconciles parameter compression with adaptive execution for Diffusion Transformers. By training a compact student for short-horizon trajectory agreement and employing asymmetric routing with selective teacher rescue, TamedFlow achieves FID 2.58 at 2.25 × speedup over DDIM-50 on ImageNet 256 × 256 , with transfer evaluated across resolutions, text conditioning, and video. Our findings show that pointwise alignment can leave compounding trajectory divergence during autonomous drafting, creating the Combination Gap. Controlled ablations support rollout supervision, and routing controls favor LTE over random decisions at nearly matched latency, suggesting that compression and adaptive inference can be made compatible when the compact model is trained as a multi-step drafter and the sampler retains a recovery path back to the teacher.

# Appendix E Efficiency and Transfer Accounting 

Deployment footprint. In a controlled A800, batch-size-2, FP32, 50-step latent-only profile with VAE decoding excluded, peak allocated VRAM is 2.570 GB for the teacher, 2.580 GB for FoCa (N = 3 ), 3.066 GB for TaylorSeer ( N = 3 , O = 3 ), and 3.075 GB for TamedFlow. Co-deploying the student and LTE therefore adds about 0.5 GB relative to the teacher-only profile. For the ImageNet and MS-COCO image experiments, all methods use classifier-free guidance at scale 3.5. The teacher FLOP reference includes the complete conditional and unconditional CFG evaluation, while the CFG-distilled student reference counts one student forward; this convention defines the teacher/student references and the TamedFlow backbone estimates. 

Routing accounting. In the reference trace at τ = 0 .80 , 14 of 22 routing events are accepted and 8 are rejected, giving a 63.6% event acceptance rate. Since each accepted event advances K = 3 

Student steps, accepted drafts cover 42 /50 = 84% of the nominal trajectory; the remaining 8 steps use Teacher rescue. The separately measured component profile records 0.07075 s for accepted-draft buckets (14.9%), 0.06031 s for rejected-draft buckets including rollback (12.7%), 0.34142 s for Teacher rescue (71.9%), and 0.00237 s for LTE scoring (0.5%). These buckets sum to 0.47485 s (up to rounding). They are event-level timing buckets from the profiling run, so dividing a bucket by the number of backbone calls would incorrectly attribute rollback and routing overhead to an individual forward. The component profile and the headline latency measurement were collected in different runs: the 0.464 s headline is the separately measured ten-run average. We therefore report both traces without using one to reconstruct the other.

# Appendix H Inference Details 

Velocity-field interface and guidance. The symbol vT denotes the effective teacher right-hand side used by the Euler solver, including the prescribed conditioning and guidance, rather than specifying the raw prediction type of the underlying DiT. Student–teacher velocity losses and teacher reference rollouts are expressed in this same solver coordinate system. The single-branch student is distilled toward the guided teacher field; a teacher rescue still evaluates the complete guided teacher update. The linear-interpolant objective in Section 3.1 introduces the velocity-form convention and, by itself, does not identify the pretrained teacher’s native output parameterization or noise schedule. An architecture name alone does not determine a checkpoint or an output-to-ODE conversion. 

Time coordinates. The solver and all rollout equations use increasing flow time t, with noise at 

t = 0 and data at t = 1 . Figure 4 retains a decreasing noise-level plotting coordinate on its upper axis; that axis label is not the forward-time variable in the solver equations. The reported value 0.22 describes that plotted noise coordinate, not forward flow time. Sampling-step indices are used to compare the routing profile with execution order. Algorithm 1 gives the full routing loop under the forward flow-time convention t0 < · · · < t N . The student drafts up to K steps without teacher access, and the LTE scores the draft from the last student forward, the same type of input used during LTE training. Rejection restores the pre-draft state before one teacher rescue step; no endpoint backbone call is added for scoring.

I.1 Routing-control protocol and call accounting 

The controls in Table 9 use the same trained student checkpoint, VAH, 50-step Euler grid, CFG configuration, and ImageNet FID-50K protocol. Latency follows the single-A800, batch-one protocol in the main experiments. These measurements evaluate inference policies without retraining the student. 

Static policies. Pure Student performs all 50 updates with the student and never calls the teacher. Random Steps independently selects a set of eight distinct integration steps per trajectory before sampling and replaces those student updates with teacher updates. Fixed Periodic uses the one-based integration-step indices 6, 12 , 18 , 24 , 30 , 36 , 42 , 48 . Neither static policy executes a speculative student draft at a teacher-designated step. Both therefore execute exactly 42 student forwards and eight teacher updates. 

Budget-constrained random decisions. Random Accept uses the same K = 3 draft-and-rollback structure as LTE, but substitutes a random accept decision with nominal probability p = 0 .636 .The policy enforces exactly eight teacher updates per trajectory. Once that budget is exhausted, subsequent drafts are accepted. A budget-feasibility override forces rejection when accepting a draft would leave too few integration steps to spend the remaining teacher budget. A rejection discards the draft, restores its starting state, and advances one teacher step. Consequently, the actual decisions are not independent Bernoulli draws throughout the trajectory, and p is not a measured acceptance rate. 

LTE policy. LTE uses the original confidence threshold τ = 0 .80 without a fixed per-trajectory teacher budget. Its teacher and student call counts depend on the trajectory. The previously reported 14 accepted and eight rejected events describe a reference execution, not a measured evaluation-set mean. Thus the random control and LTE share the execution framework and nearly match measured latency, but are not established to have identical average teacher usage.

# Appendix K Limitations 

While TamedFlow provides empirical gains across several settings, our findings are subject to several key limitations: 

Theoretical scope of rollout analysis. The discrete Grönwall bound in Proposition 3.1 characterizes error accumulation under sequential Euler drafting to motivate multi-step trajectory supervision. It 23 does not constitute a universal failure law for internal feature caching or forecasting methods, nor does it provide formal convergence or stability guarantees for higher-order ODE solvers such as DPM-Solver++. 

Approximate nature of trajectory routing. The Learnable Trajectory Estimator (LTE) oper-ates as an empirical regression model trained on offline consistency targets rather than an exact, distribution-preserving verification mechanism. Rejection decisions are heuristic; false rejections incur latency penalties through redundant teacher evaluations, whereas false acceptances may allow minor perceptual artifacts to propagate into the generated sample. 

Deployment and infrastructure overhead. TamedFlow relies on a multi-stage offline training curriculum and maintains the teacher, student, VAH, and LTE models co-resident in GPU memory during inference ( ∼ 0.5 GB VRAM overhead in our FP32 profile). As a result, it does not fully eliminate the teacher model from deployment. Furthermore, our reported speedups reflect batch-size-one latent generation on single GPUs; characterization under large-batch throughput, concurrent serving pipelines, and extreme memory-constrained edge environments remains an avenue for future work.
