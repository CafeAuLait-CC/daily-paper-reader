Robot Motion Transition with Human Motion Prior
Taehyun Byun1, Taemoon Jeong1, Yoonbyung Chai1, Woojin Wie2, Insu Park2, Kyungjae Lee3, and Sungjoon Choi1*
Abstract— Humanoid motion-tracking policies can reproduce
diverse behaviors, yet connecting independently acquired mo-
tions into a continuous sequence remains challenging. Interpo-
lation connects boundary poses without considering whether
their relative position, heading, and timing provide suitable
conditions for a transition. A generative motion prior can
produce richer motion, but poorly arranged boundaries may
cause it to generate excessive locomotion, turning, or another
unintended action. We present Spatio-Temporal Alignment
with a Motion Prior (STAMP), a framework for connecting
independently acquired humanoid motions through a Robot-
to-Human-to-Robot process. STAMP uses human motion space
to draw on rich motion priors and reuse transition knowledge
across robot embodiments. Its key component is a learned
alignment model that determines the boundary conditions for
motion inbetweening. Given the two motions retargeted to a
human rig, the alignment model ranks candidate positions,
headings, and transition durations for the following motion. A
human motion prior generates the transition under the selected
alignment. The completed sequence is retargeted to the robot
and used as a reference to train a motion-tracking policy. We
evaluate the tracking success of the generated transitions on
Unitree G1 and ROBOTIS K1. Physical robot demonstrations
further show that STAMP connects independently acquired
motions into long-horizon sequences. Project page: https:
//childtoy.github.io/STAMP-project
I. INTRODUCTION
Recent advances in physics-based motion tracking en-
able humanoid robots to reproduce diverse and dynamic
motions [1]–[3]. However, tracking individual motions and
connecting independently acquired motions into a continuous
sequence are different problems.
Robot motions are commonly generated and validated as
separate clips. When two clips are connected, the position
and heading of the following motion and the time available
for the transition are not given. Two motions can each
be plausible on their own but difficult to connect under
an arbitrary relative placement. These spatial and temporal
choices determine the body movement required between their
boundaries.
A simple approach is to return the robot to a predefined
pose between motions, but this stop-and-restart behavior
interrupts the motion flow. Another approach is to interpolate
directly between the two boundary poses. However, directly

interpolating boundary poses alone does not account for the
incoming and outgoing motion or foot-contact changes. For
a humanoid, a change in the support foot may require a step
or a transfer of body weight that pose interpolation does not
explicitly model.
Recent approaches handle humanoid motion transitions
through graph-based motion switching, motion matching, or
policy blending [4]–[6]. These methods transition at inter-
mediate states selected for compatibility, or blend policies
around the switching point. As a result, the constituent
motions may be truncated or altered, and the content of each
motion is not necessarily preserved. STAMP instead connects
the end of the preceding motion to the start of the following
motion while preserving both input clips.
Motion inbetweening generates missing motion between
given boundary conditions [7]–[9]. Human motion data pro-
vide rich examples of body movements and contact changes
for learning such transitions [10], [11]. MotionBricks further
demonstrates the use of motion inbetweening for real-world
humanoid control [12].
However, inbetweening boundaries extracted from a con-
tinuous motion already have a defined spatial and temporal
relationship. Independently acquired motions do not provide
this relationship. Applying a motion prior to an arbitrary
arrangement may require excessive locomotion, turning, or
another unintended action to connect the motions. Con-
necting such motions therefore requires selecting suitable
boundary conditions before generating the transition.
To address this problem, we propose Spatio-Temporal
Alignment with a Motion Prior (STAMP), a Robot-to-
Human-to-Robot framework that connects robot motions
through a shared human motion space. STAMP learns from
continuous human motion to rank candidate positions, head-
ings, and transition durations, then uses a human motion prior
to generate a transition under the selected conditions. The
completed sequence is retargeted to the target robot and used
to train a robot-specific motion-tracking policy. Experiments
on Unitree G1 and ROBOTIS K1 evaluate the trackability of
the resulting transitions, and physical robot demonstrations
show their use in longer motion compositions.
Our contributions are summarized as follows.
• We introduce STAMP, a Robot-to-Human-to-Robot
framework that uses human motion knowledge to con-
nect independently acquired humanoid motions across
different robot embodiments.
• We learn a spatial and temporal alignment model from
continuous human motions and counterfactual boundary
arrangements. The model ranks candidate positions,

• We evaluate robot tracking success on Unitree G1

II. RELATED WORK
A. Humanoid Motion Tracking and Transition
Physics
based
imitation
learning
enables
humanoid
robots to reproduce complex reference motions. Deep-
Mimic demonstrated the tracking of highly dynamic motion
clips [1]. AMP and PHC extended this capability toward
more diverse and robust humanoid behaviors [2], [13]. Be-
yondMimic further demonstrated reliable motion tracking on
physical humanoid robots [3].
Recent methods also study transitions between robot mo-
tions. Switch connects similar states across motion clips
through a skill graph and learns a policy that moves through
the resulting paths [4]. Perceptive Humanoid Parkour uses
motion matching to select suitable entry states between
locomotion and dynamic motions [5]. RPG blends multi-
ple expert policies to support transitions between fighting
motions [6]. These methods handle transitions through state
selection or policy learning. STAMP selects spatial and
temporal boundary conditions for independently acquired
motions and generates the connecting reference using a
human motion prior.
B. Human Motion Inbetweening
Motion inbetweening generates missing motion from ob-
served boundary contexts. Earlier learning based approaches
use recurrent networks or Transformers to recover the miss-
ing interval [7], [8], [14]. Later methods use phase repre-
sentations or diffusion models to improve motion quality
and control [9], [15]. TEACH and PriorMDM generate
temporally composed human motions from sequences of text
descriptions [16], [17].
Recent motion models support text, keyframe, trajectory,
and online constraints [11], [18]. MotionBricks applies mo-
tion inbetweening to animation and real-world control of
the Unitree G1 [12]. These models generate motion under
specified boundary conditions. When the boundaries are
extracted from a continuous motion, their relative placement
and timing are already defined. STAMP addresses indepen-
dently acquired motions by selecting their relative position,
heading, and transition duration before applying a motion
inbetweening model.
C. Human and Robot Motion Retargeting
Motion retargeting transfers motion between bodies with
different joint structures and proportions. Geometric retar-
geting uses joint correspondences and inverse kinematics to
adapt human motion to a humanoid robot [19]. Learning
based methods instead learn shared or cross embodiment
motion representations [20]–[22]. GMR provides a general
motion retargeting pipeline for constructing robot motion
from human motion data [23]. Other methods use normal-
ized limb directions and target body proportions to transfer
motion across different kinematic structures [24], [25].
STAMP adapts CoRe [25] for bidirectional retargeting,
enabling robot motions to be connected using a human
motion prior and transferred back to the target robot.

Our diffusion model adopts the motion representation and
two-stage Transformer design of Kimodo [11]. It is trained

remain observed, and the intermediate frames are generated.
At each denoising step, the first Transformer predicts
the three-dimensional root position and the sine and cosine
of its heading. These predictions are converted to a local
representation containing heading velocity, planar velocity,
and root height. The second Transformer predicts articulated

boundary contexts. The generated intermediate frames form

Training samples use different durations and are padded
to a common batch length. Padded frames are excluded
from attention and loss computation. We apply reconstruction


losses only to the unobserved transition frames, with channel
weights specified in Sec. V-A.
The models are trained separately, allowing the selected

V. EXPERIMENTS
We compare STAMP with interpolation and inbetweening
baselines on Unitree G1 and ROBOTIS K1 under the same
tracking-policy training protocol. We also evaluate alignment
selection and demonstrate long-horizon motion composition
on the physical robot, ROBOTIS K1.
A. Experimental Setup
a) Motion pairs: Our benchmark contains 18 motion
pairs across three scenarios: eight from boxing, five from
industrial maintenance, and five from security and traffic
control. The pairs include changes in locomotion, heading,
stance height, foot placement, and upper-body movement.
Each constituent motion is independently generated using
Kimodo [11] at 30 fps. Each prompt describes a single
motion without specifying its preceding or following mo-
tion. The generated motions are approximately 6–9 seconds
long. We remove initialization and terminal settling when
necessary. The generated human motions are retargeted to
Humanoids using the human-to-robot retargeting procedure
described in Sec. IV-D. All methods use the same input
motion pairs for each robot.
b) Compared methods: Root/heading interpolation
matches the following motion’s initial root position and
heading to the preceding motion’s final root frame and
connects the boundary states using quintic interpolation.
Kimodo† uses Kimodo’s motion representation and de-
noising architecture [11] and is trained for inbetweening with
boundary motion contexts instead of text. It uses the same
generator and Robot-to-Human-to-Robot pipeline as STAMP,
but retains the original boundary arrangement.
MotionBricks uses its released Unitree G1 model and
duration predictor [12] to generate transitions under the
original boundary arrangement. We evaluate MotionBricks
only on Unitree G1, for which an official pretrained model
is available.
STAMP selects the following motion’s position, heading,
and transition duration before generation with Kimodo†.
Interpolation and Kimodo† use a fixed duration of 1.0 s.
The comparison evaluates spatial alignment and duration
selection together.
c) Alignment
particles:
Relative

{0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0} s. The Cartesian product yields 384 can-

duration gives 388 particles per pair. STAMP selects the
particle with the lowest predicted cost, while the ablation
uses the rank-100 particle from the same set.
d) Model training:
Both the alignment model and
Kimodo† are trained on BONES-SEED [10] using the
SOMA representation at 30 fps with K = 10 boundary
frames. Each alignment sample contains 16 candidates span-
ning observed and identity placements, alternative durations,
and local and global spatial perturbations. The alignment
model uses two three-layer Transformer encoders of width
160 with five attention heads. It is trained for 25,000 steps
using AdamW with learning rate 2×10−4 and per-GPU batch
size 32. We select the checkpoint with the lowest validation
loss.
Kimodo† uses Transformer width 512 with eight attention
heads, 1,000 diffusion timesteps, and 50 denoising steps
at inference. Smooth L1 loss weights for root position,
heading, joint position, rotation, velocity, and contact are
(10, 2, 10, 10, 3, 4), respectively. Training uses AdamAtan2
with peak learning rate 2×10−5, 2,000 warm-up steps, cosine
decay, and global batch size 2,048.
e) Motion tracking: Each reference is tracked by an
independently trained BeyondMimic policy [3]. Pairwise
references contain the transition and K context frames on
each side, resampled from 30 to 50 Hz. Training starts from
randomly sampled reference phases.
Each policy is trained for 5,000 iterations with 2,048 par-
allel environments: 1,500 iterations without external pushes,
2,000 with moderate perturbations and domain randomiza-
tion, and 1,500 with full deployment randomization. Archi-
tecture, observations, rewards, termination conditions, and
randomization schedules are identical across methods for


each robot. We use one training seed per reference and
evaluate the final checkpoint.
B. Evaluation Metrics
Each policy is evaluated over 1,024 rollouts using 256
parallel environments. We discard the initial rollout of each
environment and collect subsequent rollouts after regular
resets. Each evaluation rollout starts at the first reference
frame, with randomized perturbations to the corresponding
root pose, root velocity, and joint configuration. The refer-
ence phase is not randomized.
Body velocity error and joint acceleration are averaged
over valid timesteps up to completion or early termination,
including both successful and failed episodes. We then
average equally over 1,024 episodes per motion pair and over
the 18 pairs, totaling 18,432 evaluation rollouts per method.
a) Success rate: A rollout is successful if it reaches the
final reference frame without triggering a tracking termina-
tion and maintains root-position, root-orientation, and end-
effector-position errors below 0.20 m, 0.40 rad, and 0.20 m,
respectively, throughout execution. The end-effector condi-
tion applies to every tracked end effector. Success rate is the
percentage of successful rollouts.
b) Body velocity error: Mean body velocity error (Evel)
is the mean Euclidean difference between simulated and
reference world-frame linear velocities over all 30 tracked
body points and evaluated timesteps. The root body is
included, and root velocity is not subtracted. We report Evel
in m/s.
c) Joint acceleration: We report the mean absolute
acceleration of the executed actuated joints:

D. Alignment Ranking Ablation
We compare candidates ranked 1 and 100 in ascending
predicted transition cost. Both conditions use the same
candidate set, motion generator, retargeting procedure, and
tracking-policy training and evaluation protocol. Only the
selected position, heading, and transition duration differ.
As shown in Table IV, rank 1 achieves higher success rates
than rank 100 by 15.62 percentage points on ROBOTIS K1
and 9.36 percentage points on Unitree G1. It produces lower
body velocity error and executed joint acceleration on both
robots. These results support the usefulness of the learned
transition cost for selecting trackable boundary arrangements
in this comparison.

F. Limitations
The learned cost provides a kinematic guide for alignment
selection, while downstream trackability also depends on
retargeting quality and robot dynamics.
We currently train a tracking policy for each composed
sequence. Future work could integrate alignment and inbe-
tweening as motion priors within a shared reinforcement
learning framework, allowing new compositions to be ex-
ecuted with less sequence-specific training.
VI. CONCLUSION
We propose STAMP, a Robot-to-Human-to-Robot frame-
work for connecting independently acquired humanoid mo-
tions. A learned alignment model selects the relative position,
heading, and transition duration before a human motion
prior generates the transition. Both models operate in human
motion space and are reused across robot embodiments. The
completed sequence is retargeted and tracked by a robot-
specific policy.
Experiments on Unitree G1 and ROBOTIS K1 show
higher tracking success and lower body velocity errors than
the evaluated baselines. The ablation supports learned align-
ment selection, and physical demonstrations show connecting
steps and longer motion compositions. A traffic-control case
study also shows lower reported motor-current RMS than
interpolation in the latter part of the transition.
