Abstract
Text-to-motion models generate for the skeletons they were
trained on, while a new character arrives as a skeleton file and
nothing else. We study text-to-motion for skeletons that no
training clip has used, with no clips of the character, no tem-
plate, no retargeting and no fine-tuning. We present TopX,
a 73M-parameter flow-matching transformer that generates
joint rotations and a root trajectory for any kinematic tree
from its tree, rest pose and joint names. Motion is normalised
by the rest pose and the size of the skeleton, and the rest pose
enters as a world-frame descriptor that does not depend on
how the rig’s author oriented each joint, with rest conventions
randomised in training. Attention is factorised over the joints
and the frames of a motion and carries a spatial–temporal en-
coding designed for skeleton graphs of any topology: queries
and keys are rotated across joints by a heat-kernel coordinate
of the skeleton graph, which is defined for every tree and does
not change with the order of its joints or the eigenbasis of its
Laplacian, and across frames by a rotary encoding of frame
order. To measure what this buys, we build SkelShift: four
motion corpora under one codec, 143,334 captioned clips on
skeletons of 4 to 142 joints, from which 4,232 clips on 2,043
skeletons are held out by clip, rig, tree class or species and
organised along topology, joint count, body plan, species and
rig source, and scored by motion quality: Fréchet distances
on public kinetic features and on kinematic descriptors, foot
skating and ground contact. Against UniMate trained on the
same clips, on test rigs without a training clip, TopX lowers
the descriptor FID from 5.73 to 4.58 when UniMate’s guid-
ance is tuned for the kinetic FID, and the kinetic FID from
1.48 to 0.55 when it is tuned for the descriptor FID, also on
motions no training skeleton performs; fewer of its frames
sink below the ground.

Introduction
Text-to-motion generation has become fluent on the human
body [11, 14, 15, 42], and nearly all of it assumes one skeleton:
the joint count, joint order and rest pose of the training body
are built into the model. Games, film and virtual production
animate casts of characters that are not human, each rigged
with its own tree, joint names, joint order and rest pose. When
such a character is created, its skeleton file is all there is—no
clips of it to normalise with, adapt to or retarget from. A text-
to-motion model for these pipelines has to animate a skeleton
from that file.
Three properties of a skeleton file make this hard. Scale:
without clips, the size of a character’s motion and the spread
of each joint’s channels are unknown, so a normalisation by
per-rig motion statistics cannot be applied. Conventions: the
order in which a file lists its joints, the names it gives them
and the orientation of each joint’s rest frame are choices of
the rig’s author, and the motion should not depend on them.
Shape: the tree itself may lie outside the training data, with a
new topology, more joints or an unfamiliar body plan. Retar-
geting [1, 44] and template bodies [45–47] exclude whatever
the template cannot express. AnyTop [12] generates on ar-
bitrary trees without captions and normalises each skeleton
with statistics of that skeleton’s motions. UniMate [31], the
closest system to ours, is text-conditioned and serves a new
skeleton from its rest pose, topology and joint names; its
released model serves up to 71 joints and 60 frames.
How far such models generalise is hard to tell, because
unseen skeletons have been evaluated on a handful of held-
out skeleton types [31], on held-out species of one shared
topology [46], or without text [33]. Measuring generalisation
needs held-out skeletons whose distance from the training
data is controlled and stated, and metrics that stay valid on
them.
A learned text–motion evaluator can lose much of
its ceiling off its training distribution—on held-out species
the real clips’ R@1 drops from 0.899 to 0.594 in [46]—so we
score motion quality with evaluator-free statistics, as UniMate
does.
We present TopX (Fig. 1), a text-conditioned flow-
matching transformer that reads a rig’s tree, rest pose and
joint names. It normalises motion by the rest pose and the
skeleton’s size, and reads the rest pose in the world frame
while being trained under randomised rest conventions. Its
attention is factorised over the joints and the frames of a mo-
tion, and the two axes carry one spatial–temporal encoding
designed for skeleton graphs: a heat-kernel coordinate of the
graph, with a tree-hop bias, on the joint axis and the frame
order on the time axis, both applied as rotations of queries
and keys.
No learned table is indexed by joint slot or by
frame, so one set of weights reads any kinematic tree. The

Related Work
Text-to-motion.
Human text-to-motion moved from varia-
tional models [14] to diffusion in pose space [42, 51] or in
a latent [7], to token models over quantised motion [15, 49],
scaled by larger corpora [11, 26] and skeleton-aware la-
tents [17]. Animal text-to-motion fixes a template: the SMAL
body [47], a topology shared by humans and animals [46],
a unified 30-joint skeleton [45]; OmniSkel adapts a human
model to new bone lengths [28]. Each fixes the kinematic tree,
or its topology, when it is trained. TopX fixes neither: one
set of weights reads any tree, so a new character is animated
from its skeleton file.
Motion on arbitrary skeletons.
Retargeting maps mo-
tion between structurally compatible skeletons [1, 44] or
through skeleton-agnostic embeddings [22, 52], and Mo-
tion2Motion [6] transfers across topologies from a few ex-
amples of the target.
AnyTop [12] generates on arbitrary
trees without captions, places the rest pose as the first frame
and biases attention by distances on the tree, and normalises
each skeleton by statistics of that skeleton’s motions; UniMo-
Gen [21] adds style and trajectory control on human skeletons.
Text-conditioned generation across topologies was shown by
OmniZoo [5], by G-MDM [23], which predicts joint rotations
without root translation, by ACT [30], which rigs a mesh and
animates it from text, and, concurrently with this work, by
UniMate [31], which serves a new skeleton zero-shot with a
spectral rotary encoding of Laplacian eigenvectors, a graph
attention bias, rest-pose rebasing and skeleton augmentation.
TopX addresses the same setting and differs in four choices
for an unseen skeleton: motion is normalised by the rest pose
and the skeleton’s size, not by statistics of a rig or a corpus, so
a new rig needs no statistics; the world-frame rest descriptor
does not depend on how each joint was oriented, and rest
conventions are randomised in training; the joint coordinate
is free of the eigenbasis, so the output does not change with
joint order even under repeated eigenvalues (63% of our train-
ing skeletons); and it generates up to 240 frames for any joint
count, where UniMate’s released model generates 60 frames
for up to 71 joints.
Evaluating unseen skeletons.
UniMate holds out seven
Truebones skeleton types (58 sequences) and reports a
kinematic-feature FID and diversity, and foot sliding on 20
legged skeletons; X-MoGen [46] holds out species on its
shared topology. For video-driven animation, TopoCap [33]
separates seen and unseen topologies and MoCapAny-
thing [13] stratifies seen, rare and unseen species; in retarget-
ing, [18] reports proportion changes and arbitrary structures
separately.
SkelShift brings such controlled axes to text-
conditioned generation: held-out units are cut from training,
the distance of every test tree to the training trees is stated,
and results are reported per axis.
Positional encodings on graphs.
Laplacian eigenvectors
are ambiguous in sign and, inside repeated eigenvalues, in ba-
sis [19, 25]. Diffusion-based coordinates avoid both: random-
walk [10] and heat-kernel [41] signatures are established node
encodings [4, 34], and WIRE [35] uses spectral and random-
walk coordinates as rotary angles [40]. We use the heat-kernel
signature as the rotary coordinate of a skeleton’s joints, paired
with a rotary encoding of frame order.

the rig’s author oriented each joint’s rest frame; the world-
frame descriptor does not, and it is the feature that tells the
network what the rest pose is.
3.2
A spatial–temporal encoding for skeleton
graphs
A motion is a grid of tokens over two axes of different kinds:
the joints of a frame form a tree that changes from rig to
rig, the frames of a joint form a sequence. TopX factorises
attention along the two axes: each of its 𝑁𝑏blocks attends
over time (one joint across its frames), then over joints (one
frame across its joints), then applies an MLP. Position is
encoded on both axes in the same way: queries and keys
are RMS-normalised per head [8] and rotated by angles of
their token’s position [40], so that position enters a logit only
through the difference of two positions’ angles. The angles
suit each axis: on the joint axis they are computed from the
skeleton graph through its heat kernel, next to a bias on hop
counts in the tree; on the frame axis they follow the frame
index. The two encodings are designed as a pair for skeleton
graphs of any topology: neither axis has a learned table of
absolute positions, so no parameter depends on the number
or the order of a rig’s joints, and the same weights read any
kinematic tree.

Frame axis.
The temporal attention rotates queries and keys
by angles of the frame index [40], with the rest frame at index
0 and base 700, the rule of [31] for a 241-frame window. It
replaces a learned table of frame positions, so position enters
the temporal logits only through frame offsets.

Skeleton and convention augmentation.
With probability
0.8 a training sample is changed by one kinematics-preserving
operation, following UniMate’s rule [31]: a joint is inserted on
a bone, a few leaves are removed, single-child joints of a chain
are merged, or every bone is rescaled by up to ±10%; positions
and velocities are re-encoded by FK, so the sample stays self-
consistent. Independently, with probability 0.5, every joint’s
rest orientation is turned by a random rotation of up to 30◦, as
if the rig had been authored with another rest pose: the world
motion is unchanged, and only the rest-relative rotations the
network has to produce change with it. The world-frame rest
descriptor is what allows the network to tell such rigs apart.
Rigs are drawn with probability proportional to the square
root of their clip count, as in [31], so that corpora of single-
clip rigs and of rigs with hundreds of clips both contribute.

The SkelShift corpus and bench-
mark
4.1
Four corpora under one codec
SkelShift brings four corpora under one codec (counts are
training clips and rigs after the benchmark is cut out): the
Objaverse-XL [9] subset of UniML3D [31] at a fixed com-
mit (5,023 clips, 4,277 rigs, 6–60 joints), whose captions are
one sentence per clip written by a vision–language model; the
AniMo4D animal library [45] (76,882 clips, 307 rigs) with its
captions, together with HumanML3D [14] (26,844 clips on
one 22-joint skeleton); further animations and species from
the same game library as AniMo4D (29,645 clips, 485 rigs),
captioned by rule from the animation names; and the True-
bones zoo [43] (708 clips, 57 rigs, up to 142 joints). Training
thus covers 139,102 clips (144 hours at 30 fps) on 5,127 rigs
and 1,364 distinct trees, with 6 to 142 joints. Every clip is
encoded by one codec, at 30 fps, grounded at its lowest point,
with one contact rule, and checked by a data gate (appendix),
so a statistic means the same thing on every corpus. Every
joint has a text description, from its source name or, when the
name conveys no anatomy, from the rest pose and the tree (ap-
pendix). As the source assets keep their licences, the corpus
is released as a per-clip list of source identifiers, splits and
annotations with the scripts that rebuild it from the originals,
and the metric code.
4.2
Held-out buckets
SkelShift holds out units rather than clips (Tab. 1). Two in-
distribution buckets anchor the scale: ID-clip holds out clips
of rigs that are trained on, and ID-tree holds out whole rigs
whose tree is isomorphic to a training rig’s, so that only pro-
portions, rest pose and names change. The out-of-distribution
buckets each move one axis further. OOD-tree holds out a
quarter of the rarely populated tree classes of bipeds and
quadrupeds, and OOD-plan 40% of the tree classes of rare
body plans (insects, birds, sea animals, snakes); every rig of
a held-out class leaves training. The OOD-J buckets hold the
UniML3D rigs whose joint count lies outside the range trained
on in that corpus (61–117, of which 250 rigs share Mixamo’s
65-joint humanoid tree, and 4). OOD-species holds out four
species of the game library—markhor and greater rhea (dev),
capybara and emu (test)—with their male, female and juve-
nile rigs. OOD-new-species holds ten rigs of species never
trained (frogs, a newt, an axolotl, beetles, a cockroach, a leaf
insect, a fruit bat, a terrapin), kept after a motion-quality gate.
OOD-rig holds eight Truebones quadrupeds (two bears, cat,
goat, hound, lynx, stegosaurus, turtle) with unseen trees.
Outside OOD-species, held-out units are hashed one third
to dev and two thirds to test; TopX’s model selection and
hyper-parameter choices use dev only. In the comparison with
UniMate, guidanceisswept ondev, andUniMateisreportedat
its best by each Fréchet distance (Sec. 5.1). Every benchmark
clip carries the distance of its tree to the nearest training tree,
the joint count and body plan, and whether its source motion
was reused on a training skeleton, so results can be stratified
by each annotation. SkelShift measures generalisation over
skeletons.
4.3
Protocol and metrics
Generation.
TopX serves every benchmark rig, including
those of ID-clip, from its skeleton file: the tree, the rest pose,
the joint names and the up and forward axes. Each clip is
generated with five sampling seeds at the length of the real
clip, capped at 240 frames, and scored as generated, at the
guidance with the lowest KIDdesc on dev (appendix).
Motion quality.
We follow UniMate [31] in scoring motion
quality by a Fréchet distance on kinematic features and by
foot sliding, computed on the played animation, the FK of the
generated rotations plus the root trajectory:
• FIDkin: Fréchet distance between the generated and the
real clips of a group on the public kinetic features of [6,
39], on which UniMate’s FID is computed: per joint,
the mean horizontal and vertical kinetic energy and the
mean acceleration over the clip, with positions in units
of the rig scale. Pooled over joints by their mean and

weighted equally except in KIDdesc, which compares the clip
distributions.
Text alignment.
A skeleton-agnostic evaluator trained on
our training split (appendix) scores caption retrieval within
rigs of the held-out game species (439 test clips with cu-
rated captions, pools of 8, chance 0.125). On the first 60
frames, UniMate’s output length, a caption retrieves the mo-
tion TopX generated for it at R@1 0.915, against 0.896 for
the real motions, 0.122 with the captions shuffled and 0.279
for UniMate’s released model; trained on the same clips, the
pair of Tab. 2 (UniMate tuned on FIDkin) reaches 0.317 and
0.192 on the emu rigs that both serve (appendix). Figure 2
shows text alignment qualitatively.
5
Experiments
5.1
Setup
Model.
TopX has 10 blocks of width 512 with 8 heads
(73.3M parameters) and samples at the guidance with the
lowest KIDdesc on dev (appendix). Each ablation trains for
half the steps of the main model, next to a full-recipe control
of the same length, and all arms share one sampler setting.
Training settings are in the appendix.
Baseline.
UniMate [31], the closest system, serves a skele-
ton zero-shot from its rest pose, topology and joint names
and generates 60 frames. We train both methods on the same
5,023 clips of the UniML3D training split, UniMate with its
official code and configuration and TopX with the recipe of
Sec. 3. Each keeps its own sampler, TopX generating at the
clip’s length; on dev, TopX takes the guidance with the low-
est FIDkin, and UniMate is reported at its lowest FIDkin and
its lowest FIDdesc (appendix). The comparison with released
weights, including AnyTop [12], is in the appendix.

5.3
Ablations
The representation of Sec. 3.1 is what serving a rig from its
file requires, and every arm shares it. Table 4 changes one
part of the model at a time: the frame or the joint rotary
encoding becomes a learned table, the heat-kernel coordi-
nate becomes UniMate’s eigenvectors with a SignNet [31],
or the augmentation (of skeletons and of rest conventions)
is removed. These parts are added for skeletons unlike the
training ones, so the table reads them where the benchmark
tests that: unseen topologies, rare body plans, new species
and Truebones rigs. On each of these the full recipe has the
lowest KIDdesc of all arms (unseen topologies 1.78, against
1.88 with the frame table, 1.87 with the joint table, 1.83 with
eigenvectors and 1.95 without augmentation), and changing
a test rig’s rest convention moves its output half as much as
without augmentation (0.44 against 0.89 bone lengths).
6
Conclusion
A text-to-motion model can serve a skeleton it has never
seen, with no clip of it, when its motion is normalised by the
rest pose and the skeleton’s size, the rest pose is read in the
world frame under randomised conventions, and its attention
is encoded for skeleton graphs of any topology. SkelShift
measures this along five shift axes. Against UniMate trained
on the same clips, TopX lowers each Fréchet distance when
UniMate is tuned for the other, on rigs with no training clip
and on novel motions. Captions are not held out, so SkelShift
measures generalisation over skeletons, and text alignment is
evaluated on the held-out game species.

Codec.
A clip enters as a tree, a rest pose and per-frame
local rotations and root translations. It is resampled to 30 fps
(spherical interpolation of the local rotations, linear interpo-
lation of the root), its global rotations and positions follow
by FK, and it is shifted vertically so that its lowest joint over
the whole clip touches 𝑦=0. The root’s horizontal track is
smoothed by a fourth-order zero-phase Butterworth filter at
1 Hz (a least-squares line for clips shorter than 90 frames) and
stored from the clip’s first frame; positions store XZ relative
to it and absolute height. Rotations are the global rotation
composed with the inverse rest orientation, written as the first
two matrix columns. The heading is the ground-plane direc-
tion of a declared carrier joint’s forward axis and is marked
invalid where its horizontal norm vanishes.
The frame is
right-handed with +𝑌up and the rest pose facing +𝑍. A data
gate checks grounding, contact, root track, heading and the
agreement of the positions with the FK of the rotations on
every clip.
Rest normalisation and validity.
The rest frame xrest of
Eq. (1) holds the rest positions relative to the root’s ground
point, the identity rotation [1, 0, 0, 0, 1, 0], zero velocity,
contact and track, and the heading (1, 0); the gains are
𝑔= (3.868, 2.944, 3.321) for position, velocity and ground
track, three constants fixed once on a calibration set of 148
clips (118 Truebones, 30 HumanML3D) before SkelShift was
drawn and shared by every rig. During training, a cell that is
exactly constant for a rig (the contact flag of a joint that never
touches the ground) is excluded from the loss and the input.
Generation uses no per-rig channel-constancy statistics: ev-
ery rig, seen or not, is served from its skeleton file with a
structural mask.

Metric definitions
All statistics are computed on the played animation at 30 fps
with +𝑌up and the ground at 𝑦=0; 𝑠is the rest diagonal of
the rig.
Kinetic features and FIDkin.
UniMate computes its FID
on kinematic features after [6, 39].
Both release an
extract_kinetic_features that returns three numbers
per joint: the average horizontal and vertical kinetic energy
and an average acceleration term, which in Motion2Motion’s
release is the mean magnitude of a window-averaged second
difference of the positions and in Bailando’s also follows the
joint’s position.
We run each release unchanged, with its
default arguments, on world joint positions divided by 𝑠and
taken relative to the first-frame root position. A vector of
3𝐽numbers cannot be compared across skeletons, so each
of the three numbers is pooled over the joints by its mean
and population standard deviation: six numbers per sequence
on every skeleton. Both sets are standardised by the mean
and standard deviation of the real set and compared by the
Fréchet distance. Motion2Motion’s release gives the FIDkin
of Tab. 2, Bailando’s is reported next to it (Tab. 10). The dis-
tance is computed per seed and averaged over the five seeds,
for groups of at least 50 real clips.
Kinematic descriptor, FIDdesc and KIDdesc.
Per clip: root
speed (mean, standard deviation, 90th percentile), root height
range, absolute yaw rate (mean) and its standard deviation,
root-relative joint speed (10th, 50th, 90th percentiles), ac-
celeration (50th, 90th), jerk (50th), mean activity, contact
fraction (codec rule), airborne fraction, dominant frequency
of the activity signal, pose spread along three axes relative to
the rest pose, net displacement, path length, mean root height,
median lowest-joint height and mean vertical root speed: 24
numbers. Scale-like features enter as log(𝑥+ 10−3); all are
standardised by the training clips’ statistics and clipped at ±5.
FIDdesc is the Fréchet distance between Gaussians fitted to the
descriptors of the generated samples of a group and of its real
clips, computed when the group has at least 50 real clips: per
seed and averaged in the same-data comparison, over all seeds
together in the comparison with released weights (Tab. 13)
and on dev (Tab. 8). KIDdesc compares the clip distributions
of a group per seed, with the kernel 𝑘(𝑥, 𝑦) = (𝑥⊤𝑦/24 + 1)3
and the unbiased estimator averaged over 100 random subsets
of min(50, ⌊𝑁/2⌋) clips per side, 𝑁the clips of the group; its
real–real value is the KIDdesc between two halves of the real
clips split by rig, averaged over ten splits.
Skate, Slide and Cov.
We follow UniMate’s evaluation of
foot sliding [31]. A rig’s contact joints are the joints whose
canonical name contains foot, toe, ball, paw, hoof or pastern
and none of whose descendants does, i.e. the distal-most such
joint of each limb chain. Canonical names come from Uni-
Mate’s rule-based name cleaner, the one it applies to new as-
sets; in the comparison with released weights, from its joint
annotation where one exists for the rig. ℓis the height of the
root above the plane through the lowest rest-pose joint. For a
contact joint at frame 𝑡, ℎis its height and 𝑑its horizontal dis-
placement since frame 𝑡−1, both in units of ℓ. A frame is near
the ground when ℎ< 0.05 and skates when also 𝑑> 0.025.
Per sequence, Skate is the share of near-ground frames that
skate and Slide the mean of 100 𝑑(2 −2ℎ/0.05) over near-
ground frames; both are averaged over the sequences, (clip,
seed) pairs, in which a contact joint comes near the ground.
Cov. is the share of contact-joint frames near the ground,
pooled over the group. Rigs without a named contact joint,
such as snakes, fish and most insects, and rigs with ℓ= 0 are
left out of the three. UniMate measures Skate and Slide on
20 biped and quadruped rigs; here every rig with a named
contact joint enters.
Ground contact and activity.
Slip is the share of grounded
joint-frames, over all joints, whose horizontal speed exceeds
0.25 𝑠per second; a joint is grounded at frame 𝑡when its
height is at most 0.05 𝑠at frames 𝑡and 𝑡+ 1, the codec’s
contact thresholds. Pen. is the share of a clip’s frames whose
lowest joint lies more than 0.005 𝑠below the ground. Float
compares the median over frames of the lowest-joint height,
in units of 𝑠, with the 95th percentile of the same quantity
over a calibration sample of training clips of the clip’s body-
plan group (biped, quadruped, other); clips whose caption
contains one of the words fly, flies, flying, jump, jumps,
13


jumping, leap, leaps, hop, hops, swim, swims, swimming,
glide, glides, soar, soars, hover or hovers are left out, both
here and in that sample. Speed is the root-relative joint speed
∥Δp∥fps/𝑠, averaged over frames and joints, summed over
the clips of a group with rig-equal weights and divided by
the same sum on the real clips. Pen. and Float are averaged
within each rig and then over rigs, as is Slip; these statistics
and KIDdesc are computed per seed and averaged over the five
seeds.
Self-tests.
Before use, the statistics of our scorer passed
a planted-defect test on the real clips: sinking a clip raises
Pen. with the depth, lifting it raises the lowest-joint height
by exactly the lift, time warps by ×0.5 and ×2 scale Speed
accordingly, and white noise raises KIDdesc monotonically
with its amplitude.
Reproducibility.
The five seeds are 7, 13, 23, 31 and 42.
Generation is deterministic per (checkpoint, clip, seed, guid-
ance scale): TopX draws every sample at batch 1 from noise
seeded by a hash of the seed and the clip identifier, so the result
does not depend on how the clips are split across GPUs or on
the order in which they are processed. UniMate’s same-data
test samples are solved in fixed groups of 64 cases, formed in
clip order before the cases are split across GPUs (Sec. H).
G
Guidance scale
For TopX’s own evaluation (Tabs. 3 and 4) and the comparison
with released weights, the protocol fixes the guidance scale of
each method on dev: the scale with the lowest mean KIDdesc
among 1.5, 2 and 3 is taken, and 2 is preferred among the
scales whose gap to it is within the larger of their 95% seed
half-widths. For TopX at 50 steps (Tab. 8) this is 2 (KIDdesc
0.142, against 0.149 at 1.5 and 0.176 at 3); the ablations, at
20 steps, keep the choice made at that step count, also 2. For
UniMate’s released weights (Sec. H.1), on the 528 dev clips of
the game-library rigs at 60 frames, KIDdesc is 0.253, 0.302 and
0.537 at 1.5, 2 and 3, so it samples at 1.5; its released default,
3.0, is the worst of the three. AnyTop is unconditional and
has no guidance. In the same-data comparison each method’s
setting is chosen on dev as well (Sec. H).

Scoring.
Every sequence is scored on its first min(60,𝑇)

methods is read from a bootstrap over rigs (2,000 resamples,

and called resolved when its 95% interval excludes zero; a
group in which not every resample defines the difference is
not read.

Table 14 gives the seed half-widths of Tab. 4. Skeleton aug-
mentation is meant for unseen topologies and body plans,
which these buckets test. We test rest-convention augmenta-
tion directly on 398 OOD test clips on 196 rigs: each joint’s
rest orientation of the test skeleton is turned by its own ran-
dom rotation of at most 30◦(the training range), the world
motion stays the same, and the model samples again with the
same seed and caption. The median change of the generated
joint positions is 0.44 mean bone lengths with the full recipe
and 0.89 without augmentation, lower by 0.45 (95% bootstrap
interval 0.41–0.50 over clips, 0.39–0.51 over rigs). For scale,
sampling the same clip with another seed moves the output
by 1.49 (1.36 without augmentation). An output that ignored
the change would move 0.80 and 0.79 respectively. The full
recipe moves less in each of the 7 OOD buckets with probe
clips.

Text–motion alignment
Evaluator.
Two towers map a caption and a motion to a
shared 512-d space: a frozen DistilBERT [38] with a train-
able head, and a graph–temporal motion encoder over world
joint positions (height kept, horizontal positions relative to
the smoothed root), velocities and the smoothed root’s dis-
placement, with per-joint rest geometry and the tree as static
input and no joint names; joint order is canonicalised, so a re-
labelling leaves the embedding bit-identical. It is trained with
a symmetric InfoNCE loss on 132,368 clips of our training
split (6,949 held for selection), with FK-exact skeleton aug-
mentation and species words masked in half of the captions.
Validity on the training distribution.
With captions de-
duplicated within pools of 32 (chance 0.031) and species
words masked, captions retrieve their real held-out clips with
R@1 0.92 on AniMo4D animals and 0.93 on the game-
library additions; shuffling frames removes 79% of the margin

Held-out species.
We measure text-to-motion R@1 within
the rigs of the held-out game species, in pools of 8 captions
of one rig (chance 0.125), over the 439 test clips on 6 rigs
that carry a curated caption and 30–240 frames, at full length.
A caption retrieves the motion that TopX generated for it at
R@1 0.953 (mean of five seeds), against 0.929 for the real
motions; with the captions shuffled among the clips the score
falls to 0.120.
