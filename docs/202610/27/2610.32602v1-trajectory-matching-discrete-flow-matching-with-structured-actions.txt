ABSTRACT 

The natural units of structured discrete generation are often not independent single-component edits but richer actions: attaching a fragment to a molecule, inserting a node together with an incident edge, or growing a hierarchy by adding a child. Yet most existing discrete flow matching methods (DFM) are restricted to independent component-wise edits over fixed-size states. This restriction stems from endpoint conditioning: endpoints fix the source and target during training but not the action sequence between them, making tractable conditional rates hard to define for complex actions that cannot be factorized component-wise. We propose trajectory matching, which conditions the flow on a full sequence of actions connecting the endpoints. This yields principled, simulation-free training targets for user-defined action spaces, including actions that change state size, modify multiple components, or depend on earlier actions. We prove that the resulting objective recovers the marginal flow and that trajectory matching strictly subsumes factorized DFM, while additionally capturing actions with prerequisite structure such as hierarchy growth or fragment-based molecule generation. Empirically, on tree and molecular graph generation, trajectory matching with domain-aligned actions matches or surpasses factorized DFM baselines (lifting MOSES validity from 92.8% to 99.2%) and transfers without retraining to substructure extension and linking. Trajectory matching enlarges the design space of discrete flow matching by turning the action vocabulary into a modeling choice. An interactive explanation of trajectory matching is available at https://niklaskemper.com/blog/ trajectory-matching/ .

1 INTRODUCTION 

Many structured generation problems are most naturally expressed in terms of actions that reflect the structure of the domain rather than the structure of the representation. In molecular graph generation, for example, the natural actions are not single-component edits but operations like attaching a fragment to a partial structure or inserting an atom together with a bond. Such actions can change the size of the state, modify several components at once, and depend on one another—an atom can only be attached to atoms that have already been inserted. The value of such structured actions is well established in autoregressive models: fragment-based molecule generation (Jin et al., 2019), grammar-based code generation (Yin & Neubig, 2017), and insertion-based graph models (You et al., 2018) all exploit domain-aligned actions to achieve strong empirical performance. However, existing discrete flow matching (DFM) (Campbell et al., 2024; Gat et al., 2024) and related non-autoregressive methods such as discrete diffusion (Austin et al., 2021) cannot easily express such actions. The reason is structural: DFM prescribes a conditional flow between sampled endpoints, and the corresponding rate must be tractable for training. To achieve this over combinatorially large state spaces, existing methods factorize the rate across components. This commits the model to fixed-size states, single-component edits, and independence between actions. Recent extensions (Havasi et al., 2025; Deutschmann et al., 2026; Lüdke et al., 2026) lift the fixed-size constraint by supporting insertions and deletions in specific domains, but they do so by mapping these operations into augmented state spaces where they again reduce to factorized single-component edits. Actions that modify multiple components or depend on one another therefore remain out of reach. 1arXiv:2610.32602v1 [cs.LG] 23 Oct 2026 Preprint We address this gap with trajectory matching: rather than conditioning on endpoints alone, we condition on a full trajectory of actions connecting them. A trajectory specifies which actions are applied and in what order, leaving the conditional flow to determine only when each action is taken. This makes the conditional rate straightforward to define for general action spaces, including actions that change state size, modify multiple components, or depend on one another. The resulting training objective is still simulation-free and provably recovers the marginal data-generating flow. To make training efficient, we require an efficiently sampleable distribution over trajectories connecting the endpoints. We give explicit constructions for several settings, including actions with hierarchical dependencies. Our framework also recovers standard factorized DFM as the special case of inde-pendent component-wise actions with uniformly sampled orderings, placing factorized updates and actions with prerequisites within a common framework. Empirically, we show that domain-aligned action spaces translate into concrete gains on tree and molecular graph generation. On tree generation, child-attachment actions yield 100% validity by construction across sampling budgets, while a factorized baseline collapses below 10% at 10 Euler steps. On molecular graphs, we demonstrate the flexibility of trajectory matching across four action spaces, ranging from factorized bond edits, which match existing DFM baselines, to fragment-based insertions, which lift MOSES validity from 92.8% to 99.2%. Additionally, a single variable-size insertion model transfers zero-shot to substructure extension and linking, substantially outperforming a factorized baseline that requires task-specific adaptation. Our core contributions are: • Trajectory matching. A discrete flow matching framework over user-defined action spaces, including size-changing, multi-component, and dependent actions, obtained by conditioning training on full action trajectories rather than endpoints. The objective recovers the marginal flow (Thm. 3.2); a Rao-Blackwellized variant reduces gradient variance (Prop. B.3). • A unifying framework. Trajectory matching subsumes factorized DFM (Thm. 4.4) while extending to partial-order action systems, capturing hierarchy growth and fragment-based molecule construction. • Empirical gains on structured generation. On tree and molecular graph generation, trajectory matching with domain-aligned action spaces yields concrete gains over factorized DFM baselines: 100% validity by construction on trees, MOSES validity from 92.8% to 99.2% on molecules, and zero-shot transfer to substructure extension and linking. 

2 ACTION -B ASED DISCRETE FLOW MATCHING 

States and Actions We model generation as a sequence of actions on discrete states s ∈ S rather than component-wise updates. This enlarges the design space beyond standard factorized DFM. Let A be the global action vocabulary and A(s) ⊆ A the set of actions available at state s; each a

produces a new state a(s)̸ = s. The choice of A is itself a modeling decision: in molecules, for instance, one might edit bonds on a fixed atom set, attach atoms one at a time, or insert whole ring fragments. Actions may modify multiple components, depend on one another, or change the size of the state. We say actions a1, a 2 are independent if the order of application does not matter, i.e., for every s with a1, a 2 ∈ A(s): a1 ∈ A(a2(s)) , a2 ∈ A(a1(s)) , and a1(a2(s)) = a2(a1(s)) . For example, unmasking two tokens in text is independent, while insertion actions generally are not.

Factorized Flows Specifying the conditional rate ut(s, a | sI , s F ) is the central design choice in discrete flow matching: it must be efficiently computable and generate an efficiently sampleable path connecting sI and sF . In general action spaces, however, many different action sequences — potentially with dependencies between actions — may connect sI to sF , making it difficult to specify such rates from the endpoints alone. Existing discrete flow matching methods therefore make strong assumptions on the action space: 1) initial, final, and all intermediate states decompose into components s = ( s1, . . . , s d) with the same fixed size d, 2) each action modifies exactly one component from its initial value siI to its final value siF , and 3) conditioned on the endpoints, components evolve independently. Under these assumptions, the conditional rate factorizes across components: for an action a acting on component i, it suffices to specify a rate ut(s, a | sI , s F ) that depends only on si, siI , and siF . Each component then evolves independently between its initial and final values. A common choice is the linear schedule 

ut(s, a | sI , s F ) = 1[ai(si) = siF ] · 1/(1 − t), (2) which generates the factorized path

3 TRAJECTORY MATCHING 

Factorized flows are simple to define and have shown strong empirical performance on a variety of tasks. However, their underlying assumptions rule out many natural modeling choices in structured domains: actions cannot change the size of the state (e.g., adding an atom to a molecule), cannot modify multiple components at once (e.g., inserting a full fragment into a molecular graph), and cannot depend on one another (e.g., a bond insertion requires both endpoints to already exist). We lift these restrictions by defining flows over general action spaces. As argued in § 2, specifying valid rates from endpoints alone is difficult in this setting, because many action sequences may connect the same endpoint pair. We therefore condition not only on endpoints but also on a full trajectory : a concrete sequence of actions transforming sI into sF . Having defined trajectory-conditioned CTMCs, we now describe how to use their rates as training targets for an unconditional model uθt (s, ·) that depends only on the current state and time. For any endpoint pair (sI , s F ), many valid trajectories may connect them. Therefore, in addition to the coupling γ over endpoints, we also specify a conditional distribution q(τ | sI , s F ) over trajectories from sI to sF . We assume q(· | sI , s F ) is supported on valid trajectories for all (sI , s F ) in the support of γ, and that sampling from q is efficient. Intuitively, q encodes an inductive bias over how 

sI should be transformed into sF : it determines which trajectories contribute to training and with what weight. Analogously to endpoint-conditioned discrete flow matching, one can show: 

Theorem 3.2. Let ut(s, a | τ ) be a conditional rate that generates the trajectory-conditioned path 

pt(s | τ ). Define the marginal probability path and marginal rate as 

pt(s) := Eτ ∼q pt(s | τ ), ut(s, a ) := Eτ ∼q [ ut(s, a | τ ) | St = s ],

where Eτ ∼q is shorthand for E(sI ,s F )∼γ Eτ ∼q(·| sI ,s F ). Then ut generates pt.

Sampling. Sampling proceeds as in endpoint-conditioned DFM: draw sI ∼ psrc and integrate the learned CTMC with rates uθt over t ∈ [0 , 1] . With Euler integration, each step of size h samples each available action a independently with probability h u θt (s, a ) and applies the sampled compatible actions to the current state; see App. D. Although the trajectory-conditioned targets ut(· | τ ) are sparse—only the next action in the sampled trajectory has positive rate—the learned model uθt

approximates the marginal rate (Thm. 3.2), which can place mass on many currently available actions. This allows multiple actions per Euler step, so sampling does not degenerate to sequential generation. In summary, trajectory matching separates three modeling decisions: i) the state and action space ,which determines what transformations are possible; ii) the source and target distributions psrc , p tgt 

and coupling γ, which specify what is transported; and iii) the trajectory distribution q(τ | sI , s F ),which specifies how endpoints are connected through action sequences. 3.3 VARIANCE REDUCTION VIA RAO –B LACKWELLIZATION 

As we have seen, the trajectory-matching objective Eq. (4) recovers the correct marginal flow by supervising the model on a single next action per sampled trajectory. We can sharpen the training signal by observing that multiple trajectories in q(· | sI , s F ) may pass through the same state s at time t, each prescribing a potentially different next action. We now show how to supervise the model on all such actions simultaneously, reducing gradient variance while preserving the optimum. To this end, define the posterior distribution over trajectories given the current state s and endpoints (sI , s F ):

pt(τ | s, s I , s F ) := pt(s | τ ) · q(τ | sI , s F )

The Rao-Blackwellized target is then the expectation of ut(s, a | τ ) under this posterior: 

¯ut(s, a | sI , s F ) := Eτ ∼pt(·| s,s I ,s F ) ut(s, a | τ ).

When this expectation can be computed efficiently (as we show for several trajectory families in § 4), the Rao–Blackwellized rate can replace ut(· | τ ) as the training target in Eq. (4). This preserves the expected gradient and the optimum while reducing gradient variance (Props. B.2 and B.3). Our ablation suggests that this yields faster convergence to comparable final performance (App. G.4). In summary, conditioning on the full trajectory gives a general recipe for defining valid conditional flows over arbitrary action spaces, including settings where endpoint-conditioned rates cannot be specified directly. This yields a simulation-free training objective whose optimum recovers the marginal data-generating flow. Gradient variance can be further reduced by Rao-Blackwellizing the target over all trajectories consistent with the current state and endpoints. 

4 INSTANTIATIONS OF TRAJECTORY MATCHING 

We now instantiate trajectory matching at increasing levels of generality. The two central design choices are the action space and the trajectory distribution q. We illustrate these choices through a running example of molecular graph generation (Fig. 2), moving from independent actions to settings outside the scope of factorized flows. We begin with the most common case: for each endpoint pair, trajectories differ only in the order of actions, while the action set itself is fixed.

τ , and Rao-Blackwellized target rate ¯ut. Independent fixed-action distribution 

The simplest case is when the required actions are pairwise independent and the trajectory distribution is uniform over their orderings. For example, in B OND EDIT , which mirrors DeFoG (Qin et al., 2025), atoms are fixed by the coupling γ and only edges can be inserted or deleted. Any ordering of these independent edge edits yields a valid trajectory. 

Definition 4.2 (Independent fixed-action distribution) . A fixed-action distribution q is independent 

if for every (sI , s F ), the actions in α(sI , s F ) are pairwise independent, and q(· | sI , s F ) is uniform over their orderings. Sampling from q is efficient whenever computing α is. The rate ¯ut also admits a closed form: 

Proposition 4.3. Let q be an independent fixed-action distribution. Then for a state s on a trajectory from sI to sF we have: ¯ut(s, a | sI , s F ) = 1[a ∈ α(s, s F )] · 1/(1 − t).

Thus, all remaining actions receive the same rate, reflecting the order-invariance. Importantly, factorized flow matching arises as the special case of trajectory matching with order-invariant, independent component-wise actions: 

Theorem 4.4. Consider a factorized flow as defined in Eq. (2) with single component actions 

aiv (s) = ( s1, . . . , v, . . . , s d). Let q be the corresponding independent fixed action distribution with 

α(sI , s F ) = {aiv : v = siF̸ = siI }. Then: 1. The trajectory-matching loss (Eq. (4) ) and the factorized flow matching loss (Eq. (1) ) have the same gradient in θ and hence the same minimizers. 2. The Rao-Blackwellized loss (Prop. 4.3, Prop. B.2) is identical to the factorized loss (Eq. (1) ). 

Dependent fixed-action distribution 

Having established that trajectory matching subsumes factorized flows, we now relax the indepen-dence assumption. Dependencies between actions arise naturally in structured domains: in hierarchy generation, adding a child requires its parent to exist; in molecular generation without a fixed atom set, inserting a bond requires both endpoint atoms to exist. A natural way to model such dependencies is through a directed acyclic graph (DAG) over actions. 

Definition 4.5. We equip the action set α(s, s F ) with a DAG whose vertices are the actions and whose edges ai → aj indicate that ai is a prerequisite of aj . We require two actions to be independent if neither is reachable from the other. A trajectory τ is consistent with α(sI , s F ) if its action sequence is a topological ordering of this DAG. Similarly to the independent fixed-action distribution, we define a dependent fixed-action distribution as uniform over all consistent trajectories: 

Definition 4.6 (Dependent fixed-action distribution) . We say q is a dependent fixed-action distribution 

if for all sI , s F every trajectory in the support of q(· | sI , s F ) is consistent with the dependency DAG 

α(sI , s F ) and q(· | sI , s F ) is uniform over all such trajectories. This family interpolates between two familiar regimes: an edgeless DAG recovers the independent fixed-action distribution, while a linear chain DAG recovers fixed-order autoregressive generation. 6Preprint General DAGs lie between these extremes and capture partially ordered action systems such as our G ROW HIERARCHY (a tree grown by attaching children to existing nodes) and F RAG GROW (a molecule grown by attaching ring fragments to a root atom); see Fig. 2 and App. E. Unlike the independent case, the Rao–Blackwellized target ¯ut can assign different rates to the remaining actions. Available actions on which many future actions depend receive higher rates, encouraging them to occur earlier. For forest-structured dependency DAGs, trajectories can be sampled and Rao–Blackwellized rates computed efficiently: 

Proposition 4.7. Let q be a dependent fixed-action distribution whose dependency DAG α(sI , s F ) is a forest. Then for a state s on a trajectory from sI to sF we have: 

¯ut(s, a | sI , s F ) = 1[a ∈ roots (α(s, s F ))] · desc α(s,s F )(a) · 1/(1 − t)

where roots is the set of roots of the DAG and desc (a) is the size of the tree rooted at a (a included). 

The resulting target interpolates between the independent and fully dependent extremes. With no dependencies between actions, it reduces to the uniform rate from Prop. 4.3, supervising every remaining action with equal weight. With a fully sequential chain of dependencies, all weight concentrates on the single next action, recovering fixed-order autoregressive supervision. Between these extremes, every currently available action receives a rate proportional to the size of its remaining subtree. Rao–Blackwellized rates for general dependency DAGs are given in Prop. B.4, but computing them exactly is intractable in general. For the forest-plus-sink dependencies arising in A TOM GROW 

and M OL EDIT , we derive an alternative conditional path with exact, tractable rates (App. E.7). Dependent fixed action distributions extend trajectory matching to partially ordered action systems that factorized endpoint-conditioned flows cannot express. Conditioning on concrete trajectories yields targets that span the full spectrum from unordered to fully sequential actions in a unified way. 

Beyond fixed action sets 

So far we assumed that all trajectories between a given endpoint pair use the same set of actions. In some settings, however, different trajectories between sI and sF involve different action sets, for example because the target admits multiple decompositions. In A TOM GROW , a given ring bond may be created when a new atom is attached by AddAtom or by a separate AddBond action after both endpoints exist. One way to obtain tractable trajectory distributions in such settings is to introduce a latent variable z specifying the decomposition. We express the trajectory distribution as a mixture, which can be sampled efficiently whenever both the latent distribution and the conditional trajectory distribution can be sampled efficiently: 

q(τ | sI , s F ) = Ez∼p(z|sI ,s F ) q(τ | z, s I , s F ). (5) For A TOM GROW , z specifies a spanning tree rooted at the seed atom: tree edges are created by 

AddAtom actions, while other edges require AddBond actions. Conditional on z, the action set and its dependencies are fixed again, forming a forest-plus-sink structure for which we have a tractable conditional path with exact rates (App. E.7). M OL EDIT uses a similar construction (App. E). Importantly, we can also understand the existing insertion- and deletion-based text model EditFlows (Havasi et al., 2025) as an instantiation of trajectory matching over a variable action set; see App. F.1. In summary, trajectory matching provides a flexible and principled framework for designing discrete flows over domain-specific action spaces. It subsumes classical endpoint-conditioned factorized flows as a special case, while naturally extending to complex actions, partial dependencies, and structured generation processes for which direct endpoint-conditioned targets are difficult to specify. 

5 EMPIRICAL RESULTS 

We evaluate trajectory matching on tree and molecular graph generation and show that: (i) domain-aligned action spaces can enforce structural validity by construction , which factorized flows cannot; (ii) our framework supports diverse action spaces over the same domain, where domain-aligned ones can outperform factorized baselines; and (iii) the resulting models transfer without retraining to structured downstream tasks such as substructure extension and linking. Molecular graph generation. We instantiate trajectory matching with four different action spaces over the same domain, illustrating the flexibility of the framework: B OND EDIT (bond edits on a fixed-size atom set, mirroring existing factorized approaches), A TOM GROW (atom and bond insertions from a seed atom), F RAG GROW (ring fragment insertions from a dataset-specific vocabulary), and MOL EDIT (insertions and deletions on noisy initial molecules); see Fig. 2. Tab. 2 reports results on ZINC and MOSES against fixed-size and variable-size baselines. QM9 results are in Tab. 8. Three findings stand out. First, B OND EDIT , our factorized instantiation, matches existing factorized DFM and discrete diffusion baselines (despite using no sampling-time optimizations), showing that the generality of trajectory matching comes at no cost in the factorized regime (Thm. 4.4). Second, ATOM GROW and F RAG GROW outperform the variable-size diffusion baseline GrIDDD (Ninniri et al., 2025) on most metrics, suggesting that directly modeling size-changing actions offers advantages over augmenting factorized diffusion with auxiliary size prediction. Third, F RAG GROW , which encodes the strongest inductive bias by treating ring fragments as atomic actions, achieves very strong DFM results on ZINC and MOSES, most notably lifting MOSES validity from 92.8% to 99.2%. This empirically supports the value of aligning the action space with the structure of the domain. 

Downstream applications. Trajectory matching naturally supports zero-shot transfer to structured downstream tasks by allowing generation to start from meaningful partial states. We demonstrate this on two tasks: substructure extension (extend a partial substructure into a valid molecule containing it) and substructure linking (join two disjoint substructures into a single valid molecule). A single ATOM GROW model trained on ZINC with a random number of root atoms accommodates both directly: extension by setting sI to the given substructure, and linking by setting sI to the union of two disconnected substructures (see Apps. E.3 and G.6). While A TOM GROW can generate extensions and linkers without a fixed target size, adapting the factorized B OND EDIT baseline requires sampling a target size in advance and freezing the conditioning substructure(s). A TOM GROW substantially outperforms the factorized baseline on both tasks, achieving a valid-and-connected fraction of 94.6% vs. 33.8% on extension across 11 substructures, including some not present during training, and 78.8% vs. 57.0% on linking across 6 fragment pairs (see Tab. 3). This works because A TOM GROW ’s trajectories pass through valid partial molecules, precisely the states on which these downstream tasks condition. More generally, trajectory matching makes the path between endpoints a modeling choice: training intermediates can be chosen to align with downstream conditional tasks, rather than being fixed by a factorized endpoint-conditioned construction.

Autoregressive models handle complex actions naturally by generating in a fixed order (Jin et al., 2019; Kusner et al., 2017; You et al., 2018), but commit to that order and to sequential generation. Non-autoregressive edit-based models such as DiffusER (Reid et al., 2022) predict a restricted set of single-token edit operations in parallel, but rely on heuristic reconstruction targets without a principled guarantee that the learned model samples from the data distribution. 

Discrete flow matching. Discrete diffusion (Austin et al., 2021) and DFM (Gat et al., 2024; Campbell et al., 2024) provide a principled framework for non-autoregressive generation that provably matches the data distribution, and have been successfully applied to text (Austin et al., 2021), graphs (Qin et al., 2025; Vignac et al., 2023), and molecules (Poletukhin et al., 2026). However, these methods share a common template restricting the action space: the conditional dynamics are determined by endpoints alone, factorize across components, and are restricted to single-component updates on a fixed-size state. ARDM (Hoogeboom et al., 2022) is an exception to endpoint-conditioning: training over random unmasking orders implicitly conditions on a trajectory, but is restricted to fixed-size, single-token unmasking operations. A small body of work relaxes the fixed-size constraint: Jump diffusion (Campbell et al., 2023) combines continuous-state diffusion with a separate jump process and an auxiliary insertion head that predicts new component values and locations. GRIDDD (Ninniri et al., 2025) similarly augments fixed-size graph diffusion with an auxiliary model that predicts how many nodes to insert at each step. Edit Flows (Havasi et al., 2025), the closest prior work, handles single-token insertions, substitutions, and deletions for text by lifting them into an augmented state space, where they reduce to factorized single-component updates. Variants of this construction have been applied to temporal point processes (Lüdke et al., 2026), 3D molecule generation (Billera et al., 2026), and protein generation (Deutschmann et al., 2026). None of these approaches supports multi-component actions (e.g., fragment attachment) or general actions with explicit prerequisites (e.g., edge insertions requiring both endpoints). Trajectory matching addresses this gap through full-trajectory conditioning, providing, to our knowledge, the first general DFM construction for such actions. It also recovers factorized DFM and Edit Flow rates as special cases, yielding a unified framework. 

7 LIMITATIONS 

Trajectory matching requires specifying both an action space A and a trajectory distribution q, making its performance dependent on domain-specific design choices. The framework is most useful when the actions encode meaningful structure, such as fragment insertions in molecular graphs. In domains where independent component-wise edits already capture the relevant structure, complex actions may offer no advantage. In this case, trajectory matching can fall back to factorized DFM. The tractability of trajectory matching depends on being able to sample from q efficiently and, ideally, to compute the Rao-Blackwellized rates in closed form. For many natural choices—independent fixed-action distributions and forest-structured dependencies—both can be done in linear time. Outside these families, both may require domain-specific constructions (e.g., Eq. (5)), so applying trajectory matching to a new domain can itself require non-trivial methodological work. Our empirical evaluation covers trees and molecular graphs across four datasets. While these settings exercise diverse action structures, evaluating the framework in further domains remains future work. 

8 CONCLUSION 

We introduced trajectory matching, a discrete flow matching framework that operates over user-defined action spaces by conditioning on full action trajectories rather than on endpoints alone. The framework subsumes factorized DFM and extends it to actions that change state size, modify multiple 9Preprint components, or depend on one another. Empirically, domain-aligned action spaces translate into concrete gains across tree and molecular graph generation. A EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

Flow and generator matching. Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023; Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden, 2023; Liu et al., 2022) learns marginal dynamics by regressing against tractable conditional dynamics. Generator Matching (Holderrieth et al., 2024) generalizes this conditional-to-marginal principle to broad classes of Markov processes through their infinitesimal generators, encompassing deterministic flows, diffusions, and continuous-time Markov chains on continuous, discrete, and manifold-valued state spaces. Discrete Flow Matching (DFM) (Campbell et al., 2024; Gat et al., 2024) can be viewed as an instance of this framework on discrete spaces and has been applied to text, proteins, graphs, and molecules (Gat et al., 2024; Campbell et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2025). Widely used DFM constructions define endpoint-conditioned paths on fixed product spaces, factorize the conditional probability paths across components, and allow individual transitions to modify only one categorical component. Within this product-space setting, Shaul et al. (2024) derive kinetic-optimal rates for general categorical probability paths within each component. Trajectory Matching lies within the Generator Matching framework (Holderrieth et al., 2024), with the complete action trajectory serving as the conditioning variable. Our contribution is a general construction of tractable conditional generators over discrete action spaces without requiring a fixed product-space representation or componentwise actions, together with efficient Rao–Blackwellized targets for several important trajectory families. 

Variable-size discrete flows. A few diffusion and flow models relax the fixed-size assumption using specialized size-changing processes. Jump Diffusion (Campbell et al., 2023) combines continuous diffusion with a dimension-reducing deletion process and learns its time reversal, including the rate and transition kernel for inserting new components. GRIDDD (Ninniri et al., 2025) augments graph diffusion with insertions and deletions and an auxiliary model for predicting graph size changes. FlexMDM (Kim et al., 2025) augments masked diffusion for text with a dedicated insertion process: an additional head predicts how many mask tokens to insert between existing positions, while the standard unmasking posterior subsequently predicts their values. Generalized Poisson Flow (Cheng et al., 2026) similarly decomposes variable-size generation into a generalized-Poisson insertion process, which learns the insertion rate and the distribution of newly inserted components, and a within-length flow that refines the current state. Edit Flows (Havasi et al., 2025), the closest predecessor to our construction, defines a CTMC on variable-length sequences with token insertions, substitutions, and deletions. To obtain tractable conditional dynamics, it lifts sequences to an augmented alignment space, where insertions and deletions are represented as transitions between tokens and a special empty symbol. The resulting conditional probability path factorizes over aligned positions, allowing the standard product-space DFM construction to be applied, with each transition modifying only one component of the augmented state. Notably, trajectory matching recovers the Edit Flow probability path and marginal rate by uniformly ordering the alignment-induced edits for a fixed alignment distribution (Prop. F.1). Related size-changing constructions have been applied to temporal point processes (Lüdke et al., 2026), proteins (Deutschmann et al., 2026), and 3D molecules (Billera et al., 2026). In particular, Billera et al. (2026) use a specialized atom split-and-deletion process conditioned on a latent forest that determines the ancestry of split atoms. This construction can be seen as a special case of our dependent fixed-action formulation, which allows general action sets and dependency structures. 15 Preprint In summary, these methods provide specialized constructions for domain-specific size-changing operations, rather than a general recipe for training flows with arbitrary multi-component actions and dependencies. 

Generation order. Generation order is an important modeling choice in discrete generative models. Autoregressive models typically fix a single generation order, whereas Autoregressive Diffusion Models (Hoogeboom et al., 2022) train over multiple componentwise unmasking orders. Standard factorized DFM similarly permits components to be modified in arbitrary order; in our framework, it corresponds to independent actions ordered uniformly at random. Trajectory Matching makes this trajectory distribution explicit by specifying q(τ | sI , s F ) over complete domain-level action trajectories, allowing generation orders to encode domain knowledge and dependencies between actions. Jointly learning this trajectory distribution—as recently explored for token insertion orders by LoFlexMDM (Patel et al., 2026)—is a promising direction for future work. 

Fragment-based molecular generation. Fragment-based molecular generators construct molecules from chemically meaningful substructures rather than individual atoms. Early approaches include junction-tree and hierarchical variational autoencoders (Jin et al., 2019; 2020) and autoregres-sive models that select and attach structural motifs (Maziarz et al., 2024). These methods demonstrate that fragment-level representations can improve chemical validity, scalability, and control over molecular structure, but rely on autoregressive or latent-variable constructions. Fragments have also been incorporated into diffusion and flow-based models. Fragment-based molecular diffusion and flow matching approaches typically generate a fragment graph based on a fixed vocabulary (Levy & Rector-Brooks, 2023; Qiang et al., 2023) and reconstruct atom-level graphs through seperate coarse-to-fine post-processing. Similarly, Poletukhin et al. (2026) generate 3D molecular structures by generating a point cloud of rigid motifs. These approaches use fragments primarily to define a coarser state representation on which a con-ventional diffusion or flow process operates. In contrast, our F RAG GROW instantiation retains an atom-level molecular graph and treats attaching an entire fragment as a single structured action, directly producing the updated atom-level graph without a separate coarse-to-fine decoder.

D TRAINING AND SAMPLING 

Algorithms 1 and 2 give the training and sampling procedures for trajectory matching. We note a few practical considerations. 

Training. The training loop is written in terms of the trajectory τ to make the connection to the theory explicit, but the trajectory itself does not necessarily need to be materialized. In practice, we use a single sampler that takes (sI , s F ) ∼ γ and directly returns a tuple (t, s, u t(s, · | τ )) — or 

(t, s, ¯ut(s, · | sI , s F )) when using the Rao-Blackwellized target. Depending on the chosen trajectory distribution, the sampler can often be efficiently implemented without explicitly constructing the full trajectory τ . For most instantiations of interest, the target rate at s depends only on quantities such as the remaining action set α(s, s F ) or the residual dependency DAG, which can be computed directly from the endpoints and the sampled state. Because the sampling routine is independent of the model, it is well suited to run in CPU dataloader workers in parallel with the forward and backward passes on the GPU. In our QM9 experiments, the observed loader wait is below 1 ms per training step, so sampling and target construction add very little exposed wall-clock time (App. G.1). 21 Preprint

The instantiations above abstract away from node and edge features (atom types, bond orders, charges, etc.). Two natural strategies extend them to feature-labeled graphs. 

Strategy A: joint prediction at insertion. Insertion actions are parameterized by features: 

AddAtom i(v) inserts a new atom with feature v in a single action, and analogously for AddBond ij (b)

and AddFragment . The action vocabulary grows by a factor of the feature alphabet, but no separate feature-edit actions are needed. 

Strategy B: separate feature-edit actions. Insertion actions produce nodes and bonds with a random feature drawn from a fixed distribution; features are then modified by ded-icated ChangeAtomFeature i(v) and ChangeBondFeature ij (b) actions. For settings such as B OND EDIT that already work with a fixed atom set, ChangeAtomFeature and 

ChangeBondFeature become additional edits on the existing structure. 

Tradeoffs and our default. Strategy A reduce the number of actions needed, but lock features in at insertion time. Strategy B keeps features editable throughout the trajectory, which lets the model correct earlier feature choices during sampling, and it composes cleanly with all instantiations above without enlarging fragment libraries by feature combinations. The best choice depend on the task and setting, but we found strategy B to perform best in most experiments. The dependency structures can be easily adapted to accomodate these additional feature-wise edit actions. E.7 EXACT CONDITIONAL PATHS FOR FOREST -PLUS -SINK DEPENDENCIES

All instantiations of trajectory matching presented in this work share a common training and sampling pipeline. The differences between instantiations are isolated to the action space, source distribution, and trajectory distribution as described in App. E; the architecture, loss function, datasets, and hyperparameters described in this section are held fixed across instantiations. 31 Preprint 

Model architecture. We use a graph-transformer backbone proposed by DiGress (Vignac et al., 2023) to predict a rate for every node and every node pair in the current state s, processed jointly by a stack of attention layers with node and pair representations updated in tandem. We use the additional positional encoding by DeFoG (Qin et al., 2025). We adopt the graph transformer unchanged in structure, with two practical adaptations: (i) we additionally include the current node count |V (s)| as an input feature, encoded with a radial-basis-function (RBF) embedding, because our variable-size instantiations operate over states of varying size and the appropriate rate depends on how much of the generation has already been completed; and (ii) for the Tree benchmark we use a substantially smaller backbone than for the molecular datasets, while all molecular datasets share the same backbone size as in DeFoG. The architecture is permutation-equivariant on nodes and node pairs by construction, so the predicted rates are also permutation-equivariant. 

Loss decomposition. The trajectory-conditioned target ut(· | τ ) is sparse: only the next action in τ

has positive rate at the current state s, while the Rao-Blackwellized target ¯ut(· | sI , s F ) has positive rate only on the small set of remaining actions α(s, s F ). G.4 TRAJECTORY -C ONDITIONED VS . R AO -B LACKWELLIZED TARGETS 

Trajectory matching admits two training targets: the trajectory-conditioned rate ut(· | τ ) defined in Eq. (3), and its Rao-Blackwellized counterpart ¯ut(· | sI , s F ) obtained by marginalizing over trajectories consistent with the current state and endpoints. Prop. B.2 guarantees that the two targets share the same optimum and expected gradient, while Prop. B.3 predicts that the Rao-Blackwellized estimator has component-wise lower or equal variance. We test the empirical impact of this variance reduction on training dynamics. Fig. 9 compares the two targets on ZINC under A TOM GROW , plotting validity over the first 40 

training epochs while keeping all other hyperparameters fixed. The Rao-Blackwellized estimator reaches ≥ 95% validity within the first few epochs and exhibits less epoch-to-epoch noise; the trajectory-conditioned estimator requires roughly twice as many epochs to reach the same threshold and remains noisier throughout. Both targets converge to comparable final validity ( ∼ 97 –99% ), consistent with Prop. B.2: the optimum is unchanged, only the trajectory toward it is smoother. 36 Preprint
