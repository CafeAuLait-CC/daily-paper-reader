## Abstract 

On the simplest non-Euclidean manifold, the 1D flat torus, we exhibit a distribu-tion where a recently proposed extension of Riemannian flow matching is globally optimized by a velocity field that pushes samples away from the data distribution. We trace this failure mode to a property we call compatibility ; a likelihood-based flow matching objective is compatible if its population minimizer recovers the marginal velocity field induced by the data and coupling. We give a general con-dition for compatibility, apply it to existing methods, and bound the worst-case error in terms of a residual component vanishing under compatibility. Next, we note that existing compatible objectives put density on velocities that are infeas-ible on compact manifolds. We propose an objective built from an exponential family over endpoints, with the conditional velocity as sufficient statistic, which is both compatible and supported only on feasible velocities. This manifests as a reweighting of the flow matching loss which, on constant-curvature manifolds, treats errors in flow speed and direction differently. Empirically, our method re-covers the correct velocity field on the test case, outperforms prior methods on high-dimensional product tori, and obtains substantial NLL improvements over prior work on two of four standard geospatial benchmarks on the sphere at mod-erate runtime overhead. On a large dataset of high-dimensional human poses, our method outperforms prior work according to sample-based metrics. 

## 1 Introduction 

Much of the success of modern generative modelling can be attributed to the development of transport-based models, which learn to deform a simple base distribution into the target data dis-tribution. These include diffusion models [Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015, Ho et al., 2020] and normal-izing flows [Tabak and Turner, 2013, Rezende and Mohamed, 2015, Papamakarios et al., 2021]. In particular, conditional flow matching models [Lipman et al., 2023, Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden, 2023, Liu et al., 2023] are effective at generating images [Hu et al., 2024], video [Jin et al., 2025], and heterogeneous tabular data [Guzmán-Cordero et al., 2025]. These models learn the transport map in terms of a time-dependent vector field and thus fall within the model class of continuous normalizing flows [Chen et al., 2018]. To overcome the scalability challenges faced by other models in this category, flow matching decomposes the intractable marginal probability path into simpler conditional paths coupling the base and data distributions. The idea is to learn the marginal velocity field by regression onto many examples of the simple fields generating these conditional paths. Many datasets are inherently non-Euclidean and more naturally modelled as distributions on curved manifolds of nontrivial topology. The dominant generalization of flow matching to manifolds is the Riemannian flow matching (RFM) model of Chen and Lipman [2024]. Recently, Eijkelboom et al. [2024] observed that the Euclidean flow matching objective can be seen as maximizing the likelihood of a fixed-covariance Gaussian model qθt (x1 | xt) of the true endpoint posterior pt(x1 | xt). We characterize loss functions for flow matching in terms of their likelihood model, whether that is explicit (as in RG-VFM) or implicit (as in RFM). We delineate two properties. First, we say a loss function is compatible if its population optimum gives the true marginal flow induced by the data and coupling (section 3.2). This is a minimal correctness check for an objective to be generally useful. Second, we say it is support-aware if the corresponding likelihood puts mass only on velocities that are achievable (section 3.4). These are independent properties in the sense that RFM is compatible but not support-aware on compact manifolds, while RG-VFM is support-aware but not compatible (table 1). Our theoretical contributions are: • We derive a general compatibility condition for likelihood-based objectives, satisfied by RFM but not RG-VFM. We show this analytically on the simplest non-Euclidean manifold, the flat torus T1. This general condition is most relevant in the manifold setting, but applies to any flow matching problem in which the conditional paths are nonlinear. • We show that the squared error in the local flow scales with the product of distributional fit and a residual term. The residual is zero for a compatible model, eliminating this source of error even when the likelihood model is misspecified. • We view RFM as maximizing likelihood over velocities in the tangent space under a Gaus-sian model of fixed covariance. This reveals how, despite being compatible, it omits global structure from its training signal. One example is compact manifolds, where the maximum flow speed is limited. Here, the RFM likelihood puts density on infeasible velocities. This motivates our proposed method, which is both compatible and support-aware. We use an expo-nential family in endpoint space with the conditional velocity as sufficient statistic. This enforces compatibility regardless of the underlying geometry, inducing a geometrically preconditioned re-gression loss with the RFM pipeline otherwise unchanged. Experimentally, we first evaluate on a simple distribution on T1 where the ideal behaviour of all models is analytic. When a model is incompatible, we see rapid spatiotemporal variation in the learned velocity field while ODE solvers have issues. We further evaluate on high-dimensional tori and four real-world geospatial datasets, following Chen and Lipman [2024] and De Bortoli et al. [2022], as well as on a dataset of human poses in SO(3) 19 with nearly 12 million training samples.

Practical computation. At first, it seems we must compute the mean map mx,t and covariance 

Vx,t at each training step, online and via numerical differentiation. The insight is that the more symmetries a manifold has, the fewer scalar functions Vx,t depends on. We obtain a formula for each scalar function via analytical differentiation under the integral sign. In fact, on many manifolds, we pretabulate these quantities with quadrature in low dimensions, often just one. Different datasets on a given manifold can reuse the same lookup table. On the flat torus Td, the covariance Vx,t is diagonal with each entry an independent function of ηi. All factors then share a single 1D lookup table. On spaces of constant curvature, isotropy cuts Vx,t down to two scalar functions of the magnitude. On product manifolds, Vx,t is block diagonal with one block per factor. This lets us compose tables and highlights that on many manifolds, the table dimension stays low as the manifold dimension grows. In training, we query the lookup tables in O(1) time, with minimal overhead (section F).

## 7 Related Work 

Guzmán-Cordero et al. [2025] previously suggested, based on the ideas of Eijkelboom et al. [2024], that likelihoods over endpoints should be exponential families with a parameter of interest as the sufficient statistic. Their work targets heterogeneous tabular data, relying on the identity ut(xt) =

As this work establishes, the nonlinearity of conditional paths on manifolds means that such constructions do not generally possess compatibility. Prior intrinsic generative models include Riemannian CNFs [Mathieu and Nickel, 2020], Moser Flow [Rozen et al., 2021], CNF Matching [Ben-Hamu et al., 2022], and score-based [De Bortoli et al., 2022] and ELBO-based diffusion [Huang et al., 2022]. However, as Chen and Lipman [2024] discuss, these require simulated random walks [Huang et al., 2022, De Bortoli et al., 2022], ap-proximations to the heat kernel [De Bortoli et al., 2022], heavy divergence computation [Huang et al., 2022] or Hutchinson estimation [Mathieu and Nickel, 2020]. Earlier simulation-free methods [Rozen et al., 2021, Ben-Hamu et al., 2022] scale poorly to high dimensions and general geometries. With the exception of RG-VFM, recent manifold extensions of flow matching mostly specialize RFM to particular geometries or applications [as in Cheng et al., 2024, Miller et al., 2024, Cheng et al., 2025a], or propose an alternative method for a single manifold class [as in Collas et al., 2025]. An orthogonal line of work focuses on accelerated inference. Consistency models [Song et al., 2023] distil one-step maps from intermediate states to data, and Riemannian consistency models [Cheng et al., 2025b] port this idea to manifolds. Similarly, flow maps [Boffi et al., 2025] use self-distillation for few-step inference, and Davis et al. [2026] propose Generalized Flow Maps for manifolds. 

## 8 Conclusion 

At population optima, likelihood maximization generically fails to recover the true marginal velocity. Our compatibility condition characterizes when it succeeds. Our residual bound shows that compat-ibility is enough to avoid this source of error even if the likelihood is misspecified. On compact manifolds, prior work satisfying the condition assigns Gaussian likelihood to infeasible velocities. We propose an exponential family in endpoint space that preconditions the loss while being both compatible and support-aware. Empirically, our method recovers the expected velocity in the ana-lytic test case, improves NLL on two spherical benchmarks, and improves sample-based metrics on high-dimensional tori and human poses. ## C Implementation details 

C.1 Network output and domain of the mean map 

For a full, regular exponential family with positive carrier and bounded sufficient statistic, under minimality Ax,t is strictly convex. Hence, mx,t (η) = ∇η Ax,t is one-to-one and maps the natural-parameter space onto the relative interior of the convex hull of feasible conditional velocities. Thus, 

m−1 

> x,t

(v) exists when v lies in that interior. At finite interior parameters, Vx,t = ∇2 

> η

Ax,t is positive definite under minimality. If v approaches the boundary, some covariance eigenvalues can tend to zero, hence V −1 

> x,t

does have the potential to become ill-conditioned or diverge in the limit. We claim our notion of support-awareness wherever the conditional velocity field is well-defined. This is the same prerequisite already required by RFM for the regression target, and by RG-VFM in the endpoint-to-velocity decoder—both assume that couplings with non-unique probability paths have measure zero. That is to say, we do not introduce branch dependence at cut loci as an additional assumption or limitation of our method. Our endpoint likelihood induces density only on velocities produced by the conditional map from endpoints to velocities, relative to a fixed choice of branch. In implementation, the network predicts an unconstrained velocity vθt (xt) ∈ Txt M. We evaluate the surrogate loss only where the mean map is invertible. Outside this region, we use a barrier-style extension obtained by clamping the mean-parameter argument before table inversion. This does not alter the population minimizer provided it lies in the interior, because the practical loss agrees with the surrogate loss on that interior and is larger outside it with large penalty gradients pointing inwards. On tori we clamp per coordinate while on spheres we clamp the norm. Across the selected checkpoints in our reported experiments, the fraction of predictions for which the bar-rier remained active was zero. Notwithstanding potentially large gradients near the boundary of the mean-parameter domain, we do not observe any stability issues in practice and did not require 21 gradient clipping, warmup, or other special techniques to train our method. We scale our loss to be on the same scale as the RFM loss by omitting the factor of 12 .

C.3 Implementation on less symmetric manifolds 

In principle, the usual steps of our method work on less symmetric manifolds—compute the mean and covariance of the conditional velocity under the endpoint family, numerically invert the mean map, and cache the resulting functions. Instead of quadrature, Monte Carlo could be feasible; tak-ing advantage of the exponential family structure lets the quantities be expressed as expectations. Computing them offline and interpolating seems the most plausible route. Neural or piecewise op-timization is plausibly most useful in either inverting the mean map, or selecting good choices of nodes and weights for quadrature. Generally, if there is a particular high-dimensional manifold sup-porting several datasets of interest, it would be prudent to learn the function approximations once, reusing them across datasets.

## E Experimental methodology and evaluation 

E.1 Evaluation framework 

Accurate measurement of generative performance on manifolds is important but many tools from the Euclidean setting are not immediately to hand. Here we discuss validity of common evaluation metrics and lay out our choices. 

Wasserstein distances. Wasserstein distances are a natural way to measure generative perform-ance [Villani et al., 2009] but are costly to compute. Sliced Wasserstein mitigates this in Euclidean space by reducing optimal transport via projection, but generalizations require Radon transform in-jectivity [Kolouri et al., 2019]. This is automatic in Euclidean space but manifolds require a choice of projection family. Attempts to apply sliced Wasserstein to manifolds thus introduce additional geometric structure, for example, global projections on Cartan–Hadamard manifolds [Bonet et al., 2025], where geodesic structure is well behaved, or spectral slicing constructions based on Laplace– Beltrami eigenfunctions [Rustamov and Majumdar, 2023]. Their metric guarantees depend on the chosen projection family being separating, and practical finite implementations are truncated ap-proximations. 

Kernel methods. Methods based on kernels can work well, but their validity depends on RKHS conditions. The energy distance [ED; Székely and Rizzo, 2013] is a nonparametric discrepancy re-quiring the space to have strong negative type to be a valid metric. On T1, geodesic distance has negative type but not strong negative type [Lyons, 2020]. We can convert to strong negative type by square root [Lyons, 2013], setting dED (x, y ) = √dist g (x, y ) in the ED formula. Kokkendorff [2002] proves compact Riemannian manifolds of negative type and dimension ≥ 2 are simply con-nected so geodesic distance on Td is not of negative type and the same trick does not work for d ≥ 2.Maximum mean discrepancy [MMD; Gretton et al., 2012] is a related discrepancy defined often with a bandwidth-parametrized Gaussian kernel. Feragen et al. [2015] show the Riemannian generaliza-tion of this kernel is not positive definite on manifolds, suggesting the Laplace kernel specifically for 

Sd (which includes T1). In our experiments, we use the median heuristic to set the Laplace kernel bandwidth. 

Our choices. Based on the above, we report Wasserstein distances for our analytic test case, where the data distribution is an equally weighted mixture of two Diracs, simplifying optimal transport (alongside the pedagogical reasons, we chose the singular form of the distributions precisely so that Wasserstein is feasible), alongside the unbiased forms of energy distance with the square-root trick and MMD with the Riemannian Laplace kernel. For the real-world geospatial benchmarks, we found the small test-set size commonly resulted in negative values for the unbiased kernel methods despite their population validity. As these are well-established benchmarks in the literature with many prior reported results, we think it reasonable to only report NLL. However, this means we cannot compare to RG-VFM as its NLLs are uninformative; see section B.2 for further discussion. As Zaghen et al. [2026] do not report NLLs in their experiments, we were unable to validate our replication directly according to this metric.

E.3 Benchmark protocols 

The analytic test case, torus sweep, and geospatial experiments use a single NVIDIA A100-SXM GPU with 80 GB of memory. The efficiency studies use the hardware specified in section F. We follow the RFM setup published by Chen and Lipman [2024] and extend its codebase to support our method and additional evaluation metrics. This splits the data into 80 % train, 10 % validation, and 10 % test, giving us the same data splits as in the RFM paper. We use seed values 0–29, 0– 4, and 0–4 for our training runs on the analytic test case, high-dimensional torus, and geospatial datasets respectively. Our backbone is a standard multilayer perceptron with time concatenated to the input. This employs Swish [Ramachandran et al., 2018] with learnable parameter and Adam with an exponential moving average [Polyak and Juditsky, 1992] (decay 0.999). For synthetic datasets, we train for 50 000 iterations with batch size 512 and 3 hidden layers of width 512. For the geospatial datasets, we train for 1 000 000 iterations with batch size and hidden width following Chen and Lipman [2024]. The analytic test case uses dopri5 as the ODE solver with tolerance 1 × 10 −5 while the high-dimensional torus uses 1 000 Euler steps with per-step projection for scalability following Chen and Lipman [2024]. ## F Computational performance 

F.1 Adaptive sampling time on SO(3) n

Sampling for our human pose experimental results in table 5 used Euler integration with 1 000 steps (following the original RG-VFM implementation and for the reasons we outline in section B.2). Here, we further provide inference NFEs and sampling timing under adaptive dopri5 (table 8). Our method only differs from RFM in the backward pass, while the forward pass is through an MLP of the same architecture. Therefore, under Euler sampling they have the same sampling cost, while under adaptive sampling the sampling time depends on the neural approximation learned. In many cases, flow matching under independent coupling has optimal solutions exhibiting signific-ant spatiotemporal variation (motivating ideas around rectification and choosing better couplings). For methods possessing compatibility, we posit that their relative ordering measured by adaptive solver NFE depends on the true marginal field induced by the data. Notably, ODE solving at a tolerance of 10 −5 for our method and RFM is comparable to the forward pass of HuProSO3’s autoregressive normalizing flow. Although HuProSO3 does not require numerical integration, it has a relatively heavier neural architecture and its authors note they have not optimized all potential parallelism [Dünkel et al., 2024, §B.1].

F.4 Compute accounting 

For the T1 experiment, the 30 seeds complete in under 2 h across all three trained methods, as each run finishes in under 1 min . On high-dimensional product tori, training is essentially independent of the dimension, at ∼1 min /run, but the final test/NLL evaluation scales with d, pushing per-run wall time from a few minutes at low d to around 1 h at d = 400 ; the entire sweep over 5 seeds and 3 methods across all reported dimensions d ∈ { 1, 2, 5, 10 , 20 , 50 , 100 , 200 , 400 } consumes roughly 

30 h of A100 time in total. On the geospatial datasets, a single run of training and evaluation com-pletes in under 5 h , with the sweep for one method of 5 seeds on one dataset taking under 24 h ;aggregated across the four datasets and the two methods we train ourselves (our method and our reproduction of RFM), this totals roughly 200 h of A100 time. The end-to-end training benchmark of section F.2 runs separately on the RTX 3090, contributing a negligible extra amount. This puts aggregate compute for the analytic test case, torus sweep, and geospatial experiments on the order of 

250 h of A100 time. The research project as a whole has required extra compute beyond the figures above—chiefly preliminary solver and tolerance sweeps for RG-VFM (a subset of which is reported in section B.2) and early prototypes that did not make the final paper—for which we did not retain detailed accounting. 30
