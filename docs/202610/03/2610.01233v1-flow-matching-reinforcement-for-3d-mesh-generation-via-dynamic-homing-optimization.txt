ABSTRACT
Flow matching is central to 3D generation, yet in practice its reinforcement learn-
ing (RL) methods are largely adapted from 2D visual generation. Representative
DPO-, GRPO-, and NFT-style objectives, when applied to negative trajectories,
mainly steer predicted velocities away from the corresponding directions without
explicitly specifying a target velocity field toward preferred samples. In 3D gener-
ation, constrained by pretrained model capabilities, rollout diversity, and reward-
distribution complexity, directly applying these RL methods yields limited gains
in geometric quality. We introduce a forward-process RL method Dynamic Hom-
ing Optimization (DHO), which reformulates negative-trajectory optimization as
positive-sample attraction-guided dynamic homing. Specifically, Minimum-Cost
Attractive Matching (MAM) assigns each negative sample a distinct positive tar-
get, and Time-Aware Dynamic Correction (TDC) then redirects its trajectory to-
ward the target using a remaining-time-aware corrective velocity. Building on
asynchronous online DHO, we develop Flow3D-Pro, an image-to-3D geometry
generation framework. Experiments show that DHO outperforms representative
DPO-, GRPO-, and NFT-style objectives in 3D generation, while Flow3D-Pro
produces higher-quality 3D geometry than existing mesh generation methods.

Inspired by recent positive-sample attraction approaches Deng et al. (2026b), we propose Dynamic
Homing Optimization (DHO), which constructs negative-sample branch objectives with attraction-
guided dynamic homing. DHO explicitly specifies both a preferred-sample destination for each
negative trajectory and the corrective dynamics required to reach it. Specifically, we introduce
Minimum-Cost Attractive Matching (MAM) to formulate preferred-target selection as a batchwise
minimum-cost one-to-one assignment, pairing each negative sample with a distinct positive sample.
Given the assigned target, Time-Aware Dynamic Correction (TDC) constructs a remaining-time-
aware velocity that redirects each intermediate state toward its target.
DHO performs policy updates on forward-process interpolants constructed from rollout endpoints, is
sampler-flexible, and does not require storing entire sampling trajectories. In addition, we develop
Flow3D-Pro, an image-to-3D mesh generation framework, which leverages asynchronous online
DHO to improve geometric fidelity and structural plausibility. Our main contributions are as follows:
• We present Dynamic Homing Optimization (DHO), a forward-process RL method for
flow matching that equips the negative-sample branch objectives with positive-sample
attraction-guided dynamic homing, and introduce Flow3D-Pro for high-quality image-to-
3D mesh generation with asynchronous online DHO.
• In DHO, we introduce Minimum-Cost Attractive Matching (MAM) to assign each negative
sample a distinct positive target through batchwise minimum-cost one-to-one matching,
and Time-Aware Dynamic Correction (TDC) to construct a remaining-time-aware correc-

RELATED WORK
2.1
3D MESH GENERATION
Continuous-time generative models underpin modern synthesis: diffusion models established high-
fidelity stochastic generation Ho et al. (2020); Song et al. (2021); Karras et al. (2022), while flow
matching Lipman et al. (2023) and its rectified flow instantiation Liu et al. (2023) learn velocity
fields for transport, with related stochastic-interpolant and fast-sampling variants further improving
efficiency Albergo et al. (2025); Song et al. (2023); Liu et al. (2024); Yan et al. (2024). In 3D gen-
eration, DreamFusion Poole et al. (2023) and Magic3D Lin et al. (2023) popularized 3D mesh opti-
mization via 2D diffusion distillation, followed by higher-quality variants Wang et al. (2023); Chen
et al. (2023). Native 3D generators have progressed from point or implicit representations Nichol
et al. (2022); Jun & Nichol (2023) to SDF and shape-latent models Cheng et al. (2023); Zhang
et al. (2023); Zhao et al. (2023), as well as textured-mesh and controllable asset pipelines Gao et al.
(2022); Zhang et al. (2024); Li et al. (2025b). Recent rectified-flow 3D systems—including TREL-
LIS Xiang et al. (2025; 2026), Hunyuan3D Zhao et al. (2025); Yang et al. (2025); Lai et al. (2025a),
TripoSG Li et al. (2025c), LATTICE Lai et al. (2025b) and other mesh flow generators Sun et al.
(2026b); Li et al. (2026)—mainly study pre-training, representation, or sampling, whereas DHO
studies RL post-training for 3D rectified-flow geometry generators.
2.2
REINFORCEMENT LEARNING FOR FLOW MATCHING
RL was adopted early for language-model alignment Guo et al. (2025); Rafailov et al. (2023), and
later extended to visual generation using human feedback Kirstain et al. (2023) and reward-guided
fine-tuning algorithms Xu et al. (2023); Lee et al. (2023). Diffusion reinforcement spans policy-
gradient tuning (DDPO Black et al. (2024)), direct preference optimization (Diffusion-DPO Wallace
et al. (2024)), and preference-tuned visual generation variants Fan et al. (2023); Clark et al. (2024).
For flow matching, representative objectives: GRPO-style methods Liu et al. (2025a); Xu et al.
(2026), represented by Flow-GRPO Liu et al. (2025a), treat generation as online RL and penalize
low-reward rollouts, with related variants refining stability, reward density or policy granularity Xue
et al. (2025); Deng et al. (2026a); Luo et al. (2026); Li et al. (2025a). DPO-style adaptations suppress
negative trajectories through pairwise preference ratios Liu et al. (2025b); Wu et al. (2025a). NFT-
style methods Li et al. (2025d), represented by DiffusionNFT Zheng et al. (2026), inject negative
velocity-space gradients on the forward process. In 3D generation, flow-based RL methods (e.g.,
Flow3D-OPD Ning et al. (2026)) are primarily adapted from these 2D visual generation algorithms.
However, these objectives mainly push the velocity away from directions that produce negative tra-
jectories, without explicitly specifying a target velocity field that guides generation toward preferred
samples, limiting gains in geometric quality. DHO addresses this by dynamically homing nega-
tive samples toward preferred regions. PFM Kim et al. (2024) trains an additional preference-flow
model on rejected-to-preferred interpolants, and applies it to outputs from the frozen base model at
inference, potentially introducing a source-distribution mismatch. DHO directly updates the original
generative base field on noise-to-negative interpolants using matched positive endpoints.

3.3
FLOW3D-PRO
Building on DHO, we develop Flow3D-Pro, a two-stage framework for image-to-3D mesh gen-
eration. Directly recovering detailed 3D geometry from a 2D reference image remains challeng-
ing. Following prevailing multi-stage paradigms Lai et al. (2025b); Xiang et al. (2026); Sun et al.
(2026a), Flow3D-Pro decomposes generation into a lifting stage (Stage I) and a refinement stage
(Stage II). Stage I lifts the 2D reference image into 3D space to establish the global geometry and
6


spatial arrangement of the mesh, whereas Stage II refines the resulting mesh with finer geometric
details. The lifting stage is built upon Hunyuan3D 2.1 Yang et al. (2025), while the refinement stage
is derived from LATTICE Lai et al. (2025b).
Stage I establishes the global geometry and structural initialization—including the overall shape,
pose, and spatial arrangement—that constrain subsequent refinement. We therefore focus post-
training on Stage I, whose high output diversity during 2D-to-3D lifting provides a broader explo-
ration space for RL to discover improved policies. Specifically, Stage I is initialized from pretrained
weights and then optimized in two sequential post-training phases: SFT, followed by DHO. Stage
II remains frozen at its pretrained weights and is used only at inference time to refine the geometry
produced by Stage I, primarily by enhancing local surface details.
3.3.1
ASYNCHRONOUS ONLINE DHO
Flow3D-Pro rolls out G meshes conditioned on each reference image. For each mesh, we render six
views and provide them together with the reference image to Gemini 3.1 Pro, which serves as the
reward model and assigns a scalar reward based on generation quality. Gemini evaluates each mesh
along three dimensions: reconstruction fidelity, geometric plausibility, and orientation alignment.
Reconstruction fidelity assesses the consistency of silhouette proportions and surface appearance
with the reference image. Geometric plausibility considers the validity of the overall shape and its
consistency across views. Orientation alignment evaluates whether the generated mesh is aligned
with the coordinate axes, which helps the learning of symmetric structures. By jointly considering
these three dimensions, Gemini assigns each mesh a reward score from 1 to 5 (higher is better).
In practice, reward evaluation by Gemini introduces substantial and highly variable latency across
meshes. To improve training efficiency, we adopt an asynchronous online DHO framework follow-
ing Zhou et al. (2026), decoupling mesh rollout and reward evaluation from policy optimization.
We allocate N GPUs to rollout and M GPUs to policy updates. Each rollout worker holds a fixed
snapshot of the latest released policy weight while generating a complete mesh group. Once Gemini
evaluation is complete, the scored group is inserted into a rollout replay buffer. In parallel, the trainer
workers continuously sample evaluated groups from the buffer and update the current policy using
the DHO objective. The trainer periodically publishes a new policy snapshot, which rollout workers
fetch before starting subsequent rollouts. Data generated by outdated policy versions are discarded
to limit policy staleness.
We balance policy synchronization and replay-buffer utilization through an update-to-buffer ratio.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
DATASET
We collect artist-created and AI-generated meshes and process them through several steps: (a) data
filtering, (b) watertight conversion, (c) point-cloud sampling and signed distance field (SDF) extrac-
tion, and (d) manual curation. Human annotators select high-quality meshes by considering global
shape quality and local geometric details, resulting in 5K meshes for SFT. For DHO, we collect 1K
diverse reference images, covering 3D and 2D cartoon styles as well as photorealistic subjects. The
collection spans humans, animals, vehicles, architecture, plants and other object categories.
We assess generalization on two evaluation sets disjoint from training. The first, GenMesh-Test, is
our curated set of 200 challenging reference images (comparable in scale to the evaluation set of Lai
et al. (2025b)) covering diverse object categories, visual styles, and levels of geometric complexity.
The second, LATTICE-Bench, is the evaluation set adopted by LATTICE Lai et al. (2025b).

4.2
IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
DHO is trained for 5K optimization steps with AdamW at a learning rate of 2.5 × 10−6. Training is
performed in bfloat16 with a per-GPU batch size of 2. We set η = 0.1, β = 1.0, the rollout group
size to G = 8, and the CFG scale to 3.0. For asynchronous optimization, we allocate N = 28 GPUs
to rollout and M = 4 GPUs to policy updates, with λmin = 4, λmax = 8, and K = 16. Stage II is
kept frozen during inference. Full training and inference configurations are in the appendix.
4.3
EVALUATION METRICS
We evaluate the image-to-geometry generation through various metrics including ULIP Xue et al.
(2023; 2024), Uni3D Zhou et al. (2024), following Lai et al. (2025b); Xiang et al. (2026), alongside
a comprehensive User Study (US) to assess perceptual quality. Human experts rank the generated
meshes of U methods from best (U - 1) to worst (0), revealing relative preference relationships. We
prioritize the User Study as our primary subjective metric for mesh generation.
4.4
COMPARISON OF REINFORCEMENT LEARNING OBJECTIVES
To facilitate a controlled comparison of RL objectives for Stage I, we evaluate the base model, SFT,
DHO, and representative RL methods: GRPO-style, DPO-style and NFT-style. All RL variants start
from the same SFT weight, share the reward model, and are evaluated on GenMesh-Test. Every
Stage I output is processed by the same frozen Stage II refiner. We repeat each experiment with three
different random seeds and report the mean results. Implementation details are in the appendix.
Table 1 shows that DHO obtains higher scores than the other RL methods, especially on the User
Study, our primary metric, demonstrating the advantage of positive-sample attraction-guided dy-
namic homing. Moreover, the improved Stage I outputs provide a more favorable basis for Stage II
refinement, indicating that better Stage I geometry carries over to higher final mesh quality.
Figure 5 further illustrates qualitative differences among RL objectives. GRPO-style, DPO-style,
and NFT-style produce implausible geometry (four fingers), convert image texture into unintended
geometry (a flame-shaped forehead), or damage an otherwise intact structure (a broken staircase).

5
CONCLUSION
We present Dynamic Homing Optimization (DHO), a forward-process RL method for flow match-
ing that equips negative-sample branch optimization with attraction-guided dynamic homing. DHO
combines MAM to assign preferred positive targets with TDC to construct homing velocities toward
them. We instantiate DHO in Flow3D-Pro using asynchronous online post-training. Experiments
show that DHO outperforms representative DPO-, GRPO-, and NFT-style objectives in mesh gener-
ation, while Flow3D-Pro achieves superior image-to-3D geometry quality over existing methods.

A.2
REWARD DESIGN
We provide the reward model Gemini with the reference image and six canonical-view renders of one
generated mesh. Following common VLM-as-a-judge practice, the prompt specifies the evaluator
role, input evidence, assessment criteria, and output format. We use the following prompt template:
Role. You are a strict 3D asset reviewer. Given a reference image and six canonical-view renders of a
generated mesh, evaluate the visible geometric evidence. Inspect all views before assigning a score.
Reconstruction fidelity. Compare the generated mesh against the reference. For silhouette proportions,
check whether the mesh introduces unsupported parts that alter the outer contour, loses or breaks visible
contour-defining parts, or poorly reconstructs salient regions such as the face, hands, or prominent acces-
sories. For surface appearance, penalize image textures, color boundaries, or material patterns that are
incorrectly converted into physical protrusions, grooves, or other geometry.
Geometric plausibility. Check whether structures that should be symmetric remain balanced. Penalize
isolated structures with no support from the reference or the object’s geometry. Across views, verify
that detail density is coherent: unseen or occluded regions should contain neither implausibly elaborate
additions nor conspicuously sparse details relative to corresponding visible regions.
Orientation alignment. Determine whether the mesh is aligned with the canonical world axes rather than
inheriting the diagonal tilt, perspective skew, or camera pose of the reference image. A dominant object axis
should appear horizontal or vertical in canonical views, and bilaterally symmetric objects should appear
macroscopically balanced in the front view.
Output. Briefly justify each of the three dimensions above. First, assign each dimension an individual
score from 1 to 5, where a higher score indicates better generation quality. Then, summarize the scores
using a weighting ratio of Reconstruction Fidelity : Geometric Plausibility : Orientation Alignment = 3 :
2 : 1, and return a single integer reward from 1 (severe defects) to 5 (high fidelity, plausible geometry, and
canonical alignment). Output the final reward in the form <answer>[1--5]</answer>.
As illustrated in Figure 7, the reference image and six canonical views jointly provide evidence
for evaluating fidelity, plausibility and orientation. Guided by the prompt template, the reward
model first assesses reconstruction fidelity, geometric plausibility, and orientation alignment, and
then aggregates the three assessments to produce an integer reward. The example demonstrates how
the final reward is grounded in explicit observations of the generated geometry.

A.3
DHO PSEUDOCODE
Algorithm 1 summarizes the DHO optimization procedure. For each prompt, rollout samples are
first ranked using group-normalized advantages. Positive- and negative-advantage samples form the
positive and negative sets, respectively, and the larger set is subsampled to obtain balanced sets for
one-to-one assignment. The retained positive samples are weighted by their normalized positive
advantages, while each negative sample later inherits the weight of its assigned target. CFG is
applied to both rollout generation and policy updates, with s = 1 corresponding to the CFG-free
19


setting. Note that Algorithm 1 only presents the core DHO update, while Flow3D-Pro executes
sample rollout and policy optimization asynchronously.
MAM computes distances in latent feature space between the two sets and solves a minimum-cost
assignment using the Hungarian algorithm, yielding a distinct positive target for every negative
sample. After sampling the flow time and constructing forward-process interpolants, TDC converts
each assignment into a time-aware regression target that accounts for the remaining displacement
to the positive endpoint. The positive branch retains the standard flow-matching target through
self-assignment. Finally, DHO combines the advantage-weighted positive and negative losses with
velocity-space regularization toward the frozen reference policy, and updates the policy using the
resulting objective.

B.1
MORE IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
Training and inference configurations for our method.
Stage I is based on the Hunyuan3D
2.1 Yang et al. (2025) architecture. SFT uses a learning rate of 5 × 10−6 and is trained for 5K steps
on 32 H20 GPUs with a per-GPU batch size of 4. DHO is trained for 5K optimization steps with
AdamW at a learning rate of 2.5×10−6. Training is performed in bfloat16 with a per-GPU batch size
of 2. We set the homing coefficient η to 0.1, the regularization coefficient β to 1.0, the rollout group
size G to 8, and the CFG scale to 3.0. For asynchronous training, we allocate N = 28 GPUs to
rollout and M = 4 GPUs to policy updates, with λmin = 4, λmax = 8, and K = 16. Although DHO
supports both ODE- and SDE-based rollouts, in practice, we favor SDE sampling for greater rollout
diversity. After DHO training, Stage I inference uses 50 ODE sampling steps. The output voxel
representation of Stage I has a resolution of 518. Stage II is based on LATTICE Lai et al. (2025b)
and uses a shape VAE to decode structure-conditioned latents into an implicit geometric field for
mesh extraction. Meshes are extracted at a decoding resolution of 1536. It uses 5 ODE sampling
steps and a CFG scale of 5.0, and remains frozen for geometric refinement during inference.
RL objective comparison settings.
In the RL objective comparison experiments, for a fair com-
parison, all RL variants share the Stage I architecture, SFT initialization, reward model, and
GenMesh-Test evaluation set, and their outputs are processed by the same frozen Stage II refiner.
Our implementations of GRPO-style, DPO-style, and NFT-style methods follow Flow-GRPO Liu
et al. (2025a), Flow-DPO Liu et al. (2025b); Wu et al. (2025a), and DiffusionNFT Zheng et al.
(2026), respectively. The positive and negative samples in DPO-style are paired using MAM, and
βDPO−style is set to 5000.0. For GRPO-style, we reparameterize Flow-GRPO’s SDE from its origi-
nal data-to-noise time convention into the equivalent noise-to-data formulation adopted in this work.
We set the noise-level κGRPO−style to 0.2, matching the setting used in DHO. βNFT−style is set to
1.0. All methods use asynchronous online training, with GPU configurations held constant across
methods for both training and inference. Training is performed in bfloat16 with a per-GPU batch size
of 2 and a rollout group size of G = 8. Inference uses 50 ODE sampling steps. All RL objectives
are trained for 5K optimization steps using AdamW with a learning rate of 2.5 × 10−6.
B.2
USER STUDY EVALUATION CRITERIA
For relative preference scoring, technical art experts are asked to sort all generated meshes from
highest to lowest. The main considerations for the User Study are fidelity and plausibility. Fidelity
mainly reflects how recognizably the generated mesh conveys the identity and overall proportions of
the reference image, while plausibility mainly reflects whether the resulting shape reads as a coherent
and well-formed 3D object in its own right. Experts make this judgment holistically from their
professional experience rather than following a prescribed checklist. All evaluations are conducted
in a blind manner: method identities are anonymized and the presentation order is randomized for
every sample. Each reference image is independently evaluated by three experts, who rank the
outputs of all methods from best to worst. The resulting ranking scores are averaged across the
three experts and then across all reference images. Human experts rank the generated meshes of U
methods from best (U - 1) to worst (0), revealing relative preference relationships.

This section provides additional quantitative and qualitative evaluations of the key components of
DHO. For the component studies, we use the same Stage I architecture, training pipeline, reward
model, and evaluation protocol, varying only the factor under study. Since each User Study produces
a relative ranking of the variants within its own comparison group, these scores should be interpreted
within each table or figure rather than across different ablations. We conduct a series of ablation
studies on Stage I and evaluate the resulting models on GenMesh-Test.
Target assignment.
We first isolate the target-selection mechanism in MAM. All training and
evaluation settings are held fixed while MAM is replaced with random pairing, nearest-neighbor
matching, or soft attraction. Adapted from the positive-sample attractive component of Drifting
21


Models Deng et al. (2026b), soft attraction uses a normalized similarity-weighted aggregation over
all retained positive samples to produce a soft multi-target direction for each negative sample. This
comparison examines whether minimum-cost one-to-one assignment provides a more effective hom-
ing target than unconstrained or soft diffuse alternatives.
Figure 8: Effect of the target-assignment strategy in DHO.
Figure 8 shows that MAM obtains the highest scores on all three metrics, outperforming soft at-
traction as well as random and nearest-neighbor pairing. Soft attraction aggregates multiple positive
samples and can retain a diffuse multi-target direction near equilibrium, whereas MAM assigns each
negative sample a single positive target under a one-to-one constraint. These results support the use
of deterministic, minimum-cost one-to-one assignment over unconstrained or soft multi-target guid-
ance.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Designing a reward that comprehensively characterizes the geometric quality of a reference-image-
guided 3D mesh remains difficult, and our current dimensions are not fine-grained enough for
higher-precision geometric properties to be accurately reflected. We plan to further decompose the
reward dimensions and collect feedback from more professional artists to refine the reward design
and improve generation quality.
In addition, DHO is applied only to the lifting stage while the refinement stage remains frozen,
since the latter is tightly conditioned on the Stage I output and its rollouts therefore exhibit limited
diversity for policy optimization. We plan to explore ways of enriching the rollout diversity of the
Stage II.
In current 3D generation, constrained by pretrained model capabilities, rollout diversity, and reward-
distribution complexity, DHO with positive-sample attraction-guided dynamic homing achieves bet-
ter mesh post-training quality than representative DPO-, GRPO-, and NFT-style objectives, which
steer predicted velocities away from negative-trajectory directions without explicitly specifying a
target velocity field toward preferred samples. Since 2D visual generation differs from the 3D setting
in pretrained priors and reward distributions, we plan to carry out further exploratory experiments to
examine the performance of DHO in 2D visual generation.
