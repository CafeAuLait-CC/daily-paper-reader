Abstract 

Counterfactual generation seeks to sample outcomes under a hypothetical intervention or decision using observational data collected under the factual assignment mechanism. We develop a flow-matching approach that combines a sample-split, doubly robust training objective with a learned coupling between observed source outcomes and target outcomes drawn from a fitted conditional outcome model. To enable finite-step generation, we leverage a score-corrected stochastic sampler based on a Gaussian-smoothed interpolation. Our main theoretical contribution is a coupling-sensitive KL bound for constant-step Euler discretization: the error is controlled by moments of the source–target displacement under the chosen coupling, rather than by global uniform regularity of the velocity field, and has near-linear dependence on the ambient dimension. We also establish finite-sample non-parametric guarantees for the learned velocity and score fields when both the conditional outcome model and the source–target coupling are estimated from data. These bounds separate approximation, coupling-replacement, nuisance-estimation, generalization, and Monte Carlo errors and, combined with the sampler analysis, yield an end-to-end guarantee for counterfactual generation. Experiments on synthetic and semi-synthetic image benchmarks support the coupling-dependent theory and show that, at finite discretization budgets, the stochastic sampler can outperform the corresponding deterministic ODE sampler.

21 Introduction 

Counterfactual generation seeks to model the distribution of outcomes that would arise if a treatment were assigned throughout a target population. The full distribution reveals heterogeneity, variability, and tail behavior that an average alone cannot capture. With observational data, however, treatment assignment may depend on background covariates. Individuals who receive treatment a can therefore differ systematically from the target population, so a generator trained directly on their outcomes may reproduce this selection bias. Concretely, we observe data ( C, A, Y ), where C denotes background covariates, A is the treatment, and Y ∈ Rd is the outcome. Fix a treatment value a. The observed and target outcome distributions average the same conditional outcome law over different covariate distributions:

The observed law weights covariates as they occur among individuals who received treatment a; the target instead retains the covariate distribution of the full population. Under overlap, this target is identified from the observed-data law. If consistency and conditional exchangeability also hold, it coincides with the interventional outcome distribution under treatment a (Hernán and Robins, 2020; Dance et al., 2026). This structure suggests transporting observed outcomes to the target rather than generating the target from an unrelated reference distribution. Given a coupled source–target pair ( Y0, Y 1), flow matching learns a time-dependent velocity field by regressing the displacement Y1 − Y0 along paths between the paired endpoints. The coupling determines which source and target outcomes are connected. Structured choices, such as optimal-transport couplings, can shorten these displacements and facilitate flow learning (Pooladian et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023). Counterfactual flow matching nevertheless poses three linked problems: the target-dependent training loss must be estimated from observational data, the source–target coupling must itself be learned, and the learned dynamics must be simulated accurately with finitely many steps. Recent work primarily addresses the first problem through doubly robust training. DoubleGen (Luedtke and Fukumizu, 2025) combines a prediction oracle ̂ PY |C=c,A =a for PY |C=c,A =a with an estimate of the inverse propensity αa(c) = 1 /P(A = a | C = c). Oracle samples provide conditional training losses, while observed treatment-a outcomes provide inverse-propensity-weighted residual corrections. This construction trains a generator directly for the counterfactual target. In a complementary approach, Dance et al. (2026) use PY |A=a as the flow source and derive a debiased objective that accounts for its dependence on the observed-data law. They also extend the construction to learned optimal-transport couplings. Appendix B.1 gives a detailed comparison with their method. Our focus is the joint effect of coupling estimation, field estimation, and finite-step simulation. Existing theory relates flow straightness to finite-step Wasserstein error (Bansal et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2023), but straightness is a property of the induced flow and cannot be read directly from the selected coupling. Other analyses rely on global Lipschitz bounds (Zhou and Liu, 2025; Liu et al., 2026; Guan et al., 2026b), which reflect worst-case regularity over the state space. Neither approach yields a guarantee controlled directly by the source–target displacement under the chosen coupling. Appendix B provides a broader comparison with related finite-step sampling results. Examples 1 and 2 in Section 2.4 isolate the two resulting obstacles. Even with fixed Gaussian smoothing, Example 1 constructs couplings whose displacement approaches zero while the velocity derivative diverges and the Euler ODE sampler has infinite KL error. Without smoothing, Example 2 3shows that arbitrarily small displacement does not control the integrated squared derivative of the score. Together, the examples motivate stochastic sampling, positive smoothing, and regularity control averaged along the interpolation rather than uniformly over the state space. We address these obstacles with a Gaussian-smoothed interpolation and a score-corrected SDE. With exact velocity and score fields, the SDE preserves the interpolation marginals and reaches the smoothed target. We estimate the propensity model, prediction oracle, and entropic optimal-transport coupling on an auxiliary data split, then train the velocity and score fields on an independent split using doubly robust objectives. Our analysis combines displacement-dependent discretization control with finite-sample field bounds, separating smoothing, coupling, estimation, and numerical errors in an end-to-end guarantee. Our main contributions are as follows. • Score-Corrected Stochastic Sampling. We analyze a score-corrected SDE for a Gaussian-smoothed interpolation with constant smoothing. The two counterexamples delineate the re-spective roles of stochasticity and positive smoothing in obtaining coupling-dependent finite-step control. • Coupling-Sensitive Discretization Theory. For constant-step Euler discretization, we prove a KL error bound controlled by 

D5 = (E∥Y1 − Y0∥5)1/5, (1) rather than by global uniform regularity. The fifth moment is the highest displacement moment required by our derivative bounds; its empirical analogue is a coupling diagnostic. With exact fields and the prescribed noise tuning, Euler-step complexity is near-linear in dimension for fixed smoothing, D5, and target accuracy. • Finite-Sample Learning and End-to-End Guarantees. We derive finite-sample bounds for sample-split, doubly robust velocity and score estimators when the conditional outcome model, propensity model, and source–target coupling are learned from data. The bounds separate neural approximation, coupling replacement, nuisance estimation, generalization, and Monte Carlo errors. Combined with the sampling analysis, they yield an end-to-end W1 guarantee relative to the unsmoothed counterfactual target. • Empirical Validation. Experiments on synthetic and semi-synthetic image benchmarks support the predicted dependence on coupling quality and show that, at finite discretization budgets, the score-corrected SDE can outperform the corresponding deterministic ODE sampler.

# C Reader’s Guide and Proof Roadmap 

This section lists the subclaims needed for the end-to-end result and points to the proof of each one. 

Appendix Organization. Appendix A provides experimental details and additional results. Appendix B gives additional related-work details first. We then collect the notation and algorithmic details, give the proofs of Examples 1 and 2, and present the proof of Theorem 1, the coupling and field-estimation analyses, and the proof of Theorem 2. 

Motivating Examples. The proofs of Examples 1 and 2 are given in Appendices E and F, respectively. 

Sampling Theorem. Theorem 1 follows from the following chain. 1. Marginal Correctness of the Exact SDE. Lemma 1 shows that qt solves the Fokker–Planck equation of the score-corrected SDE and satisfies the integrability and continuity conditions required by the superposition principle. It therefore yields a weak solution Z with Zt ∼ qt for every t ∈ [0 , 1]. 2. Girsanov KL bound for the Euler process. Lemma 2 localizes the exact-to-Euler drift perturba-tion, applies Girsanov before the stopping time, and passes to the unstopped process by weak convergence and lower semicontinuity of KL. Proposition 3 verifies the pathwise integrability condition when the drifts and sample paths are continuous. 22 3. Posterior Derivative Formulas. Lemmas 3 and 4 express the score, velocity Jacobian, velocity Hessian, score Jacobian, and score Laplacian as conditional moments of D = Y1 − Y0 and the Gaussian variable G.4. Integrated Derivative Bounds. Lemmas 5 and 6 convert the posterior identities into L2(qt)bounds using conditional variance contraction, Cauchy–Schwarz, and explicit Gaussian mo-ments. 5. Euler Increment Bounds. Lemmas 7 and 8 apply Itô’s formula along the exact SDE. The score proof uses the continuity equation to cancel Dxst vt, leaving only the Hessian, mixed velocity–score, and pure score terms controlled by the integrated derivative bounds. 6. Collection of Constants. The proof of Theorem 1 substitutes the Euler increment bounds into the Girsanov reduction and records the explicit coefficients Ah and Bh, together with the explicit τ 4, τ 6, and displacement terms. Corollary 1 then adds the Gaussian smoothing error and converts KL to W1 using a maximal coupling, Pinsker’s inequality, and a second-moment bound for the Euler recursion. 

Field-Estimation Propositions. The proof of Proposition 1 first derives Proposition 5 and then verifies its hypotheses for the velocity and score losses. 1. Coupling Construction and Replacement Error. Lemma 9 proves Lipschitz stability of the EOT conditional kernel. Lemma 10 constructs an ideal kernel Q with the required source and target marginals, and Corollary 3 controls the joint-law discrepancy between Q and ̂ Qn.2. Exact Excess-Risk Identities. For both fields, conditional projection identifies excess population loss with the grid-averaged squared L2(qtk ) field error. The score proof uses the conditional-mean parametrization su(x, t ) = {u(x, t ) − x}/σ 2, for which the Gaussian term cancels from the loss. 3. Approximation. Propositions 6 and 7 construct network approximations uniformly on a bounded cube and control the Gaussian tail outside the cube. 4. Loss Perturbations. The coupling-replacement error is controlled using Corollary 3, together with the endpoint Lipschitz bound for the loss. For the doubly robust error, we first write the population bias as EC [(1 −̂ α/α a)( mv −̂ mv)]. We then control mv −̂ mv through the conditional-generator W1 error and the stability of ̂ Qn, and apply Cauchy–Schwarz to obtain the final product bound. 5. Statistical Generalization. Lemma 13 controls the empirical-to-population difference for any bounded loss class from a proper finite cover. The two field analyses supply their own network covering numbers and loss-to-network transfer constants. 6. Monte Carlo Approximation. We first decompose the implementation error according to the structure of the doubly robust loss and define separate observed-correction and auxiliary Monte Carlo classes. For each class, the corresponding error can be written as the difference between a conditional expectation and its realized Monte Carlo average. Lemma 13 is then applied conditionally to each array. A key step is to condition on the training observations and fitted auxiliary objects, which gives the conditional independence required in Proposition 5. 7. End-to-End Substitution. Combining the preceding velocity- and score-field steps gives the integrated errors ε2vel and ε2score . Substitution into the explicit sampling theorem, followed by the Wasserstein conversion, proves Theorem 2. 23 Technical Novelty. Our proof makes two main contributions. First, for fixed Gaussian smoothing, we obtain near-linear rather than quadratic dimension dependence in the number of uniform Euler steps, with D5, σ, and target accuracy held fixed. We bound velocity derivatives and mixed velocity–score terms using displacement moments, and keep the noise factors in the score terms explicit. With exact fields, choosing τ 2 = σD 5/d then reduces the dimension dependence and makes every discretization term shrink with D5, including the pure-score terms. This gives both improved dimension dependence and full coupling-sensitive control of the discretization error. Second, we give an end-to-end analysis of counterfactual generation with a learned source–target coupling, rather than only independent pairing. We analyze a doubly robust loss that combines prediction-based losses with weighted corrections from observed outcomes, rather than the usual flow-matching loss. Because the training loss itself is approximated using finitely many random draws from the prediction oracle and the coupling, we bound the error caused by this Monte Carlo approximation. We also separate this error from the finite-data error and the Monte Carlo error in the target samples used to learn the coupling. Combining these learning bounds with the Euler and smoothing errors gives a guarantee for the final output distribution.
