# ABSTRACT 

Trajectory crossing remains a critical bottleneck in Flow Matching (FM), and previous works typically view these crossings from a theoretical optimization perspective causing velocity averaging. They attempt to address it indirectly by post-hoc distillation or endpoint coupling, without explicitly regulating the intermediate trajectories. In this paper, we introduce a new network learning perspective: crossing points inherently induce large local Lipschitz constants in the target velocity field, leading to two drawbacks. First, high Lipschitz constants correspond to high-frequency signals in the velocity field that neural networks struggle to fit due to spectral bias. Second, they also imply drastic velocity variations, leading to severe numerical integration errors in few-step inference. To alleviate this, we propose CoFlow, a framework that introduces the contrastive learning paradigm into FM to explicitly repel trajectories during training, thereby lowering the local Lipschitz constants of the velocity field. Specifically, we formulate CoFlow from a Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE) perspective by injecting a repulsive drift term. This drift actively guides the forward process of “positive samples” away from “negative trajectories”, effectively reducing the local Lipschitz constant. Furthermore, we derive an equivalent stochastic interpolant formulation from this SDE, providing a simple and tractable design space to control the influence of negative samples. Extensive experiments on ImageNet 256 ×256 demonstrate that CoFlow significantly reduces FID compared to standard FM in few-step inference (e.g., 20 steps), with no added training overhead. # 1 INTRODUCTION 

Continuous-time generative models, formulated as stochastic differential equations (SDEs) or ordinary differential equations (ODEs), have driven remarkable breakthroughs in high-quality visual synthesis. 1arXiv:2610.01408v1 [cs.CV] 1 Oct 2026 Preprint Specifically, diffusion models (Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2020) first gradually corrupt data with noise according to a predefined forward SDE and then learn a score function to reverse this process. However, probability paths induced by these forward SDEs are highly curved, thus requiring hundreds of sampling steps during inference. To overcome this, recent flow matching (FM) (Lipman et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022; Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden, 2022) enables the construction of straight probability paths and directly learns a velocity field, substantially reducing the required sampling steps. Although designed to straighten paths for rapid sampling, standard flow matching still requires numerous steps (e.g., 50–100) in practice to generate high-fidelity samples. From a theoretical optimization perspective, this gap stems from trajectory crossings (Liu et al., 2022): when trajectories cross 1, the marginal velocity at intersections inherently becomes the expectation of conflicting velocities, yielding abrupt changes in the velocity field. During inference, these drastic velocity variations will amplify the truncation errors of numerical ODE solvers, thus degrading few-step generation performance. To alleviate this issue, prevailing approaches primarily operate on the boundaries: they either rely on post-hoc distillation to bypass intermediate integration (Yin et al., 2024; Song et al., 2023; Salimans & Ho, 2022; Liu et al., 2022), or optimize endpoint couplings to reduce crossings (Tong et al., 2023; Davtyan et al., 2026). While both approaches are effective, a more straightforward direction seems to remain unexplored: why not fix the simple couplings and directly adjust the intermediate trajectories during training to explicitly mitigate crossings? 

To bridge this gap, we first examine trajectory crossing from a neural network learning perspective, and use the local Lipschitz constant to quantify the smoothness of the velocity field. As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), when trajectories cross, spatially adjacent points have drastically different velocity targets, causing the Lipschitz constant of the target velocity field to be extremely large. This large Lipschitz constant leads to two critical drawbacks: 1) It corresponds to high-frequency signals in the velocity field, which neural networks struggle to fit due to spectral bias (Rahaman et al., 2019). This will make the velocity field difficult to learn accurately. 2) Theoretically, a high Lipschitz constant directly amplifies the truncation error of ODE solvers, degrading few-step sampling quality. ො𝑥 𝑡  ො𝑣 𝑡

Building upon this perspective, a compelling direction emerges: Explicitly repelling these conflicting trajectories during training to suppress the Lipschitz constant should enable the network to learn a smoother ( i.e. , with a lower local Lipschitz constant, see Fig. 1(b)) velocity field. Motivated by this, we introduce CoFlow , a novel framework that systematically rectifies training trajectories via neg-ative samples. Drawing inspiration from contrastive learning (Chen et al., 2020; He et al., 2020), CoFlow explicitly forces the trajectory of a target sample (positive) to repel adjacent negative paths ( e.g. ,paths targeting other data points in a mini-batch). Theoretically, we demonstrate that this dynamic is mathematically equivalent to injecting a repulsive drift into the forward stochastic process, closely related to Doob’s h-transform (Zhou et al., 2023). From this SDE we can further derive a closed-form, stochastic interpolant (SI) formu-lation (Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden, 2022), which provides a flexible design space to control the influence of negative samples. We fur-ther prove that, when the repulsive drift vanishes at both endpoints, CoFlow still maps the source distribution to the target distribution, as in standard flow matching. A 2D experiment (Fig. 3) provides some initial evidence for our motivation, showing that CoFlow can learn a velocity field with lower Lipschitz and decrease the truncation errors during infernce. A closely related contemporary work is DeltaFM (Stoica et al., 2025), which also explores negative samples by introducing an auxiliary contrastive loss to the standard objective to penalize velocity similarity with negative samples. Despite sharing a similar motivation, DeltaFM focuses on velocity-level alignment rather than altering intermediate trajectories, as shown in Fig. 2. Notably, another theoretical contribution of our work is proving that the optimal velocity field learned by DeltaFM is mathematically equivalent to an inference-time affine transformation of the standard FM velocity field. Leveraging this insight, we can seamlessly incorporate this transformation into CoFlow at inference time, achieving further performance gains without extra training overhead.

In summary, our main contributions are as follows: 

1) A Lipschitz perspective on trajectory crossing. We quantify trajectory crossing via the local Lipschitz constant, showing that its explosion amplifies numerical truncation errors and thereby degrades few-step sampling quality. 

2) CoFlow framework inspired by Doob-h transform. We propose CoFlow, which explicitly repels conflicting trajectories via negative samples during training to suppress local Lipschitz constants, thus learning a smoother velocity field. We derive an exact closed-form formulation that enables efficient training without costly numerical simulation. 

3) Theoretical insight into DeltaFM. We theoretically prove that DeltaFM is mathematically equivalent to an inference-time affine transformation, revealing its machanism and allowing it to be combined with CoFlow for further performance gains. 

# 2 RELATED WORKS 

Few Step Generation. Improving generation quality with few sampling steps is an important goal in diffusion and flow-based models. Existing efforts to achieve this can be broadly categorized into three paradigms. The first paradigm focuses on optimizing noise-data coupling during training to explicitly reduce trajectory crossing, such as Minibatch OT-CFM (Tong et al., 2023). The second paradigm relies on post-hoc rectification or distillation. For example, ReFlow (Liu et al., 2022) and InstaFlow (Liu et al., 2023b) generate new synthetic data-noise pairs to retrain the model, while distillation methods like DMD (Yin et al., 2024; Salimans & Ho, 2022; Song et al., 2023) attempt to directly distill a pre-trained model for one-step generation. Recently, a third paradigm has emerged, which introduces the concept of mean velocity by incorporating extra conditioning to naturally enable single-step sampling, like Shortcut Model (Frans et al., 2024) or MeanFlow (Geng et al., 2025).CoFlow explores a complementary approach: directly adjusting intermediate training trajectories using negative samples to encourage a smoother velocity field with lower local Lipschitz constants, thereby supporting more accurate few-step sampling. 

Contrastive Learning in Generative Models. Contrastive Learning (He et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2020) is a powerful learning paradigm in deep learning, fundamentally relying on negative samples to benefit training. While extensively utilized in models like GANs (Goodfellow et al., 2014), its usage into the training of continuous-time generative models has remained relatively underexplored. Interestingly, at inference time, Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) (Ho & Salimans, 2022; Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021) acts as a de facto contrastive mechanism, pushing the target conditional generation 3Preprint trajectories away from unconditional ones. The remarkable effectiveness of CFG strongly implies that explicitly incorporating contrastive learning into the training phase holds great potential. Pioneering work includes Drifting Models (Deng et al., 2026) and DeltaFM (Stoica et al., 2025). 

# 3 METHOD 

In this section, we detail the formulation of CoFlow, a framework that incorporates negative samples into continuous-time generative modeling. Typically, continuous-time generative models can be described from two complementary perspectives: the stochastic diffusion perspective (e.g., score SDEs (Song et al., 2020)) and the stochastic interpolant(SI) perspective (Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden, 2022), which directly constructs the conditional probability path. Currently, SI has emerged as the prevailing paradigm (Ma et al., 2024), as it provides a more flexible design space. However, it often obscures the underlying physical intuition behind the stochastic diffusion dynamics. Therefore, to rigorously introduce our method, we leverage the connection between these two paradigms. Specifically, we first introduce the mechanism of CoFlow from the SDE perspective, providing an intuitive motivation for injecting a repulsive drift via negative samples. Subsequently, we derive its equivalent formulation within the stochastic interpolant framework. Finally, we demonstrate how to train a velocity field in our framework. 3.1 PRELIMINARIES

The standard forward diffusion trajectory described in Eq. (1) transports a source point x0 towards a random destination in the prior p1. However, in many practical scenarios, such as image-to-image translation (Zhou et al., 2023; Chung et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023a; Kawar et al., 2022), the source-target pairs are explicitly defined. Thus, it becomes necessary to modify the forward trajectory such that it deterministically terminates at a specific target state x1.This can be achieved via Doob’s h-transform (Zhou et al., 2023). Specifically, conditioning the SDE on a fixed terminal endpoint x1 at t = 1 introduces an additional drift term to Eq. (1), yielding the conditional bridge SDE that terminates at x1:

# 4 EXPERIMENTS 

Experimental Setup . We evaluated CoFlow on class-conditional ImageNet 256 ×256 using SiT (Ma et al., 2024) backbones. Images were encoded into the latent space using a pre-trained SD-VAE (Rom-bach et al., 2022) before training. Unless otherwise specified, all models were trained for 400K steps with a global batch size of 256, using AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) with a constant learning rate of 1 × 10 −4 and no weight decay. For evaluation, we generated 50K images using a 20-step Euler–Maruyama (EM) sampler by default. We reported FID (Heusel et al., 2017), sFID (Nash et al., 2021), Inception Score (IS) (Salimans et al., 2016), Recall (Sajjadi et al., 2018), and Precision (Sajjadi et al., 2018). All remaining settings followed the SiT configuration. 4.1 COMPARISON WITH STANDARD FLOW MATCHING 

Setting . We compared CoFlow with standard FM using SiT-B/2 and SiT-XL/2 using the training protocol described above. For each method, we trained with and without REPA. For CoFlow, it was trained using the state residual as the negative signal, λ = 0 .2, and β(t) = t(1 − t), as described in Sec. 3.4. The results in Table 1 are obtained without classifier-free guidance (CFG). 

Few-Step Generation . Table 1 reports generation quality at NEF = 20 . Without REPA, CoFlow reduces FID from 61.43 to 52.79 on SiT-B/2 and from 38.50 to 29.65 on SiT-XL/2. The other four metrics also improve and the improvements are retained when using REPA. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of CoFlow in improving few-step generation. Fig. 4 provides a qualitative comparison using the 20-step EM sampler. 

Effect of Sampling Budget . Fig. 5 reports FID-10K across different numbers of evaluation (NFE). CoFlow substantially outperforms standard FM at low NFEs, and this gap generally narrows as NFE increases, with two methods achieving similar FID at 250 NFEs. This trend is consistent with our motivation: learning a smoother velocity field with lower local Lipschitz constants can decrease numerical truncation errors under few sampling steps. As the sampling budget increases, finer discretization reduces these errors for both methods, diminishing the advantage of CoFlow. These results suggest that CoFlow primarily improves sampling efficiency in the few-step setting. 4.2 COMPATIBILITY WITH OTHER METHODS

Negative signal. We first used random noise as signal to test whether random perturbations alone could explain CoFlow’s gains. This control changes FID only slightly, from 86.99 to 86.49, suggesting that simply perturbing the trajectories cannot bring the observed improvement. We then compared three signals derived from negative trajectories: the state, endpoint, and velocity residuals. The state residual (xrt − xt) performs best across all three metrics, achieving an FID of 79.55. We hypothesize that the state residual is more suitable to reduce local Lipschitz constants, as it guides repulsion using the relative positions of trajectories. 

Sampling policy. We compared the Random, Nearest, and Lipschitz policies described in Sec. 3.4. Although negatives can be selected using states at time t, the selected negatives may change as t

varies, introducing discontinuities in the stochastic interpolant in Eq. (12). To avoid this, we used endpoint states x0 to select negatives for the Nearest and Lipschitz policies as a practical compromise. It turns out all three policies improve upon standard FM, with Lipschitz achieving the lowest FID of 78.12. This is consistent with our motivation to improve few-step generation by reducing local Lipschitz constants. Because random sampling remains competitive with an FID of 79.55, we adopt it as the default policy for its simplicity. 

Strength factor. We varied λ ∈ { 0.05 , 0.2, 0.5, 1.0}, with λ = 0 corresponding to standard FM. Increasing λ from 0.05 to 0.5 improves FID from 85.66 to 75.06. However, increasing λ further to 1.0 degrades FID to 87.43, slightly worse than standard FM. These results show that moderate repulsion improves generation quality, while an excessively strong adjustment can be detrimental. 

# 5 CONCLUSION 

This paper presents CoFlow, a framework that utilizes negative samples to repel crossing training trajectories, which is inspired by Doob-h transform. Motivated by the relationship between trajectory crossing and local Lipschitze constants, CoFlow adjusts intermediate to encourage a smoother velocity field. We derived a closed-form stochastic interpolant that preserves the endpoint distributions and enables simulation-free training. Experiments on ImageNet 256 × 256 demonstrate improved few-step generation and compatibility with methods like REPA and OT-CFM. We also developed an inference-time velocity adjustment that is equivalent to DeltaFM, which can further improves CoFlow without additional training. Our findings suggest that contrastive learning can serve as a mechanism for adjusting training trajectories to improve few-step generation in flow matching. 9Preprint
