Abstract
Few-step distillation reduces the sampling cost of diffusion and flow models, but existing approaches
often require auxiliary score models or specialized architectures. We show that simple on-policy
velocity regression already provides a strong foundation for 4-NFE generation. To represent teacher
velocity variation within each coarse transition, we introduce Pivot, a training-only residual proxy
that separates local corrections from the student’s interval-mean prediction. We further repel
the student from weaker teacher predictions, providing a lightweight mechanism for avoiding
undesirable modes. The resulting framework, Simplified Matching Distillation (SMD), remains
entirely regression-based, requires neither online score estimation nor adversarial training, and
leaves the deployed generator unchanged. We evaluate SMD on image generation and editing, 3D
generation, video synthesis, and joint audio–video generation, scaling to 33 billion parameters. At
4-NFE, SMD outperforms strong few-step baselines and surpasses multi-step teachers on several
benchmarks. It also supports cross-model, multi-teacher, and causal-video distillation.

Introduction
Diffusion and flow models [2, 3, 4, 5] have become a foundation for image, video, and 3D generation [6,
7, 8, 9, 10]. Their exceptional generation quality, however, comes with a heavy inference burden, as the
inherent denoising sampling typically requires tens to hundreds of sequential function evaluations.
This high computational overhead thus creates a significant bottleneck for real-world deployment and
interactive applications, making inference efficiency an urgent priority. Few-step distillation [11, 12, 13,
14, 1] is the way to address this challenge. By compressing a pretrained teacher model, the denoising
process could be significantly reduced to a few steps, enabling rapid generation without sacrificing,
and sometimes even enhancing the original generation quality [15]. Consequently, few-step distillation
now has become an important and indispensable stage to post-train a frontier diffusion foundation
model.
Among existing distillation paradigms, on-policy distillation, which trains a few-step student on states
generated by the student itself, has emerged as the dominant approach for accelerating diffusion
models. Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD) [11] is no doubt standing as a pillar of this paradigm
and has inspired a series of follow-up works [15, 16, 17, 18]. By re-noising on-policy student generated
samples, DMD approximates a reverse-KL gradient using the score difference between the teacher
and the student, where the student score is dynamically tracked by an online fake model [11]. Despite
its remarkable progress, DMD and its variants often suffer from low-diversity, unstable training
dynamics and over-saturated output. To mitigate these issues, recent trajectory-based methods such
as π-Flow [14] and PDD [1] simplify training by directly supervising student rollouts with teacher
predictions within each sampling interval. Although this removes the need for online score estimation,
the few-step generator must still represent the dense velocity field within each interval, relying on
either a learned policy [14] or a parallel decoder [1], which adds architectural components.
We show that simple on-policy velocity matching is already a strong baseline for few-step diffusion
distillation. Our approach requires no auxiliary fake score model, architectural modification, or
elaborate policy design. Instead, it simply matches the student velocity predictions to the teacher
velocities queried at the same states, while keeping the student velocity constant within each sampling
interval. This baseline produces strong 4-NFE results, with photorealistic detail and varied outputs.
Finding 1. Direct velocity matching between student and teacher prediction is already a strong
baseline for few-step diffusion distillation.
Building on this finding, we further apply this simple baseline to a variety of diffusion teachers [19, 7, 8].
However, when distilling video generation models, we observe that training dynamics can occasionally
suffer from instability, as shown in Figure 11. Through the curvature analysis on the velocity field, we
observe actually within a coarse integration step, the dense teacher velocities can vary substantially [20],
especially for modeling complex and high-dimensional modality like video, hence exposing a single
student prediction to varying targets over an entire interval to calculate the imitation loss will make the
optimization conflict and jitter. To address this issue, we introduce Pivot proxy, a lightweight, training-
only residual network that captures local velocity variations via a proxy path. By decoupling local
velocity modeling from the overall student prediction, Pivot proxy stabilizes training while incurring
no inference overhead. To further correct the student errors, we exploit weaker predictions from the
same teacher as informative negative references and introduce a repulsive regularization term, which
steers the student away from these inferior predictions without compromising faithful imitation of
the teacher target. We term the resulting framework Simplified Matching Distillation (SMD). True to
its name, SMD remains entirely regression-based. It requires no modification to the architecture or
inference pipeline of the few-step generator, and involves neither score estimation nor adversarial
objectives.

Such simplicity makes SMD both practical and scalable. Trained purely on-policy without any target

3
Method
SMD combines a simple on-policy velocity-regression baseline with two training-only refinements.
For each coarse transition of the student rollout, the baseline queries a frozen teacher at intermediate
states along the student’s current path and matches a single coarse student velocity to the resulting
local predictions. Pivot augments this transition with an endpoint-preserving proxy path whose
local velocity can vary within the interval, while reference repulsion complements the target teacher
prediction with a weaker prediction from the same teacher. Together, these components define SMD.
Both refinements are removed at inference, preserving the student architecture and sampler.
Throughout this section, vT denotes the frozen target teacher prediction, including its native guidance
rule, and vθ denotes the student. Conditioning is implicit unless stated otherwise.
3.1

idea. We match the student’s velocity to the teacher’s local predictions at states along the student’s
own coarse transition. Despite the simplicity of both the state construction and the matching objective,
this formulation already enables effective few-step distillation.

3.3
Repulsion from a Reference Prediction
We further refine the distillation signal using an additional prediction from the same teacher as
a negative reference. We complement imitation of the target prediction with repulsion from this
reference, as illustrated in Figure 10(b). We retain vT(xτ, s) as the imitation target and denote the
reference prediction at the same state and time by evT(xτ, s). Its construction depends on the teacher’s
conditioning and inference procedure, as described below. We optimize
L = Eτ [∥v(τ) −vT(xτ, s)∥2 −λ ∥v(τ) −evT(xτ, s)∥2] ,
0 ≤λ < 1.
(17)
The first term matches the target teacher prediction, while the second encourages separation from the
reference prediction. Setting λ = 0 recovers imitation alone.
Constructing reference predictions.
The repulsive objective does not require a particular construc-
tion of evT. For teachers using classifier-free guidance, an unconditional or conditional prediction
can serve as the negative reference, corresponding to reference guidance scales w = 0 and w = 1,
respectively, while vT remains the guided prediction used for imitation. For embedded-guidance
models, we use a lower guidance input instead. For models with guidance already distilled into their
weights, we can instead obtain evT through an additional forward pass with a layer skipped. These
choices use the same pretrained model and adapt the negative reference to its inference procedure.
Training and inference.
We train the student and Pivot jointly on on-policy rollouts using Eq. (17).
The proxy path is used only to construct supervision at the sampled query points. The next rollout
state follows the student’s coarse update and is detached before the next transition. We average losses
over transitions, query points, and batch samples. Optimizer updates occur outside the rollout.
At inference, only the student follows the coarse updates on the same time grid. Neither teacher
predictions nor Pivot are evaluated, so the student architecture and number of function evaluations
are unchanged. Algorithms 2 and 3 summarize the training loss and student sampler, respectively.
Finding 5. The student can learn the coarse update without fitting every local velocity with a
single prediction. Pivot models within-step velocity variations through a zero-integral residual,
preserving the student’s transition endpoints.

Multiple Experts as Teachers
We next ask whether one student can combine complementary supervision from several sources. We

Table 7 shows that routed multi-teacher distillation improves all six reported metrics over self-
distillation, including HPSv2 and ImageReward, which are not direct specialization objectives, and
exceeds the original 28-NFE base teacher on every reported metric. Individual specialists remain

and broadly strong results elsewhere. Figure 23 compares generations from the base teacher and the
distilled students. At inference, only the student is evaluated.

7
Related Work
Few-step generation from diffusion and flow-based models has attracted broad research interest. Exist-
ing approaches largely fall into trajectory-level and distribution-level paradigms. Beyond these two
main lines, we survey methods that bridge or extend both paradigms, along with other complementary
acceleration techniques. Finally, we discuss the emerging connections between few-step distillation
and reinforcement learning for diffusion models.
Trajectory-Level Distillation.
Trajectory-level methods train a student to reproduce the teacher’s
sampling trajectory in fewer steps. The earliest approach, progressive distillation [12], repeatedly
halves the step count by training students to match teacher predictions over merged intervals. Guid-
ance distillation [45] later folded classifier-free guidance into the student, removing the need for dual
forward passes at inference. Both can degrade at very few steps, motivating subsequent objectives.
Consistency models [13] introduced a self-consistency constraint requiring that any point on the
same probability-flow ODE trajectory map to the same clean output, allowing both distillation from a
pretrained teacher and standalone training. Follow-up work improved stability via better schedules
and loss weighting [46], enabled efficient fine-tuning from pretrained diffusion models [47], moved the
formulation to latent spaces [48, 49, 50], generalized it to multi-step generation [51, 52, 53, 27, 54, 55],
and developed continuous-time variants that remove discretization dependence [56, 57, 37]. Target-
driven timestep selection [58] further improves consistency distillation by optimizing which timestep
pairs to train on. A concurrent report [59] offers practical guidance on consistency distillation.
Flow-map methods establish the mathematical foundations connecting flow maps to stochastic inter-
polants [60, 61], learning solution operators that transport states between arbitrary time pairs rather
than mapping only to the clean endpoint. Consistency trajectory models [62] first parameterized the
full ODE solution. MeanFlow [63] derived a self-consistency identity for the time-averaged velocity
that enables one-step generation. Extensions cover objective decompositions [64], JVP-free formula-
tions [65, 66], continuous-time unification with flow matching [67, 68, 69], data-free training [70, 71],
and recursive formulations that support any-step generation from a single model [72, 73]. Another
line of trajectory-level distillation follows rectified flow [5], where methods such as InstaFlow [74] and
PeRFlow [75] straighten ODE trajectories for fewer-step integration. OPSD-V [76] uses real long videos
as privileged temporal context to construct cleaner teacher caches, providing dense denoising-level
supervision along the student’s on-policy rollout for post-training few-step autoregressive video
generators.
π-Flow and PDD are particularly close to our regression-based formulation [14, 1]. π-Flow predicts
a network-free policy that supplies time-varying velocities for integration over substeps within one
network evaluation. PDD predicts multiple denoising updates in parallel, and its output heads can be
fused for inference. SMD instead places within-interval residual capacity in a training-only branch
whose basis functions integrate to zero. The deployed student keeps its original head and requires
neither Pivot nor substep integration.
Distribution-Level Distillation.
Distribution-level methods directly compare the student’s generated
distribution with the teacher or data distribution. Their training objectives can draw on discriminator
feedback, score estimates, or sample statistics.
Adversarial distillation draws on the generative adversarial network [77, 78, 79, 80] paradigm, using
learned discriminators to provide distribution-level feedback. ADD [81] combined score distillation
with adversarial losses for few-step generation, LADD [82] moved discrimination to latent space using
generative features from pretrained diffusion models, and progressive adversarial schemes [83] inter-
polate between trajectory and adversarial objectives across distillation stages. Further developments
36


Microsoft Research
include GAN-diffusion hybrids [84, 85], post-training adversarial fine-tuning for video [86, 87, 88],
adversarial distillation for 3D generation [89], intermediate-state adversarial matching [90], and
controllability-preserving distillation [91, 92].
Score-based distribution matching emerged as an alternative that avoids explicit discriminator training.
Score distillation sampling [93] first used a pretrained diffusion model’s score to optimize parametric
representations for text-to-3D generation. Variational score distillation [94] refined this by intro-
ducing a learned approximation to the variational distribution’s score, reducing over-smoothing.
Diff-Instruct [95] generalized this mathematical framework to training arbitrary generators from
pretrained diffusion models, deriving the integral KL objective and training an auxiliary score net-
work on student samples. Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD) [11] scaled this approach to
text-to-image synthesis, and DMD2 [15] improved optimization stability and multi-step extensions.
SwiftBrush [96, 97] demonstrated that pure variational score distillation, without adversarial training,
suffices for one-step synthesis that surpasses the teacher.
Extensions include decoupling CFG internalization from distribution matching [98], separating signals
across noise levels [99, 100], hierarchical structure-to-detail decomposition [101], removing the auxiliary
network via flow-map surrogates [102], scaling to flow architectures [16, 18], self-adversarial flow
formulations [29], video adaptation through transition and score-gradient matching [25, 103], adaptive
matching [104], diversity-preserving objectives [105], and teacher-feature drifting [106].
Alternatives to KL include Fisher divergence via score identity distillation [107, 108], implicit match-
ing [109], f-divergence weighting [110], proper scoring rules [111], EM distillation [112], and moment
matching approaches [113, 114]. A recent line of work applies DMD-style streaming distillation to
convert bidirectional video diffusion models into fast autoregressive generators [17, 44, 115, 40, 116].
Score-regularized continuous-time consistency [37] combines consistency training with a lightweight
DMD-style correction. Trajectory distribution matching [117] evaluates divergences along interme-
diate states. Video-specific designs balance coverage and sharpness [118, 38, 119, 120]. Scale-wise
distillation [121] decomposes generation by spatial frequency.
Other Distillation and Acceleration Techniques.
Other approaches connect few-step generation
with step-size conditioning, adaptive trajectories, and self-training. Shortcut models [122] learn step-
size-conditioned predictions that interpolate between local velocity estimation and global one-step
generation. Instance-aware adaptive trajectories [123] learn per-sample flow paths, while ArcFlow [124]
treats policy-based generation as an imitation learning problem. Other work studies training one-step
diffusion models without teacher distillation [125] and accelerating diffusion models with a single
sample [126].
Complementary acceleration techniques further reduce inference cost. Sampling methods and sched-
ules improve efficiency through fast scheduling [127], analytic variance estimation [128], pseudo-
numerical methods [129], exponential integrators [130], unified predictor-corrector frameworks [131],
and optimized timestep placement [132, 133]. Caching exploits temporal redundancy. DeepCache [134]
skips redundant U-Net computations, T-GATE [135] decomposes and caches attention outputs at
scheduled timesteps, and token-level caching [136], spectral-evolution policies [137], and sensitivity-
based strategies [138] target transformer architectures. For video, pruning with adaptive routing [139]
and spectral progressive generation [140] exploit inter-frame redundancy. FreeU [141] re-weights
skip connections without retraining, while FreeInit [142] refines initial noise via low-frequency itera-
tion. Architectural acceleration through sparse attention [143] and sliding-tile attention [144] exploits
spatiotemporal locality.
Connections to Reinforcement Learning.
Preference alignment and reinforcement learning have
become important components in the training of modern large generative models. Recently, many
37


Microsoft Research
works have applied RL methods such as GRPO [145] and DPO [146] to diffusion models through
the reverse process [147, 148, 149, 150] and the forward process [151, 152, 153]. Human preference
models [154, 155, 156, 157, 158] provide the reward signals in these settings.
A growing body of work combines few-step distillation with diffusion RL. Unified frameworks aug-
ment distribution matching with non-differentiable rewards [159, 160, 161, 162], and the distribution
matching objective itself can be reconceptualized as a reward signal for distilled generators [163].
8
Conclusion
Strong few-step diffusion distillation need not rely on auxiliary score models, adversarial training,
or specialized inference architectures. SMD builds on simple on-policy velocity regression with a
training-only Pivot proxy for local velocity variation and repulsion from weaker teacher predictions.
Across diverse generative tasks and models with up to 33B parameters, SMD delivers strong few-step
performance while leaving student inference unchanged. At 4-NFE, it outperforms strong few-step
baselines and surpasses multi-step teachers on several benchmarks. Our experiments further extend
SMD to cross-model, multi-teacher, and causal-video distillation, demonstrating the applicability of
on-policy regression beyond self-distillation.

Image Generation

We select checkpoints by combined GenEval, DPG-Bench, and OneIG-Bench-EN performance. We
report the mean of the five OneIG-Bench-EN dimensions as Overall. All released baselines are
evaluated using the official benchmark procedures. PDD results are quoted from its paper.

Video Generation
SMD distillation of Wan2.1 uses the 248K-entry preprocessed dataset released by rCM [37]. Students

DMD2∗baseline.

fake-score/discriminator updates at a 1:4 ratio. Both model scales use teacher CFG 6 and learning rate
10−4 for the student and fake-score model. Both baselines are evaluated after 500 iterations.
Evaluation follows the Self-Forcing protocol with five videos per prompt, 832 × 480 resolution, 81
frames, and 16 FPS. We generate with expanded prompts and score their short versions. We evaluate
released rCM and AnyFlow checkpoints and quote PDD results from its paper.

H3 Evaluation
Both inference budgets use the same evaluation set, with prompts, seeds, resolution, and duration
matched between methods. GPT-5.6 Sol evaluates 24 chronological frames twice with reversed
candidate order. Scores are averaged across orders and prompts. We report four visual dimensions
on a 1–5 scale. Prompt alignment measures visually verifiable fulfillment of the requested content
and camera behavior. Structural coherence measures the credibility and stability of anatomy, objects,
geometry, materials, and subject identity. Temporal coherence measures continuity, object permanence,
and physically plausible motion and interactions. Visual naturalness measures the absence of rendering
artifacts and implausible color or lighting. Audio clarity is the PAM score multiplied by 5, while audio
quality is Audiobox Aesthetics Production Quality divided by 2. Both are therefore reported on a 0–5

C.2
Multiple Experts as Teachers
We expand four Flow-GRPO [147] training splits into four 30K-prompt categories and combine them
into a 120K-entry pool. Self-distillation uses the base teacher for the full pool, while each specialist uses
one fixed reward teacher. In multi-teacher training, each batch contains prompts from one category
and uses its corresponding teacher. The four categories are sampled with equal probability.

This is an equivalence-first visual-quality assessment. Do not judge audio.
Score visually observable prompt fulfillment only under `prompt_alignment`;
do not let prompt completeness, exact camera wording, framing compliance,
shot count, or missing requested events affect the other three dimensions.
Ignore prompt requirements that can only be verified from audio. Do not guess
model identity.
First assess each candidate independently on:
1. `prompt_alignment`: visual correctness and completeness of requested
subjects, attributes, actions, interactions, setting, event order, shot
structure, framing, and camera behavior. Score what is visibly verifiable in
the sampled frames. Do not use rendering polish or artifact severity as
alignment evidence.
2. `structural_coherence`: recognizable and credible anatomy, objects,
geometry, materials, and subject identity within sampled frames.
3. `temporal_coherence`: identity and shape stability, physically plausible
motion and interaction, object permanence, and broad continuity across the
sampled sequence.
4. `visual_naturalness`: absence of disruptive rendering artifacts,
oversharpening, halos, crunchy or plastic texture, artificial tone mapping,
and implausible color or lighting.
Use integer scores: 5 excellent, 4 good with minor/localized defects, 3
material but usable limitations, 2 major or pervasive failures, 1 failed.
Sharpness, detail, saturation, contrast, pseudo-HDR, cinematic grading, and
raw motion magnitude are never positive evidence. Mild softness, natural
motion blur, shallow depth of field, and restrained detail are neutral unless
they destroy recognizable structure.
Then decide whether a careful human viewer would perceive a MATERIAL visual
quality difference. `equivalent` is the default. Break equivalence only when
one candidate has a persistent quality advantage visible at multiple
separated times or a major defect that substantially impairs viewing.
Localized imperfections, different stylistic treatment, minor score
differences, and prompt-following differences do not break equivalence.
