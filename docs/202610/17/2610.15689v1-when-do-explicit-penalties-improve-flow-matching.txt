# When do explicit penalties improve flow matching? 

Lea Kunkel, Alberto González-Sanz and Sophie Langer Oktober 2026 

Abstract 

Flow matching can learn structure directly from data, raising the question of when additional constraints improve generation. We study penalties that encode such constraints, with physics-informed flow matching as a motivating example. Satisfying a constraint more closely need not make the generated distribution more accurate. We therefore compare penalised and unpenalised generative modelling through their expected squared 

2-Wasserstein error. Our analysis covers both vector-valued and functional data in a Hilbert-space setting. We first derive geometric criteria that account for constraint violations and the distribution of samples after projection onto the constraint set. We then solve the statistical comparison explicitly for specific velocity-field classes and constraints via flow matching. In this model, penalisation improves accuracy exactly when the reduction in estimation variance outweighs the increase in squared bias. The resulting formula shows how this trade-off depends on sample size, the model class, the noise schedule, and the penalty weight. 

# 1 Introduction 

In scientific applications, obtaining data can require costly numerical simulations. In astrophysics, for example, the cost of high-fidelity cosmological simulations can limit the available training data (Zeghal et al., 2025). Generative models offer a promising approach by approximating the distribution of simulation outputs and provide a cheaper way to produce additional samples. Flow matching does this by learning a velocity field that transports a simple reference distribution towards the data distribution (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023b; Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden, 2023). It has shown strong performance in tasks ranging from high-resolution image synthesis (Esser et al., 2024) to speech generation (Chen et al., 2024). With limited training data, however, the learned distribution may still differ substantially from the target. Additional knowledge about the data, such as physical laws, algebraic relations between variables, or a known underlying subspace, can be incorporated through penalties on constraint violations. In physics-informed flow matching , physical residuals or conservation errors enter the training objective (Baldan et al., 2026; Debeire et al., 2026). Numerical experiments with related physics-informed diffusion models also suggest that such penalties can reduce over-fitting (Bastek et al., 2025). The statistical benefit of such penalties is not guaranteed. In the edge case of a hard constraint, the admissible samples are restricted, but the constraint generally does not determine how probability is distributed among them. Satisfying a constraint more closely can therefore come at the cost of accurately reproducing the distribution within the constraint set. For soft constraints, where violations are weighted against improvements elsewhere, a similar but more subtle setting occurs. Intrinsic adaption mechanisms compete with the external influences of the penalty. This raises the question we aim to answer: when does adding a constraint penalty improve the distribution estimation ability of flow matching?

Our contributions. Building on functional flow matching (Kerrigan et al. (2024); Zhang and Scott (2026)), we analyse when constraint penalties improve generation in separable Hilbert spaces. This setting accommodates both vector-valued and functional observations. We develop the comparison from two complementary perspectives. 1. Geometric criteria for improved generation. We separate distributional error into the distance of generated samples from the constraint set and the discrepancy between their projected distribution and the target. For affine constraint sets, this decomposition is exact (Proposition 3.3). It shows that moving samples closer to the constraint set improves generation only when this gain outweighs any deterioration in the projected distribution (Corollary 3.6). For curved sets, we control the additional remainder under assumptions on curvature and proximity to the set (Assumption 3.4 and Theorem 3.5). These criteria apply independently of how the distributions were learned (Corollary 3.6). 2. Exact statistical comparison in an affine model. For affine residuals and a restricted affine velocity-field class, we derive the penalised empirical minimiser and its exact change in expected 2-Wasserstein risk. The generated distribution has a fixed shape and a learned location. Within this model, penalisation improves accuracy precisely when the reduction in estimation variance exceeds the increase in squared bias (Theorem 4.3). The comparison makes the dependence on sample size, the hypothesis class, the noise schedule, and the penalty weight explicit. In particular, for a fixed penalty and noise schedule, any strictly positive increase in squared bias eventually outweighs the variance benefit as the sample size increases. Corollary 4.7 extends the analysis to arbitrary residuals. The affine model connects the two perspectives: the geometric and statistical comparisons yield the same change in distributional error (Proposition 4.4). 3. Extension to fixed feature models. As a first step towards network based velocity field estimators, we extent our analysis to fixed feature models. Theorem 5.4 shows that penalisation always improves distribution estimation for sufficiently small λ > 0.

Related literature. Several recent works establish statistical guarantees or closely related results for unpenalised flow matching, corresponding to λ = 0 in (1). Gao et al. (2024) establish a suboptimal convergence rate of the distributional estimator with respect to the 2-Wasserstein distance. Benton et al. (2024) provide an analysis of flow matching excluding the approximation error of the velocity field. Gong et al. (2025) investigate approximation-theoretic properties of ReLU neural networks for representing vector fields associated with higher-order trajectories. Fukumizu et al. (2025); Zhou and Liu (2025) partly improve previous results, but their proofs 2exhibit difficulties with the Lipschitz constant of the intrinsic vector field, which grows with the sample size and directly affects the resulting convergence rate. Stéphanovitch (2026); Kunkel (2026) investigate this specific pitfall in flow matching analysis. Kunkel and Trabs (2026) obtain minimax convergence rates in an over-parametrised setting. Mena et al. (2025) study further properties, including asymptotic behaviour and the connection to optimal transport maps. For intrinsically low-dimensional target distributions, Chakraborty et al. (2026) obtain high-probability bounds governed by the Wasserstein dimension, using stochastic sampling after flow-matching training. Together with Kumar et al. (2026), these results illustrate how flow-matching estimators can exploit low-dimensional data structure without an explicit constraint penalty. Known structure is incorporated explicitly in physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), which penalise violations of governing equations during training (Raissi et al., 2019). Mishra and Molinaro (2023) use PDE stability to bound solution error in terms of training residuals and quadrature error. Doumèche et al. (2025) establish consistency of PINNs under additional ridge regularisation and strong convergence for linear PDE systems with an additional Sobolev penalty. More directly related to our statistical question, Doumèche et al. (2024) study regression with a penalty based on a linear differential operator and show that physical information can improve convergence rates in specific settings. These results concern the estimation of a solution or regression function. In generative modelling, the additional task is to recover how probability is distributed among admissible samples. Alternative approaches to incorporate constraint penalties in flow matching include fine-tuning with weak-form PDE residuals (Tauberschmidt et al., 2026) or corrections during sampling (Utkarsh et al., 2025) (see Section 6 for more details). As mentioned above, our analysis concerns penalties added to the flow matching training objective.

# 4 Penalised flow matching under affine hypothesis classes 

We now investigate how the penalty in (13) changes the fitted velocity field and the distribution it generates. We restrict on affine velocity field classes for which both the training objective and the generated distribution can be computed explicitly. Within this class, the generated distributions share a fixed shape and differ only in their learned location. The comparison of their 2-Wasserstein errors therefore reduces to comparing the accuracy of the estimated location, leading to a mean-estimation problem. For affine penalties, we obtain a closed-form penalised estimator and an exact comparison of its expected 2-Wasserstein error with its unpenalised counterpart.

# 6 Discussion and limitations 

Our work provides a first theoretical underpinning of penalised flow matching and identifies conditions under which explicit penalties can lead to improved generation performance. Section 3 provides geometric conditions that decompose the overall 2-Wasserstein performance measure in a normal error, a tangential error and a remainder as given in Definition 3.2. For affine constraint sets, generation improves precisely when the reduction in the normal outweighs the increase in the tangential error. For curved sets, an additional remainder accounts on their interaction, and bounds on this remainder yield sufficient conditions for improvement. In the mean-estimation setting of Section 4, improved generation occurs when the reduction in estimation variance exceeds the increase in squared bias. For a fixed noise schedule, a fixed penalty that introduces a strictly positive bias eventually becomes harmful as the sample size increases. This highlights the potential benefit of explicit penalties in low-sample regimes, particularly relevant for scientific applications in which training data require costly numerical simulations (Peherstorfer et al., 2018). Section 5 establishes a different improvement mechanism for a nonlinear model with fixed features. Within the chosen architecture, a sufficiently small penalty reduces the remaining noise perpendicular to the constraint space while leaving the normal components separate and thus unchanged. This improvement holds for every fixed sample size under the stated assumptions. Together, these results show that the effect of a constraint penalty depends on its interaction with the velocity-field class, the noise schedule and the available observations. In particular, the smoothing induced by flow matching can itself leave samples outside the constraint set, such that an explicit penalty can lead to improved generation. On the other hand, the effect of the penalty are highly involved. Considered use requires a carefully assessed cost-benefit analysis. This work is only the first step in a profound understanding of explicit penalties within generative models and provides several interesting directions for further research: • A very natural extension is to analyse settings in which information is imperfect, e.g., observations are contaminated and thus fulfil the constraint only approximately or the residual map itself underlies some uncertainty. In these regimes penalties might either compensate for additional noise or worsen the generation performance by steering the data 13 towards a wrong constraint set. Another very interesting case are penalties that the newly generated samples should fulfil, but the available training data do not. This could be particularly interesting for, e.g., enforcing moral or legal restrictions on outputs. In view of the dangers of uncontrolled AI output, this question will become increasingly relevant. Here, the major challenge is how to formalise such a constraint. • Neural networks are known to learn data structures implicitly such that explicit penalties appear less beneficial in general. However, this only holds when the network architecture is carefully chosen and adapts to the data structure on which the networks are trained. Reversing the perspective implies that penalties might compensate for badly chosen architectures that are unable to capture the underlying data structure. Embedding structural information in the penalty might positively influences feature learning. • Constraint penalties may also improve statistical accuracy by reducing overfitting. Such benefits were already observed in the context of physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) (Doumèche et al., 2024) and have been empirically proven for physics-informed diffusion models (Bastek et al., 2025). However, explicit penalties such as physical laws alone need not prevent overfitting. The analysis of PINNs by Doumèche et al. (2025) shows that additional regularisation, independent of the specific constraint, can be necessary. In generative modelling, a model may likewise reproduce admissible training samples without accurately recovering the underlying distribution. • While our results do not provide any statistical rates yet, the bias-variance decomposition in Theorem 4.3 provides the starting point. Following the results known for vanilla diffusion (Oko et al., 2023) similar statistical rates could be derived under structural assumptions such as factorisable densities (Kwon et al., 2026) or manifold support (Azangulov et al., 2024). For penalised generative models we would expect faster convergence rates for small-sample regimes. • While we assume a general time distribution η, studying optimal choices of this distribution is particularly relevant for penalised settings, since the precision of the penalty evaluation during training heavily depends on the starting point for a fixed Euler scheme. Even in the unpenalised flow matching setting, it has been observed that the uniform distribution is not always the best choice (Esser et al., 2024). Thus, a rigorous analysis could lead to a significantly improved performance. • While our geometric comparison of Section 3 can assess outputs from any generative method (given the constraint assumption is fulfilled), Section 4 focuses on penalties applied to the training objective, inspired by physics-informed diffusion models or flow matching (Baldan et al., 2026; Bastek et al., 2025). Alternative approaches suggest to perform generation in transformed coordinates and map the generated samples back through a function whose outputs necessarily satisfy the constraint (Liu et al., 2023a). Constrained generative dynamics define the process directly on a constraint set or introduce reflections at the boundaries of an admissible domain (Chen and Lipman, 2024; Lou and Ermon, 2023), while generator guidance steers sampling updates using gradients of the constraint during training (Bansal et al., 2023). To the best of our knowledge, theoretical underpinning of any of these methods is still lacking, limiting a direct comparison of which method to choose.
