ABSTRACT
Diffusion and flow matching are now dominant frameworks for generative model-
ing in continuous domains, but they typically require multiple network evaluations
at inference. This has motivated extensive research on one-step generative mod-
eling primarily built on diffusion and flow, though existing approaches often rely
on complex training machinery. In this work, we introduce Priming Models, a
simple two-stage framework for training one-step generators. Motivated by the
difficulty of distributional training from scratch, Priming Models are first trained
on a reconstruction objective, e.g., pixel-space denoising at a fixed noise level, be-
fore undergoing feature-space distributional post-training. Our central scientific
finding is that this simple reconstruction stage is already enough to entirely alle-
viate the difficulty of distributional training. Empirically, across class-conditional
image generation, text-to-image generation, and world modeling, Priming Mod-
els significantly advance the Pareto frontier of training compute vs. generation
quality. On ImageNet at 256 × 256 resolution, we achieve a state-of-the-art FID
of 1.19 among one-step generators using approximately one-tenth the training
compute of prior methods with comparable FID. We open-source our codebase
at https://github.com/JaeyeonKim01/priming.

INTRODUCTION
A central goal of generative modeling is to achieve high sample quality with efficient training and
inference. Diffusion and flow-based models (Song et al., 2020; Ho et al., 2020; Lipman et al.,
2022; Albergo et al., 2025) have demonstrated remarkable generative capabilities and now serve as
backbones for several domains (Ho et al., 2020; Abramson et al., 2024; Chi et al., 2025), but they
rely on iterative sampling with multiple network evaluations.
This has motivated considerable effort toward one- and few-step generation (Salimans & Ho, 2022).
Much of this progress builds on diffusion and flow formulations, including consistency-based mod-
eling (Song et al., 2023; Lu & Song, 2025), flow-map learning (Boffi et al., 2024; Geng et al.,
2026a;b), and distributional distillation (Luo et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2024a;b). While promising,
these approaches require a considerable amount of extra machinery to work, like specialized stabi-
lization strategies, Jacobian–vector products, classifier-free guidance, a teacher to distill from, etc.
A complementary approach is to train a generator by directly matching its output distribution to the
data distribution. This naturally supports generation in a single network evaluation and has a long

history in GANs (Goodfellow et al., 2014) and maximum mean discrepancy-based generators (Li
et al., 2015; 2017). Translating these objectives into effective training, however, requires significant
engineering effort (Gulrajani et al., 2017; Mescheder et al., 2018).
This latter approach has seen a recent resurgence of interest, with Drifting Models (Deng et al.,
2026) and W-Flow (Han et al., 2026) demonstrating that direct distributional learning can produce
high-quality one-step generators at scale. Their training recipes nevertheless rely on longer training-
time schedules and subtle tricks like style-conditioning (Karras et al., 2019). Thus, the simplicity of
one-step inference still comes at the cost of substantial complexity in getting these models to train.
In this work, we therefore ask: Does training a one-step generator need to be this complicated?
We introduce Priming Models, a drastically more straightforward framework for one-step genera-
tive modeling with just two basic components:
[Stage 1] Reconstruction pretraining: We pretrain the network with a simple reconstruction
objective – in fact, even denoising at a fixed corruption level suffices.
[Stage 2] Distributional post-training: We use the pretrained network directly as a one-step
generator that maps Gaussian noise to samples, then refine it by matching generated and real
distributions in feature space, e.g., using an optimal transport loss.
Empirically, Priming Models achieve strong performance with substantially reduced training com-
pute across three visual domains:
• ImageNet: Priming Models achieve state-of-the-art FID (1.19)1and Fr´echet distances across
other feature spaces among one-step generators, outperforming Pixel MeanFlow (Lu et al.,
2026b), Drifting (Deng et al., 2026), and even multistep diffusion baseline JiT (Li & He,
2026), with approximately 10× less compute than Drifting (Figure 1, left).
• Text-to-Image: Priming Models substantially outperform one-step MeanFlow on GenEval
and DPG, while reducing training compute by a factor of 1.9.
• World models: Priming Models outperform the Drifting baseline (Lu et al., 2026a) with
approximately half the training compute, while also achieving performance comparable to the
(multi-step) flow matching baseline.
A key factor behind our strong empirical results is that reconstruction pretraining substantially
accelerates distributional post-training (Figure 1, right). Without the former, even when the dis-
tributional objective provides useful correction directions, the network’s Jacobian may hinder how
effectively the parameters update to realize these improvements. Through controlled experiments,
we demonstrate that reconstruction pretraining ameliorates this by aligning the Jacobian and shaping
how readily the model’s outputs can be refined.
Together, our findings suggest a surprisingly minimal design principle for generative modeling: use
reconstruction pretraining to prepare a generator for distributional post-training. Priming Models
realize this principle, enabling high-quality one-step generators to be efficiently trained from scratch.

PRIMING MODELS
We first present our motivation behind Priming Models. Distributional objectives, such as Sinkhorn
divergence or Wasserstein distance, offer a natural approach to one-step generative modeling by
directly matching generated and real distributions. The central challenge, however, lies in trans-
lating these objectives into effective training procedures, a difficulty that helped motivate inter-
est in alternatives such as diffusion models (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021). Indeed, early maximum
mean discrepancy (MMD)-based generators were demonstrated on relatively small datasets (Li
et al., 2015; 2017; Genevay et al., 2018), while GANs have achieved high-quality generation at
scale (Goodfellow et al., 2014; Arjovsky et al., 2017; Brock et al., 2018; Karras et al., 2019; Sauer
et al., 2022) but remain sensitive to optimization (Gulrajani et al., 2017; Mescheder et al., 2018).

leads us to ask:
Question: Can a suitable initialization make distributional post-training more effective?
Our intuition is that distributional training is challenging from scratch because at the start, the net-
work has no information about the structure of natural images. To that end, we seek to first learn a
related capability: recovering clean-image structure from corrupted inputs. Denoising reconstruc-
tion provides a natural way to learn this capability. Corrupted inputs retain information about the
underlying images to guide recovery, while their clean counterparts provide direct supervision.
Our hypothesis is that learning to reconstruct provides a useful initialization for subsequent distri-
butional optimization. This motivates reconstruction pretraining as a first stage toward learning the
full noise-to-image mapping.
2.1
PRIMING MODELS: TRAINING RECIPE
Building on this intuition, Priming Models train a one-step generator in two stages: reconstruction
pretraining and distributional post-training. Both stages optimize the same network fθ, conditioned
on an input c, such as a class label or text prompt. We denote the empirical training distribution by
pdata; here our notation suppresses the implicit dependence on c.
[Stage 1] Reconstruction pretraining. We first pretrain the network to reconstruct clean samples
from corrupted inputs. Our main instantiation uses a conventional denoising objective (Vincent
et al., 2008; Vincent, 2011), with the noise level s sampled from a prescribed distribution p(·). In
particular, we minimize
Lθ = Ex0,ϵ∥fθ(xs, c) −x0∥2,

p = δ0.8, fixing the noise level at s = 0.8 throughout. This choice follows the intuition above: a

This stage is quite flexible: Our pretraining stage also accommodates other forms, for instance,
masking each patch, as well as reconstruction in the representation space of a pretrained RAE (Singh
et al., 2026). We revisit these alternatives in Section 4.1.
[Stage 2] Distributional post-training. After pretraining, we use fθ directly as a one-step genera-
tor: Given Gaussian noise ϵ ∼N(0, I), the network yields ˆx0 = fθ(ϵ, c). We denote the resulting

then post-train this generator by matching generated and real distributions in feature space. Given a
frozen feature encoder ϕ and a differentiable distributional loss F, we optimize
Lθ = F(ϕ#qθ, ϕ#pdata),
(2)
where ϕ#(·) denotes the pushforward distribution via ϕ. The choice of F is simple but also flexible,
accommodating several established objectives: Sinkhorn divergence (Sinkhorn, 1964; Cuturi, 2013)
and maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) (Glaunes et al., 2004; Gretton et al., 2012). We implement
these objectives through the following surrogate
F(a, b) = D(a, b) −D(a, sg[a]),
where D is a functional on pairs of feature distributions and sg denotes the stop-gradient. The
first term encourages alignment between generated and real samples, while the second provides a
repulsive self-interaction among generated samples.
Our main choice of D is an optimal transport (OT) cost, yielding a surrogate for the Sinkhorn
divergence. Alternatively, a Laplacian kernel D(a, b) = Ex∼a,y∼b[−exp(−∥x −y∥)] gives us
a surrogate for squared MMD. We find that even the simpler mean pairwise distance, D(a, b) =
Ex∼a,y∼b[∥x −y∥], is effective. During training, we estimate these objectives using minibatches of
samples and backpropagate through ϕ ◦fθ to update θ. Appendix B provides the justification for
these surrogates and implementation details. Below we briefly review optimal transport.

Comparison with other prior work. Mainstream approaches to one-step generation build on dif-
fusion or flow-matching trajectories (Liu et al., 2023a; Song et al., 2023; Boffi et al., 2024; Geng
et al., 2026a; Salimans & Ho, 2022; Yin et al., 2024b). In contrast, Priming Models require neither
prescribed trajectories nor a pretrained diffusion or flow model.
Recent work (Feng et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026) uses distributional losses to improve already strong
one-step generators. In Priming Models, reconstruction pretraining produces poor initial one-step
samples (Figure 1, right), and distributional post-training enables high-quality generation.
As noted at the beginning of Section 2, Drifting (Deng et al., 2026) and W-Flow (Han et al., 2026)
train models from scratch using distributional losses. Priming is more efficient than these meth-
ods and does not rely on additional modeling components such as classifier-free guidance or style
embeddings; refer to Section 3.

EXPERIMENTS
We now demonstrate that Priming Models significantly advance the Pareto frontier of train-
ing efficiency vs. generation quality across three visual domains: class-conditional generation on
ImageNet (Section 3.1), text-to-image generation (Section 3.2), and world modeling (Section 3.3).

3.2
TEXT-TO-IMAGE GENERATION
We now evaluate Priming Models on text-to-image generation at 512×512 resolution to assess their
effectiveness beyond ImageNet-scale.
Setup. We build on MiniT2I (Wang et al., 2026a), an open-sourced codebase for pixel-space text-
to-image generation with publicly available training data. Our primary baseline is the MiniT2I
MeanFlow checkpoint. For our model, we adopt the same 258M-parameter MM-JiT-B/16 generator
backbone. Text captions are encoded by the 341M-parameter encoder of FLAN-T5-Large (Chung
et al., 2024), which remains frozen throughout training. Following MiniT2I, we scale the Gaussian
noise by a factor of 2, i.e., ϵ ∼N(0, 22I), throughout training and inference.
Pretraining. We first train with the reconstruction objective on CC12M (Changpinyo et al., 2021),
then continue reconstruction training on a curated mixture of 120k images from the codebase. Both
phases use a global batch size of 1024. We use two pretraining noise configurations: a fixed cor-
ruption level s = 0.8 and corruption levels sampled from a reweighted logit-normal distribution
p(s) ∝s−2 LogNorm(s; 0.8, 0.82).
Post-training. We then post-train the model using the OT objective in (3) with ϵ = 0.05. At
each update, we sample 64 distinct text captions. For each caption, we generate 32 samples online,
applying stop-gradient to 16 of them. We compute the objective independently for each caption
group using globally pooled features from SigLIP, ViT-MAE, and Inception-V3. For each feature
branch, we normalize features by their within-batch pairwise RMS distance. Since each caption is
paired with a single real image, the generated–real OT term reduces to the average squared feature
distance to that image. Post-training runs for 4,000 updates without EMA.
Multi-Step Extension. Inspired by Consistency Models (Song et al., 2023), we extend Priming
Model to support multi-step generation. Specifically, during post-training, we define a finite set of
noise levels S ⊂(0, 1] that includes s = 1 and train the network to predict a clean image fθ(xs, c)
from inputs at each noise level s ∈S. We apply the same OT objective with xs as the input, enabling
the model to generate clean samples from both pure noise and partially noisy inputs. The multi-step
post-training schedule is detailed in Appendix C.2.

Finally, we apply Priming Models to action-conditioned world models on two robot-manipulation
domains: RT-1 (Brohan et al., 2023) and Bridge-V2 (Walke et al., 2023). In particular, we train a
Priming Model to predict the next frame conditioned on recent observation and action histories.
Setup. We follow the data processing pipeline and the architecture choice of U-Net with action con-
ditioning from DriftWorld (Lu et al., 2026a). For both datasets, frames are resized to 256 × 256 and
then encoded into the SD3-VAE (Esser et al., 2024) latent space, in which our Priming Model op-
erates. Our primary baseline is DriftWorld and 32-step Flow Matching, trained with approximately
the same EFLOPs as Priming.
Training. We first perform reconstruction pretraining for 64 epochs using the reweighted logit-
normal distribution from Section 3.2, with a global batch size of 64 and an EMA decay of 0.9999.
The distributional post-training begins from these weights. At each update, we process 32 conditions
with 16 live and 16 stop-gradient generated samples, together with one real next frame per condition.
We use the OT objective (3) with ϵ = 0.05, and pairwise-RMS feature scaling. Post-training runs
for 4,000 updates.
Evaluation. We evaluate EMA checkpoints on the held-out validation sets, with a single network
evaluation per predicted frame. SSIM, PSNR, and LPIPS metrics are used to compare generated
frames to the corresponding ground-truth frames; FID and FVD (Unterthiner et al., 2018) measure
distributional agreement. Both datasets are evaluated in 8-frame autoregressive chunks, re-anchoring
on the real trajectory between chunks. FVD uses the first 16 predicted frames of each trajectory. We
additionally report the time required to generate a frame and EFLOPs, including both pretraining
and post-training. Complete conditioning, training, and rollout details are provided in Appendix C.3.
Results. Table 3 reports generation quality and training compute on RT-1 and Bridge-V2. Despite
generating each sample in a single step, Priming Models outperform both the 32-step flow-matching
baseline and DriftWorld on every reported quality metric, except FVD on RT-1. Compared with
DriftWorld, they use 59.7% less training compute on RT-1 (12.37 versus 30.71 EFLOPs) and 74.4%
less on Bridge-V2 (9.50 versus 37.05 EFLOPs). Notably, we do not rely on additional techniques

Pretraining with masking. We substitute the denoising corruption with patch-wise masking: ran-
domly selecting 80% of the image patches and replacing them with independently sampled Gaussian
noise, leaving the remaining patches uncorrupted. We train Priming-B to reconstruct x0 using the
same reconstruction and perceptual losses as in the default pretraining. After 160 epochs of pre-
training followed by 12,500 steps of distributional post-training, the model achieves an FID of 1.87
with an EMA decay of 0.999 (Table 4; Masking).
Pretraining as distillation from RAE. We next demonstrate that representation distillation provides
an alternative initialization for distributional post-training. Using the frozen encoder of a pretrained
RAEv2 (Singh et al., 2026) as a teacher, we train a class-conditioned student encoder with 300M
parameters to recover clean-image representations from corrupted inputs. Specifically, we minimize
E|fθ(xs, c)−sg[Eteacher(x0)]|2
2, matching the student’s output to the teacher’s representation of the
corresponding clean image. We then compose the trained student encoder with the pretrained, frozen
RAEv2 decoder to produce images in pixel space. After 12,500 steps of distributional post-training,
the model achieves an FID of 2.34 with an EMA decay of 0.999 (Table 4; RAE).
Ablation study: Perceptual loss. We compare Priming-B-160 (Section 3.1) with the same 160-
epoch pretraining without perceptual loss. After 25,000 matched OT updates, removing percep-
tual loss worsens terminal EMA FID from 1.38 to 2.07 and FDr5 from 11.23 to 16.13 (Table 4;
w/o perc.). We hypothesize that supervision in external feature spaces helps the model capture use-
ful structure of the data manifold.
Ablation study: Noise level. We next examine the effect of the noise level used during pretraining.
Using the same Priming-B pipeline, we pretrain each model for 80 epochs at a fixed noise level,
comparing our default s = 0.8 with s ∈{1.0, 0.9, 0.7, 0.5}. After 12,500 OT post-training updates,
s = 0.8 yields the lowest terminal EMA FID (1.65), followed by s = 0.7 (1.76), s = 0.9 (1.97),
s = 0.5 (2.46), and s = 1.0 (65.08). These results support our intuition that the effective noise level
should balance retaining the clean image’s information with keeping the noise level close to pure
Gaussian noise.
4.2
EXAMINING POST-TRAINING DESIGN CHOICES
Other choices of D.
We recall our post-training objective F(a, b) = D(a, b) −D(a, sg[a]).
Starting from the same Priming-B checkpoint after 160 epochs of pretraining, we evaluate
two alternatives to OT in the full ResNet-MAE and ConvNeXt feature spaces: the pairwise
distance D(a, b) = Ex∼a,y∼b[∥x −y∥2] and negative Laplacian-kernel similarity D(a, b) =
Ex∼a,y∼b[−exp(−∥x −y∥2)]. At 25,000 updates, their terminal EMA FIDs are 1.95 and 1.85,
respectively, compared with 1.38 for OT (Table 1). The FDr5 are 11.55 and 11.22, compared with
11.23 for OT (Table 4; Energy/Laplacian). These results show that Priming Models work with
multiple distributional losses, with OT yielding the best FID among the choices tested.
Other choices of feature space. We also replace the full ResNet-MAE and ConvNeXt feature
spaces with simpler, lightweight spaces, SigLIP, Inception-V3, and ViT-MAE, keeping the same
9

perceptual Priming-B-160 initialization and 25,000 OT post-training setup. The terminal EMA
checkpoint reaches FID 1.65 and FDr5 6.13, versus 1.38 and 11.23 for the full-feature OT reference
(Table 1, and Table 4; SigLIP/MAE/Inception). Inception, SigLIP, and ViT-MAE are components
of FDr5, so the improvement in FDr5 is not an independent held-out-feature result.
FD-loss. Fr´echet distance (Dowson & Landau, 1982) is widely used to evaluate generative models,
but directly optimizing it was believed to be challenging, as reliable estimation typically requires
tens of thousands of samples. FD-loss (Yang et al., 2026) addresses this challenge by accumulating
feature statistics across training steps through a feature queue or exponential moving averages, while
backpropagating only through the current batch, and has demonstrated consistent improvements in
pretrained one-step generators. We show that FD-loss also fits within the Priming Model frame-
work as an alternative distributional post-training objective. Applied to the pretrained Priming-B
checkpoint, it yields efficient post-training; we provide the setup and results in Appendix E.
5
CONCLUSION
In this work, we introduce Priming Models, a simple two-stage framework for training one-step
generative models end-to-end. Reconstruction pretraining prepares the generator for efficient distri-
butional post-training, enabling strong empirical performance across multiple visual domains.
That said, our work leaves several open questions. Priming Models rely on pretrained feature en-
coders. Although we demonstrate our method with multiple sets of feature encoders, this depen-
dence raises the question of whether it can be eliminated. The relationship between reconstruction
pretraining and Jacobian alignment also warrants deeper investigation. Finally, closing the remain-
ing performance gap with multi-step diffusion and flow models remains an important challenge.
Outlook. More broadly, our results suggest that we may be overthinking the problem: the special-
ized formulations and elaborate training machinery used in prior work are not the only path to train
one-step generators. We hope our work opens the door to simpler and more scalable approaches to
one-step generative modeling.

A
RELATED WORK
In this section, we review prior work on one- and few-step generative modeling, organized by their
methodological category.
Category 1: Trajectory straightening and flow-map learning. These lines of work obtain few-
step generative models by learning from a diffusion or probability-flow trajectory. Progressive dis-
tillation repeatedly compresses a teacher sampler into fewer steps (Salimans & Ho, 2022). Consis-
tency Models instead enforce agreement among predictions made at different points along the same
trajectory (Song et al., 2023), while subsequent work improves their stability and scaling or extends
consistency to trajectory segments (Geng et al., 2025; Song & Dhariwal, 2024; Lu & Song, 2025;
Kim et al., 2024). Rectified Flow and reflow (Liu et al., 2023a;b) seek straighter transport paths that
are easier to integrate in few steps. These straightening methods modify the coupling and resulting
trajectory so that coarse numerical integration can accurately reach its endpoint.
A related but distinct family learns the finite-time transition across an interval of a flow-matching
trajectory, such as a flow map or average velocity. This includes Flow Map Matching, Shortcut
Models, MeanFlow, Improved MeanFlow, and Inductive Moment Matching (Boffi et al., 2024; Frans
et al., 2025; Geng et al., 2026a;b; Zhou et al., 2025). These methods differ in their targets and
training estimators, but generally supervise a particular time-dependent transport or consistency
relation.
Priming: Priming Models do not attempt to recover a prescribed trajectory or flow map: reconstruc-
tion pretraining provides an initialization for the network, which we then directly use as a one-step
generator.
Category 2: Teacher-guided distillation. Another line of work distills a pretrained diffusion
teacher into a one-step student, with much of the focus on designing effective distillation objectives.
Diff-Instruct (Luo et al., 2023) uses score-based distributional objectives derived from a diffusion
teacher, while DMD and DMD2 (Yin et al., 2024b;a) optimize the student using the difference be-
tween teacher and student score estimates. Score Identity Distillation (Zhou et al., 2024) provides a
related score-based formulation. Adversarial Diffusion Distillation combines score distillation with
an adversarial objective to improve fidelity in the low-step regime (Sauer et al., 2024).
Priming: These approaches inherit the cost and machinery required to obtain and query that teacher.
In contrast, Priming Models are trained from scratch and therefore do not require a pretrained
teacher.
Category 3:Distributional loss (from scratch). Directly matching a generator distribution to the
data distribution has a long history. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) learn a one-pass
generator through an adversarially trained discriminator (Goodfellow et al., 2014), with Wasserstein
objectives and regularization improving the geometry and stability of training (Arjovsky et al., 2017;
Gulrajani et al., 2017; Mescheder et al., 2018). Generative Moment Matching Networks use maxi-
mum mean discrepancy (MMD) in place of adversarial discrimination, while MMD-GANs further
learn the MMD kernel adversarially (Li et al., 2015; 2017). Genevay et al. (2018) trains implicit
generative models directly using a Sinkhorn divergence. These works, however, are limited in em-
pirical scope and, more importantly, differ from Priming Models in that we apply the distributional
objective after a reconstruction pretraining stage.
Recent works revisit direct one-step learning at a more practical scale. YOSO (Luo et al., 2024)
stabilizes adversarial one-step training through a self-cooperative diffusion GAN, while Adversarial
Flow (Lin et al., 2025) trains with an adversarial objective but constrains the generator to learn the
deterministic transport map associated with flow matching.
Priming: Priming Models share the goal of direct distribution matching, but do not optimize it
from random initialization: reconstruction pretraining first teaches the generator to correct corrupted
inputs toward clean images, after which a distributional objective refines it into a one-step generator.
Category 3-1: Drifting Models. Drifting Models (Deng et al., 2026) construct an attraction-
repulsion field using a normalized Laplacian kernel and train the generator to match stop-gradient
targets obtained by displacing its generated features along this field. W-Flow (Han et al., 2026)
derives a training-time velocity field from the Wasserstein gradient flow of the Sinkhorn divergence,
using barycentric projections computed from entropic optimal transport plans. Van Assel et al.
16


Preprint
(2026) additionally examine how the choice of frozen feature space affects transport stability in
distribution matching.
Priming: Priming Models introduce reconstruction pretraining before distributional optimization,
and this stage is key to our strong compute vs. quality trade-off. Our implementation also seeks to
dispense with the additional engineering machinery used in these works. Both reported ImageNet
architectures include learned random style embeddings, and both frameworks support classifier-
free guidance; Drifting additionally normalizes its constructed drift field. Priming does not use
these additional mechanisms and instead optimizes an OT-based distributional surrogate. All four
models reported in Section 3 share exactly the same post-training configuration, further illustrating
the simplicity of our approach.
This suggests that reconstruction pretraining can make distributional optimization more efficient
while supporting a simpler training recipe. We hypothesize that this initialization reduces the need
for additional training mechanisms, although our experiments do not isolate their individual contri-
butions.
Category 4: Distributional loss (fine-tuning). Distributional objectives have also been used to
adapt pretrained generative models. MMD-DDM (Aiello et al., 2023) fine-tunes pretrained diffusion
models with MMD to improve quality under a restricted sampling-step budget. DiffRatio (Chen
et al., 2025) estimates the density ratio between noisy real and generated distributions to train a
one-step model without a teacher score. FD-loss (Yang et al., 2026) makes feature-space Fr´echet
distance practical as a training objective by maintaining population-level feature statistics, and ap-
plies it to pretrained one- and multi-step generators. More recently, iRDM (Feng et al., 2026) scales
MMD-based post-training across multiple pretrained representation spaces.
Most closely related to our work, D2O (Zheng & Yang, 2025) shows that adversarial fine-tuning
alone can convert a pretrained diffusion model into a one-step generator, while Yang et al. (2026,
Section 4.3) show that FD-loss can convert JiT, a multi-step diffusion model, into a competitive
one-step generator.
Priming: Priming Models build on the perspective that pretraining can prepare a network for sub-
sequent distributional optimization. We investigate how simple this pretraining task can be, show-
ing that reconstruction—even denoising at a single fixed noise level without noise-level condition-
ing—can provide an effective initialization for our distributional training, despite poor initial one-
step samples (Figure 1, right). We further examine alternative pretraining tasks (Section 4.1) within
the same framework.
Finally, viewing reconstruction pretraining and distributional post-training as a complete training
procedure also enables comparisons with prior methods based on total training compute across both
stages, measured in EFLOPs.

Unlike the objectives for Sinkhorn divergence or MMD, FFD is minimized directly: there is no gen-
erated self-interaction term or independent generated reference batch. Gradients propagate through
the generated moments (µq, Σq) to qθ, whereas the real moments (µp, Σp) are fixed.
In practice, FD-loss replaces the population moments with accumulated estimates so that the objec-
tive can be optimized without placing the full feature population in a single minibatch (Yang et al.,
2026). FD-loss performs marginal matching instead of conditional distribution matching.
E.2
FD-LOSS IMAGENET EXPERIMENTS
Setup. We evaluate FD-loss as an alternative distributional post-training objective for the pixel-
space Priming Models from Section 3.1. Priming-B has 115.1M parameters and is used for the pre-
training ablations, while Priming-L has 405.1M parameters and is used for the pretraining-duration
study. Both models use 16 × 16 patches and generate ImageNet-1K images at 256 × 256 resolution
in one network evaluation. These experiments are separate from the OT-post-trained results in the
main text.
Reconstruction pretraining uses Muon with learning rate 10−3 and global batch size 1,024. Unless
stated otherwise, we use the fixed corruption level s = 0.8 and the pixel, LPIPS, and ConvNeXt
reconstruction objective described in Section 3.1. FD post-training lasts 20 epochs and uses AdamW
with learning rate 10−5, cosine decay, five epochs of linear warmup, and global batch size 256. We
follow the multi-representation FD-SIM configuration of Yang et al. (2026): the training objective
combines equally weighted FD terms from frozen SigLIP, Inception-V3, and MAE encoders. Real
feature moments are precomputed, while generated moments are accumulated across updates with
decay 0.999.
We evaluate the final online weights; the accumulated feature statistics are used only during FD post-
training. Each result uses 50,000 generated images with uniformly sampled labels. We report FID,
precision, recall, and FDr6 following the FD-loss evaluation protocol. Here, FDr6 is the validation-
normalized Fr´echet distance averaged across six representations and differs from FDr5 used in the
main experiments.
Priming-B pretraining ablations. We first pretrain Priming-B for 80 epochs and apply the same
20-epoch FD-loss stage to every configuration. Table 9 compares pixel reconstruction alone with
the addition of LPIPS and ConvNeXt perceptual supervision under fixed and logit-normal corruption
schedules. Perceptual supervision lowers FDr6 from 7.49 to 4.95 for fixed s = 0.8 and from 8.30
to 5.57 for the reweighted logit-normal distribution. Fixing s = 0.8 also outperforms the tested
logit-normal schedule under either reconstruction objective.
Keeping perceptual reconstruction fixed, we then sweep s ∈{0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0}. Among these
values, s = 0.8 gives the lowest FDr6. At s = 1, the corrupted input is independent of its paired
clean image, and performance deteriorates sharply. Thus, simply matching the eventual Gaussian
generator input is insufficient; retaining some clean-image information during reconstruction pro-
vides a substantially better initialization.
