Abstract
We study Text-and-Image-to-Image (TI2I) flow matching methods for controllable image
editing. In a broader generative modeling context, Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) is estab-
lished as a key component responsible for high quality outputs. However, we argue that its
role in TI2I modeling is poorly understood. Specifically, we make the surprising observation
that the CFG mechanism is implicitly harmful for editing in the noisy phase of flow model-
ing, hindering instruction following and controllability. We propose a simple correction in
the early flow steps, enabling a richer alignment-preservation tradeoff at no extra cost. The
effectiveness of our approach is demonstrated on both pixel-space and latent TI2I flows.

Introduction
Text-and-Image-to-Image (TI2I) editing is the task of modifying a source image according to a text prompt
while retaining the source wherever no change is intended. The source image provides the concrete visual
instance, including scene, object identity, composition, appearance and incidental details.
The prompt
instead serves as an instruction specifying how that instance should change. Crucially, a successful edit must
apply the requested changes without reopening the rest of the image for synthesis.

TI2I editing methods studied in the literature typically adapt pretrained Text-to-Image (T2I) models. These
methods are often training-free, so their design space concentrates on inference-time mechanisms (Kulikov
et al., 2025; Yang & Ye, 2026). This adaptation paradigm has produced a broad and useful set of techniques
but it also inherits limitations from models trained primarily for T2I generation. In parallel, a newer line
of systems trains text-and-image-conditioned flows directly for editing (Wu et al., 2025; Black Forest Labs,
2025a). These models are increasingly important yet their properties remain comparatively underexplored.
In this paper, we study this setting of bespoke TI2I flow matching, rather than editing through adaptation
of a T2I generator.
Specifically, we focus on the nuance of Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG), which is a
central mechanism of modern generative flows (Ho & Salimans, 2021). In T2I generation, increasing the
CFG weight, ω, is commonly understood as improving text alignment at the cost of sample diversity and, at
large values, visual naturalness (Zheng & Lan, 2024; Karras et al., 2024). In editing, however, the relevant
tradeoff is alignment versus preservation with respect to the source. In that case, we find that CFG produces
a poorly shaped guidance tradeoff. That is, small ω weakens instruction following but may not reliably reduce
distortion, while large ω is often essential for alignment but tends to amplify unnecessary changes.
We identify the source of this inefficiency and attribute it to the well-established and widely adopted recipe
of Ho & Salimans (2021).
As demonstrated in Figure 1, we propose a simple inference-time correction
that largely mitigates the issue.
Our intervention requires no additional evaluations, leaves the trained
model unchanged and recovers a richer set of operating points as ω varies. We show this in two settings.
For a controlled analysis, we use a PixelGen (Ma et al., 2026b) finetune for pixel-space editing. We then
demonstrate our approach in more general editing, benchmarking via Qwen-Image-Edit (Wu et al., 2025)
and FLUX.2 (Black Forest Labs, 2025a), representative off-the-shelf latent flows tailored for the task.

2.2
Text-and-Image-to-Image Modeling
TI2I models generate edit candidates conditioned jointly on a source image, cimg, and a text prompt or
instruction ctxt. That is, the target distribution here is of the form p(· | cimg, ctxt). Unlike T2I generation,
which has weaker conditioning, sampling from p(· | ctxt), TI2I editing requires balancing two competing
guiding signals and goals. Namely, adherence to the requested edit and preservation of source content.
Most editing methods in the literature do not train TI2I editing models directly.
Instead, they adapt
pretrained T2I models at inference time, effectively exploiting their strong generative priors while avoiding
additional optimization costs (Meng et al., 2022; Rout et al., 2025; Kulikov et al., 2025; Yang & Ye, 2026).
2


Alternatively, it is also possible to train a generator directly for image editing. Early diffusion-based examples
include Brooks et al. (2023), which learns to follow natural-language editing instructions from paired editing
data. More recently, large-scale multimodal flow models have been trained natively with both image and text
conditioning, enabling direct modeling of the TI2I distribution (Wu et al., 2025; Black Forest Labs, 2025a).
Notably, in these systems, the source image enters the model as a first-class conditioning signal. In particular,
for flow matching models, conditioning is typically incorporated into the velocity field, vθ(xt, t, cimg, ctxt), so
that the sampling trajectory is jointly determined by the current state, source image and textual instruction.
2.3

tional models. Intuitively, CFG aims to sample from tilted distributions, qt,ω(· | c) ∝q1−ω

Therefore, given the equivalence of scores with velocities, the implementation requires training both an

Despite the theoretical elegance of CFG, subtle technical details make it a non-trivial mechanism, with more
nuanced dynamics and interpretation than the original motivation suggests (Zheng & Lan, 2024; Karras
et al., 2024). Moreover, its design in the presence of multimodal conditioning remains relatively unexplored.
3
Methodology
Before proceeding with analysis, we summarize our experimental setup. For completeness, we consider both
pixel-space and latent-space TI2I models. In particular, pixel-space modeling enables a controlled and more
interpretable setup. To this end, we produce a TI2I finetune of PixelGen (Ma et al., 2026b) at 512 × 512
resolution.
For latent-space modeling, we experiment with Qwen-Image-Edit (Wu et al., 2025) and the
FLUX.2 family (Black Forest Labs, 2025a) at 256 × 256, 512 × 512 and 1024 × 1024 image resolutions.
Throughout the paper, we use synthetic datasets, created by existing foundation models.
After quality
control, each contains approximately 200 entries. Specifically, we first generate text triplets of source image
descriptions, edit instructions and post-edit descriptions via Claude Opus 4.7. Source descriptions are then
used to generate source images via FLUX.2. Additionally, for local editing with PixelGen, we constrain the
text triplets to describe scenarios where changes are expected to be strictly confined within specific regions in
the images. In that case, we use the Segment Anything Model (SAM) of Carion et al. (2026) to automatically
obtain corresponding segmentation masks. For latent-space editing with Qwen and FLUX.2, which we found
to be significantly more capable, we allow general and challenging triplets to effectively test limitations.
Further details on infrastructure, data, model configurations and hyperparameters are given in Appendix A.
3.1
Understanding Guidance & Its Design Space in TI2I Flows
Our goal is understanding when editing failures emerge and how to interpret them, with the expectation
that this will lead to actionable insights. Concretely, here we investigate TI2I flow modeling under CFG.

We conclude this section by emphasizing the key findings of the above experiments.
First, our locality
analysis suggests that edit-relevant signal is concentrated in the noisy phase. That is, the spatial structure
of ∆vθ(·, t, c) effectively encodes where the edit will land before any detail has been resolved in xt. Second,
as Figure 2 illustrates anecdotally, intermediate predictions in this same phase are already predictive of the
final outcome, i.e., we expect a successful edit and an over-aggressive one to diverge early. Taken together,
this identifies the noisy phase as the regime in which TI2I editing is effectively decided, and in which any
corrective intervention is likely to be both necessary and sufficient. We turn to such an intervention next.
3.2
The Unconditional Branch Pathology and a Simple Remedy
One interpretation of non-monotonic localization profiles observed in Figure 3 is that they reflect a modeling
defect. Specifically, edit failures, as shown on the right in Figure 2, may substantially degrade the average
LG in early stages of flow modeling by placing support outside of ΩM. Then, as t increases, reparation of
the profiles intuitively requires dispersing this excess mass, causing LG to initially increase before collapsing.
Given this reasoning and the analysis of Section 3.1, suggesting that the observed trend is independent from
sampling dynamics, we are therefore motivated to investigate model-intrinsic failure cases near t = 0.
Our insight is that, while TI2I models are trained for editing, they are only implicitly taught to preserve the
source’s structure. In particular, we can formalize this phenomenon by examining the text-unconditional
velocity, vθ(·, t, ∅), which, given no text instruction, we intuitively interpret as a no-edit flow toward the
source.
Concretely, for t ≈0, say t < τ for some τ > 0, we expect this to be well-approximated by
(cimg −Id)/(1 −t), since noisy inputs in this regime carry little information. However, as illustrated in
Figure 4, this no-edit constraint is not necessarily captured during training. Instead, CFG trains the text-
unconditional branch to approximate the same target as the conditional branch, effectively forcing an edit
even in the absence of a text instruction. In that case, it follows that the unconditional branch steers data
toward the mean edit Ectxt[x1 | cimg], which need not reside on the data manifold. As demonstrated in Figure
5, where we perform TI2I modeling with an empty text prompt, the results are therefore not guaranteed to
be close to the source. That is, our analysis suggests that CFG implicitly hurts source preservation.

3.3
Early Correction Unlocks a Richer Alignment-Preservation Tradeoff
Having introduced early correction, we now turn to evaluation. As discussed in Section 1, we are interested in
quantifying the alignment-preservation tradeoff. We consider various proxies for each axis that are commonly
adopted in the literature. Alignment is quantified by comparing the post-edit images with target post-edit
descriptions via CLIP similarity (Radford et al., 2021), PickScore (Kirstain et al., 2023) and HPSv3 (Ma
et al., 2025). Separately, we use the Aesthetics predictor (Schuhmann, 2022), a no-reference, unconditional
human preference proxy operating only on images. For preservation, we compare post-edit images with
corresponding source images via PSNR, LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) and DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024).

We studied CFG in TI2I flows and ar-
gued the recipe of Ho & Salimans (2021)
is poorly integrated. Our observation is
that CFG trains the text-unconditional
branch to approximate the mean edit
rather than a no-edit flow, so the mecha-
nism responsible for alignment implicitly
erodes preservation. We localized this ef-
fect to the noisy phase of modeling and
proposed an intervention that substitutes
a no-edit velocity into the unconditional
branch for t < τ.
It requires no addi-
tional evaluations and recovers markedly
richer alignment-preservation tradeoffs.
We do not present a rule for τ. In prac-
tice, however, a uniform choice delivers
consistent improvements. Moreover, we
note that open TI2I models often do not
provide complete information about their
development, which may involve post-
training we cannot account for. In these
cases, we cannot cleanly attribute the
success or relative ineffectiveness of our
intervention to flow modeling alone.
Our findings caution against blind transfer of techniques across tasks. Despite its ubiquity, CFG is not a
silver bullet and its naive application is harmful for source preservation in editing. We therefore view our
work as an invitation to rethink such recipes. Instead of treating editing as T2I generation with an additional
conditioning signal, we argue that TI2I modeling benefits from task-specific context, here a no-edit prior
grounding generation to the source, and hope this perspective informs the design of future systems.

A
Experimental Setup
We ran inference on Linux machines using RTX Quadro 6000, RTX A5000, H100 GPUs and CUDA 13.0.
Benchmarks were generated via Claude Opus 4.7, FLUX.2 [klein] 4B Base (Black Forest Labs, 2025a)
(1024 × 1024, 50 steps, ω = 4, Euler) and SAM 3 (Carion et al., 2026), with compositions in Figure 10.
For TI2I editing, which consumed most of our compute budget, we used the Euler solver with 25 steps.
Preliminary runs with the recommended 50 steps showed little difference and did not justify the extra cost.
Regarding our pixel-space experiments, we finetuned PixelGen-XXL (Ma et al., 2026b) (512 × 512, bf16) on
NHR-Edit (Kuprashevich et al., 2026), OmniEdit (Wei et al., 2025), X2I (Xiao et al., 2025) datasets for TI2I
modeling and on Fine-T2I (Ma et al., 2026a) for T2I preservation. We used the JiT flow-matching velocity
loss from Li & He (2026) and LPIPS, DINOv2, REPA (Yu et al., 2025) regularizers applied similarly to the
original PixelGen recipe, gated at t = 0.5. We additionally applied unconditional T2I regularization and a
KL penalty (β =1e-3) against a frozen reference to preserve base generative quality. The model was trained
with the AdamW optimizer, a learning rate of 1e-4 and a batch size of 128 for 20k iterations.
We note that our adaptation of PixelGen reuses the T2I backbone without introducing any new parameters.
In particular, source conditioning is realized through token concatenation, following the Kontext paradigm
(Black Forest Labs, 2025b). That is, source images are embedded like the noisy inputs and their tokens are
appended to the noisy token sequence inside the transformer. To distinguish them, noisy tokens carry the
denoising timestep whereas source tokens are marked as noise-free, i.e., t = 1. With this setup, information
therefore propagates through the model’s existing joint attention, requiring no adapters or auxiliary encoders.
