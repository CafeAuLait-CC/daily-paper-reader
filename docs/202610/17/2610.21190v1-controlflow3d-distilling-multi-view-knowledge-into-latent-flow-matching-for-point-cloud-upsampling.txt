Abstract
Point cloud upsampling is essential for 3D applications but remains challenging
due to sparse, under-constrained observations. Multi-view images can resolve
geometric ambiguity, but existing multi-modal methods do not fully exploit visual
cues. We propose ControlFlow3D, a framework that distills knowledge from
pretrained foundation models into a lightweight generation network during training,
enabling strong performance without multi-view images available at test time.
Specifically, our method performs conditional flow matching in a structured latent
space defined by a frozen 3D encoder. Multi-view image tokens and 3D latent
tokens are jointly clustered into shared prototypes via unsupervised soft KMeans,
the resulting prototypes guide the flow trajectory through velocity control and are
aligned by a consistency loss that progressively transfers image knowledge into
the base network. A Mamba-based geometry decoder then maps latent features to
dense coordinates with linear complexity. We also construct ShapeNetPU, a 35K-
object multi-modal benchmark that is 31× larger than prior datasets. Extensive
experiments across multiple benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of our
method and its strong generalization capability. Code and dataset are available at
https://github.com/I2-Multimedia-Lab/ControlFlow3D.

Introduction
Point cloud upsampling aims to generate a denser point set from a sparse input while preserving the
underlying 3D surface geometry [1, 2, 3, 4]. This task is useful for 3D perception and reconstruction
systems, where point clouds are widely used in autonomous driving [5, 6], robotics [7, 8, 9], and
augmented reality [10, 11]. In practical sensing scenarios, point clouds can be sparse and non-uniform
because of limited sensor resolution, occlusions, and viewpoint-dependent sampling. Recovering
dense geometry from such observations remains challenging because the sparse input provides only
partial measurements of the underlying surface.

The main difficulty of point cloud upsampling is that a sparse input provides incomplete observations
of the underlying surface, so multiple dense point sets may be consistent with the same input.
Additional visual observations can provide useful cues for reducing this ambiguity, including object-
level semantics, visible boundaries, and geometry-aware latent features [12, 13, 14]. However, using
such cues at inference time can reduce the practicality of an upsampling model, especially when
synchronized multi-view images are unavailable.
Existing multi-modal upsampling methods such as MVP-Net [12] rely on rendered multi-view depth
images during both training and inference. Generated by projecting point clouds into 2D views, these
depth maps introduce preprocessing and may lose fine 3D structure; they also mainly encode depth
and contour rather than richer visual priors from pretrained multi-view image models. SSPU-Net [13]
avoids test-time images by using differentiable rendering as a self-supervised signal, but image
features are never fed to the upsampling network, preventing it from internalizing visual semantics.
These limitations motivate our distillation setting: we leverage rich features from pretrained multi-
view image models as training-time guidance, while keeping the final model point-only at inference.
Figure 1: Motivation and empirical overview of ControlFlow3D.
In this work, we propose ControlFlow3D, a framework that distills knowledge from pretrained multi-
view models into an efficient point cloud upsampling network. A central challenge is transferring
multi-view knowledge without paired pixel-to-point supervision. We address this through a shared
prototype space, where joint clustering of image and 3D tokens discovers cross-modal semantic
correspondences in an unsupervised manner. During training, image patch tokens from VGGT [15]
and 3D target tokens from Utonia [14] are projected into a common space and jointly clustered by
soft KMeans [16, 17]. Each cluster yields two complementary prototypes: an image-side prototype
that aggregates semantic cues from multi-view observations, and a 3D-side prototype that aggregates
dense latent structure at the same query support. A prototype consistency loss aligns the two sides,
encouraging the 3D-side prototypes to absorb the texture and semantic cues encoded in the image-side
prototypes. The 3D-side prototypes, now enriched with distilled visual knowledge, then produce
query-wise scale and shift controls over the latent velocity field via soft query-to-prototype assignment.
Through this design, multi-view images guide the flow trajectory during training, while the base
velocity network progressively internalizes the cross-modal knowledge and operates on sparse point
clouds alone at inference.
Built on the above core design, ControlFlow3D is composed of three complementary components.
First, conditional flow matching is performed in a structured latent space defined by a frozen pretrained
3D encoder, providing a geometry-aware representation that stabilizes generation. Second, the
prototype-based distillation mechanism injects multi-view knowledge into the velocity field through
semantic alignment and control. Finally, a Mamba-based geometry decoder maps the generated latent
features to dense Euclidean coordinates, enabling efficient high-density reconstruction.
Training this multi-modal pipeline at scale requires precisely aligned 2D and 3D data. Existing
benchmarks such as PU-GAN [2] and PU1K [3] are too small for large-scale multi-modal training,
and none provide synchronized multi-view images aligned with point clouds. We therefore construct
ShapeNetPU, a high-fidelity multi-modal dataset derived from ShapeNet, containing 35K objects
with clean point clouds and synchronized multi-view images, providing 31× more data than prior
benchmarks.
Extensive experiments demonstrate state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance on multiple benchmarks,
with 1.5× faster inference than flow-matching baselines and 7.3× faster than diffusion-based methods.
Our method also generalizes well to other upsampling benchmarks [2, 3] and to real-world scene-level
point clouds [18].

Overall, our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose a new paradigm for efficient point cloud upsampling that distills knowledge
from foundation models into a generation network.
• We propose an unsupervised shared-prototype distillation mechanism that jointly clusters
multi-view image tokens and 3D target tokens into shared centers, making 3D prototypes
absorb visual knowledge and produce guidance for the latent velocity field.
• We design a Mamba-based geometry decoder that efficiently maps generated latent features
to dense point coordinates with linear-complexity sequence modeling.
• We construct ShapeNetPU, a large-scale, high-fidelity multi-modal benchmark for point
cloud upsampling, with clean point clouds and synchronized multi-view images.

Related Work
Flow Matching. Flow matching [19, 20, 21] has emerged as a powerful generative paradigm that
learns a velocity field transporting a simple prior to the data distribution, offering simulation-free
training and efficient ODE-based sampling. It has been successfully applied to high-dimensional
visual generation tasks [22, 23, 24]. We adopt flow matching for 3D upsampling but address a different
setting: transporting sparse-conditioned latent features toward dense 3D targets, with multi-view
image guidance distilled into a point-only inference model.
Point Cloud Upsampling. Existing methods fall into regression-based and generative approaches.
Regression-based methods directly predict dense point coordinates [1, 25, 3, 26, 4, 27, 28]. Although
these methods achieve strong benchmark performance, deterministic coordinate regression may
favor averaged predictions in ambiguous regions. This reflects the ill-posed nature of point cloud
upsampling, where a sparse input may correspond to multiple plausible dense surfaces [1]. Generative
methods [2, 29, 30, 31] model more flexible output distributions, but typically operate in the raw
coordinate space, where the lack of structural regularization can cause unstable generation. To reduce
the ambiguity of sparse 3D observations, prior work has explored external modalities, primarily
for point cloud completion [32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37] rather than upsampling. For upsampling, SSPU-
Net [13] imposes image-space consistency via differentiable rendering, but uses images only as a
pixel-level loss signal without feeding image features into the network. In contrast, MVP-Net [12]
and PULLM [38] fuse multi-view depth or language features into the pipeline via concatenation
or cross-attention, requiring these external modalities at inference. Our method differs from both:
through unsupervised prototype alignment, we distill multi-view knowledge into the generation
network during training, allowing the model to operate on point clouds alone at inference. More
related works are discussed in the Appendix.

Methodology
3.1
Overview
Given a sparse input point cloud (and multi-view images during training), our goal is to produce a
dense and geometrically consistent point cloud. The framework is organized as follows. We first
define a structured latent space using the frozen 3D encoder (§3.2). Within this space, we design a
conditional flow-matching generation network (§3.3) that transports sparse-conditioned features
toward the dense prior target. To distill multi-view knowledge into this network, we introduce
an unsupervised prototype-guided velocity controller (§3.3): image and 3D tokens are jointly
clustered into shared prototypes, which guide the flow trajectory during training and are progressively
absorbed into the base velocity network via a consistency loss. Finally, a Mamba-based geometry
decoder (§3.4) maps the generated latent features to dense coordinates, completing the latent
encoding-to-decoding pipeline alongside the frozen encoder.

3.3
Multi-View Distilled Latent Flow Matching
This section presents the core generation network: a conditional latent flow that transports sparse-
aligned features toward the 3D target, with multi-view knowledge distilled into the velocity network
during training to enable image-free inference.
Sparse geometry conditioning.
The sparse input point cloud serves as the geometric con-
dition for latent transport.
We encode the sparse observation Pin using a lightweight Point-
Net++ [8] backbone FE(·) to obtain point-wise local features and a global shape descriptor:
Flocal = FE(Pin, Q), Fglobal = MaxPool(Flocal). The sparse-query support Q anchors all sub-
sequent feature operations to explicit 3D locations. The local sparse features are aligned to Q, and
the conditioning vector is then formed as c = Concat[Flocal, Fglobal, Q], ensuring that the latent
dynamics remain continuously grounded in the observed sparse geometry.
Conditional Latent Flow Matching. With the sparse-query support Q and condition c already
defined above, we construct the source state x0 = Gather(E3D(Pin), Q) + σϵ, where ϵ ∼N(0, I)
denotes Gaussian noise, and σ controls the noise strength. Here, Gather(·) reuses the FPS indices
used to sample Q to select the corresponding sparse latent tokens from E3D(Pin), yielding Nq tokens
of dimension D. In the implementation, Gaussian noise is added to the gathered features, followed
by 3-NN interpolation onto the sparse-query support Q. The target endpoint is x1 = Ztar. Although
both x0 and x1 are encoded by E3D, they carry fundamentally different information: x0 captures

Unsupervised Prototype-Guided Velocity Control
While Ztar defines the target latent endpoint, the sparse condition c alone may leave the transport
trajectory ambiguous. Multi-view images carry rich complementary cues, such as appearance,
boundaries and part-level semantics, that can disambiguate this trajectory. Rather than directly fusing
2D features into the 3D pipeline, we design a distillation mechanism based on unsupervised prototype
learning: image knowledge guides the velocity field during training and is progressively absorbed into
the base network, enabling image-free inference. To transfer multi-view cues without pixel-to-point
correspondence while retaining point-only inference, we use shared prototypes as a compact interface
for training-time velocity guidance.

3.5
Overall Training Objective
The full framework is optimized by combining latent flow matching, prototype alignment, and
coordinate reconstruction. The overall objective is:
L = Lgeo + λFM LFM + λproto Lproto.
(10)
Each loss term reflects the separation of concerns in our framework: LFM drives the latent trajectory
toward the Utonia 3D prior target, Lproto aligns the multi-view prototypes with the 3D prior to keep
the visual controller geometrically consistent, and Lgeo supervises the final coordinate reconstruction.

Experiments
4.1
Datasets and Metrics
Our main evaluation is conducted on ShapeNetPU, a multi-modal point cloud upsampling benchmark.
It contains 35K valid samples from 13 categories. Each object is associated with eight selected views.
We also evaluate on PU-GAN [2] and PU1K [3]. Following prior works [3, 4, 41], we report Chamfer
Distance (CD), Hausdorff Distance (HD), and point-to-surface distance (P2F), all in units of 10−3.
Lower is better for all three metrics. For more details of the datasets, please refer to the Appendix.
4.2
Quantitative and Qualitative Comparison on ShapeNetPU
Quantitative Comparison.
Table 1 compares our method with representative regression-based and generative baselines on
ShapeNetPU under 4× and 16× upsampling. For a fair comparison, we have replicated all the
methods based on our experimental setup and on the dataset. Our method achieves the best results on
all three metrics under both settings. Two comparisons are particularly informative. First, our method
outperforms PUFM, the strongest coordinate-space flow matching baseline, supporting our claim
that latent-space generation is more stable than coordinate-space generation. Second, the largest gain
appears in P2F, which measures distance to the underlying continuous surface rather than to discrete
target points. The roughly 50% improvement over competing methods suggests that the 3D latent
space provides a strong surface-aware representation, while multi-view guidance further helps the
generated points align with the underlying geometric manifold.

Compared with the sparse input, the generated point clouds recover denser and more continuous

4.3
Quantitative and Qualitative Comparison on PU-GAN and PU1K
To evaluate the transferability of our method, we further conduct experiments on the PU-GAN and
PU1K benchmarks to assess cross-dataset generalization. Note: PU-GAN and PU1K use overlapping
local patches as input samples, whereas our ShapeNetPU pre-training uses complete object-level
point clouds; the non-overlapping patches in our framework are only used inside the decoder. Thus,
PU-GAN/PU1K provide less geometric and semantic context to the frozen 3D encoder, and their
lack of aligned multi-view images further weakens the benefit of visual distillation. Cropped patches
provide incomplete geometry, making it harder for the frozen 3D encoder to extract sufficiently
informative latent features, while the absence of images also prevents the prototype controller from
fully exploiting visual guidance. These factors explain the limited gains on PU-GAN as shown
in Tab 2. PU1K shows stronger results partly because it contains PU-GAN and ShapeNet-derived
shapes, making its distribution closer to ShapeNetPU; after removing all PU1K test shapes from
the ShapeNetPU pre-training split, this reflects distributional overlap rather than test-shape leakage.
Although our PU-GAN metrics are not uniformly state of the art, Figure 5 shows that our visual
details, such as local structures and surface continuity, are comparable to recent SOTA methods. This
indicates that the learned information from ShapeNetPU remains transferable despite patch-based
inputs and missing multi-view supervision.

Ablation Study
We conduct ablation studies on ShapeNetPU under the 4× setting to demonstrate the effectiveness of

Table 3 shows that the gains do not come from a single isolated component. Removing image
distillation weakens the training signal that connects sparse geometry to dense surface structure, as
also shown by the visual comparison in Figure 7. Image distillation improves CD, HD, and P2F while
retaining point-only inference. Figure 8 visualizes corresponding prototype assignments across the
3D input and image views.
Using VGGT as the 2D backbone outperforms DINOv2, indicating that stronger multi-view visual
tokens better guide latent transport. In the 3D space, a larger-scale pretrained encoder further improves
performance. This is expected because the frozen 3D encoder defines the target latent space, and
its representation capacity affects latent supervision quality. Nevertheless, the overall trend remains
stable as long as the encoder provides an informative geometry-aware latent space.
For the prototype count, increasing Kproto from 2 to 8 consistently improves all metrics, while
10 prototypes bring no further gain, suggesting that too few prototypes under-represent multi-view
semantics, whereas excessive clustering may fragment the shared prototype space.

Generalization to Real-World Point Cloud Upsampling in ScanNet
Finally, we evaluate qualitative generalization on ScanNet [18], a real-world indoor RGB-D scanning

5
Limitation and Conclusion
Limitation. Our framework depends on a frozen pretrained 3D encoder and thus inherits the
expressiveness of its latent space. When the input is an incomplete local patch rather than a complete
object-level point cloud, the encoder may lack sufficient geometric and semantic context, and out-
of-distribution shapes may not be faithfully represented by the pretrained 3D encoder. Although
inference is image-free, training still requires aligned multi-view images, limiting scalability to
datasets with such pairs.
Conclusion. We presented ControlFlow3D, a multi-view distilled latent flow matching framework for
point cloud upsampling. Our method shifts generation from unstable raw coordinates to a structured
latent space defined by a pretrained 3D encoder, and uses an unsupervised shared-prototype distillation
mechanism to transfer multi-view visual knowledge into the velocity network during training. A
Mamba-based geometry decoder maps the generated latent features to dense coordinates with linear-
complexity sequence modeling. We further construct ShapeNetPU, a multi-modal upsampling dataset
for training. Extensive experiments on multiple benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness and strong
generalization of our method.

B
Additional Related Work on Knowledge Distillation
Knowledge distillation for point cloud learning. Knowledge distillation transfers information
from richer predictions, features, or auxiliary modalities into a target model [44]. In point cloud
learning, prior methods adapt the transferred signal to 3D structure through point-level or voxel-
level supervision [45, 46]. Other works introduce local-structure, global-context, or cross-modal
constraints to improve the student representation [47, 48, 49]. These methods mainly rely on direct
15


imitation or auxiliary losses, such as matching logits, responses, attention maps, intermediate features,
or visual supervisory signals.
Distillation for point cloud reconstruction and upsampling. For reconstruction and upsampling,
existing work transfers completion priors or vector-quantized codebook knowledge to improve dense
geometry prediction [50, 51]. MVP-Net is closely related because it uses rendered multi-view
depth images for upsampling [12]. However, it still requires inference-time depth images, extra
preprocessing, and direct point-depth feature fusion. These methods show the value of geometric
distillation, while remaining different from our training-time image distillation setting.
ControlFlow3D uses shared-prototype distillation instead of direct feature imitation or direct concate-
nation of 2D and 3D features. During training, VGGT image tokens and Utonia dense-target tokens
are projected into a common prototype space, where image-side and 3D-side prototypes are aligned
without explicit pixel-to-point correspondence. The aligned prototypes modulate the latent velocity
field during training, and inference uses only the sparse point cloud and sparse 3D features.
C
Dataset Preparation
Existing point cloud upsampling benchmarks, such as PU-GAN [2] and PU1K [3], are mainly
designed for patch-based learning. Their source models are relatively limited, and each object is
usually decomposed into many overlapping local patches. This design has two limitations. First,
the network mainly observes isolated local neighborhoods rather than complete object-level context,
making it difficult to model global structural consistency across different regions. Second, during
inference, multiple upsampled patches are usually merged and then downsampled by FPS. Although
this strategy reduces duplicated candidates, it may alter the original density distribution of the object.
Since different regions of a shape do not necessarily have the same sampling density, fusion based on
overlapping local patches may affect the preservation of fine geometric details.
To address these issues, we construct a new multi-modal point cloud upsampling dataset, denoted
as ShapeNetPU. Unlike previous patch-only benchmarks, ShapeNetPU provides both whole-object
context and non-overlapping patch-level upsampling pairs, making it suitable for global-to-local
upsampling pipelines. Starting from the R2N2 release [33], we collect 43,783 samples, each with 24
rendered views and camera metadata. For each object, we select eight views with relatively diverse
camera poses as the multi-view image input. We then match each sample to its corresponding OBJ
mesh in ShapeNetCore.v1 [52].
We generate the point-cloud supervision directly from the matched ShapeNetCore.v1 OBJ meshes.
For each mesh, we first perform area-weighted random surface sampling to obtain 50K surface points.
This dense random set is then downsampled by farthest point sampling (FPS) to 20K points, which is
used as the intermediate surface pool. The 50K-to-20K procedure reduces the randomness of direct
low-count sampling while keeping the final pool well spread over the mesh surface.
From the 20K surface pool, we construct paired sparse and dense point clouds with aligned non-
overlapping patches. We first run KMeans on the 20K points to obtain eight object-level spatial
patches. For each patch, we apply a two-stage FPS strategy. In the first stage, points are over-sampled
within each KMeans patch, using an over-sampling factor α before the final quota is enforced. The
local FPS is initialized from the point closest to the object centroid, which gives a deterministic initial
point for each patch. In the second stage, candidates from all patches are merged and selected by a
global FPS procedure with a strict per-patch quota. This global second pass prevents any single patch
from dominating the sampling process and alleviates local density inconsistency caused by uneven
raw mesh tessellation or random surface sampling.
The final sparse input contains 2048 points, with eight patches of 256 points each, while the dense
target contains 8192 points, with the corresponding eight patches of 1024 points each. Points are
written in patch order, and the patch index lists record the contiguous vertex ranges, so the model can
recover the sparse-dense patch correspondence without re-clustering during training. A stable seed
derived from the sample path makes the preprocessing reproducible.
After preprocessing and filtering invalid cases, ShapeNetPU contains 35,762 valid 3D models from
13 ShapeNet categories. Each sample contains the whole-object sparse point cloud, dense target point
cloud, multi-view images, and original mesh. The sparse and dense point clouds retain the standard
4× patch upsampling setting with 256 input points and 1024 target points per local patch.

Network and Experimental Details
D.1
Utonia Details
Utonia is used as the frozen 3D encoder that defines the structural latent space in our framework.
Rather than asking the flow model to generate Euclidean coordinates directly, we first encode both
sparse and dense point sets into a compact geometry-aware feature space. In implementation, the
pretrained Utonia encoder processes point clouds with their original 3D coordinates and returns point-
wise features, which are then projected to a 128-dimensional latent representation. These features are
stored offline for both sparse inputs and dense ground truth point sets, so the training loop can focus
on learning the transport dynamics rather than repeatedly running the frozen 3D foundation model.
Keeping the encoder frozen preserves the geometric priors learned from large-scale pretraining and
provides a stable target distribution for flow matching. If the encoder were jointly optimized with
the generator, the target endpoint would keep changing during training and make the latent transport
problem less well defined.
Figure 10: Visualization of point-wise Utonia features on representative shapes. The feature responses
vary smoothly over coherent geometric regions, indicating that the frozen 3D encoder provides a
structured latent space for sparse-to-dense transport.
The two Utonia feature streams play different roles. The sparse-aligned feature initializes the source
state x0, while the dense ground-truth feature defines the target state x1. We align the dense feature
to the sparse query support through three-nearest-neighbor interpolation, so each flow token has a
dense-feature endpoint at an inference-available query location.
D.2
Sparse Geometry Condition Network Details
The sparse geometry condition network is deliberately lightweight and input-driven. We use a
PointNet++ MSG encoder-decoder to extract both local and global descriptors from the sparse input
point cloud. The set abstraction hierarchy samples 1024 →256 →64 →16 points and uses two

coarse descriptors back to the original sparse resolution, producing a point-wise local feature map
with 256 channels. A max-pooled global descriptor is projected to the same 256-dimensional channel
space.
From the sparse coordinates, we select 256 flow-token locations by FPS. The local condition is
gathered or interpolated to these token locations, while the global descriptor is broadcast to every
token. The final condition token for each query therefore contains three pieces of information:

D.3
Latent Flow Matching Network Details
The latent velocity network predicts the displacement from the sparse latent state to the dense latent
target. Given xt ∈R128×256, the network first injects the time embedding through a FiLM-style
conditional linear layer. We use the compact time code [t, sin(t), cos(t)], which gives the velocity field
both monotonic progress information and periodic basis functions for smoother conditioning. Four
cross-attention blocks then repeatedly exchange information between latent flow tokens and sparse
geometry condition tokens. Each block contains a multi-head cross-attention layer, a feed-forward
layer, residual connections, layer normalization, and time-dependent affine modulation.
The prototype-guided controller is applied after the base velocity field during training. Multi-view
image features are provided as patch tokens from VGGT. To avoid noisy token-wise 2D and 3D fusion,
we compress the visual evidence into Kproto = 8 shared semantic prototypes by default. These
prototypes summarize local visual evidence and provide a compact basis for query-wise semantic
modulation. Each soft-KMeans update uses cosine-normalized tokens and a temperature-controlled
assignment, with four update iterations by default.
At training time, the prototype clustering observes both VGGT image tokens and query-aligned
dense 3D target tokens. The image tokens provide cross-view cues for velocity modulation, while the
dense target tokens constrain the shared prototypes using the geometry of the target surface. The soft
query-to-prototype assignment distributes prototype-level scale and bias terms back to each query
token, yielding the controlled training velocity
evθ = g(I, xt, t) ⊙vθ(xt, t, c) + b(I, xt, t).
(30)
In the implementation, the prototype scale is parameterized as 1 + 0.35 tanh(·) and the bias as
0.35 tanh(·), so the control signal stays centered around the base velocity and remains bounded. We
further regularize the image prototypes and 3D target prototypes with a cosine consistency loss, which
discourages the visual controller from overemphasizing appearance cues that are not geometrically
supported.
For inference, we integrate the learned ODE with a fixed 5-step Euler solver. Starting from the
sparse-aligned latent state, the model repeatedly evaluates the learned velocity field and updates the
latent tokens. A small residual endpoint MLP is then applied to the final state before decoding, which
corrects endpoint bias accumulated by the finite-step rollout.
D.4
Decoder Network Details
The geometry decoder converts terminal latent tokens into dense point coordinates through a direct
feed-forward reconstruction path. Given the sparse point cloud, sparse local condition, and predicted
latent endpoint, it produces coordinates without adding a separate sampling or diffusion process.
The latent flow outputs 256 global query tokens. For each sparse patch, these tokens are used as a
flow-condition token set and fused with the patch’s local sparse features inside the decoder.
We use a two-stage Mamba-based decoder with upsampling factors (2, 2). The first stage takes a
sparse patch and produces a 2× point set. It fuses the sparse local condition, positional embedding,
and latent flow condition through a cross-attention layer, followed by a multi-scan Mamba block.

residual head:

D.5
Implementation Details
We implement ControlFlow3D in PyTorch and train it with distributed data parallelism on four
NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs. Unless otherwise specified, all models are trained for 500 epochs with a
global batch size of 128, corresponding to 32 samples per GPU. The global test batch size is also set
to 128. We set OMP_NUM_THREADS=1 and MKL_NUM_THREADS=1, and use four dataloader workers
per training process with a prefetch factor of 2. We use AdamW as the optimizer with an initial
learning rate of 2 × 10−4 and weight decay 1 × 10−4. The learning rate follows a cosine annealing
schedule and decays to 1 × 10−6 by the end of training. Gradients are clipped with a maximum norm
of 1.0 for stable optimization.
D.6
Training Protocol
For ShapeNetPU training, each sample contains a sparse input of 2048 points and a dense target of
8192 points. Point coordinates are normalized by the sparse input centroid and scale before being
processed by the model. We use a patch-based training protocol by default, where the sparse input
is divided into eight non-overlapping patches of 256 points and the dense target is divided into the
corresponding eight patches of 1024 points. The latent flow still observes the complete sparse object
context, while the Mamba-based decoder receives patch-level supervision for local reconstruction.
The training objective combines the global coordinate reconstruction loss, local patch-level recon-
struction loss, latent flow matching loss, and prototype consistency loss. We use the weighted
objective
L = Lgeo + λFMLFM + λprotoLproto,
(32)
where Lgeo contains the global coordinate reconstruction term and the local patch-level reconstruction
term. In the default setting, the local patch-level term is weighted by 0.35 within Lgeo. The flow-
matching loss weight is linearly scheduled from 0.003 to 0.0015, with a 20-epoch warm-up at the
initial value. The prototype consistency loss weight is linearly scheduled from 0.01 to 0.005. During
training, multi-view image features guide the latent velocity field.
D.7
Loss-Weight Selection and Ablation
The auxiliary latent losses have different numerical scales from the coordinate reconstruction loss.
We therefore use small decaying weights: λFM provides latent endpoint supervision early in training,
and λproto aligns image-side and 3D-side prototypes before becoming a lightweight regularizer. This
ablation changes only these auxiliary weights and keeps the multi-view controller architecture fixed,
so it differs from the “w/o distillation” setting in the main paper.
The default schedule gives the lowest errors in this ablation. Too little auxiliary supervision weak-
ens latent alignment, while larger weights can overemphasize feature-space objectives relative to
coordinate reconstruction.

D.9
Supervised Fine-Tuning on PU-GAN and PU1K
To evaluate transferability on standard public benchmarks, we further perform supervised fine-tuning
on PU-GAN [2] and PU1K [3]. Since these benchmarks do not provide synchronized multi-view
images, fine-tuning is conducted in a sparse-only setting starting from the supervised ShapeNetPU
checkpoint. Both benchmarks are trained on the official 4× patch training sets: each sparse training
patch contains 256 points, and the corresponding dense target contains 1024 points. The official
PU-GAN training set contains 24K patch pairs, and the official PU1K training set contains 69K patch
pairs. Sparse Utonia features are used as the feature initialization for the sparse input points.
We fine-tune all model parameters with the AdamW optimizer. The base learning rate is 2 × 10−6,
with weight decay 1×10−5 and betas (0.9, 0.95). Following the different roles of model components,
we use learning-rate multipliers of 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 for the sparse geometry backbone, latent flow
network, and Mamba decoder, respectively. The learning rate follows cosine annealing with a
minimum value of 0.1 times the base learning rate. Gradients are clipped with maximum norm 1.0.
We use two RTX 4090 GPUs for each fine-tuning run, with a global batch size of 128. PU-GAN is
fine-tuned for 50 epochs, while PU1K is fine-tuned for 40 epochs.
The supervised fine-tuning objective is the Chamfer-distance reconstruction loss computed after
a 5-step sparse-only rollout. During testing, we follow the patch-based evaluation protocol: each
2048-point input shape is divided into 256-point patches, patch predictions are merged, and FPS is
used to match the target output size of 8192 points. Testing is performed every epoch with patch size
256 and patch rate 3.0. This protocol keeps the fine-tuning and public-benchmark evaluation aligned
with the patch-based setting of PU-GAN and PU1K.
D.10
Inference and Evaluation
At inference time, ControlFlow3D takes only the sparse point cloud as the raw input, and sparse
3D features are computed from this point input. We use a fixed 5-step Euler solver for latent flow
integration. The predicted terminal latent tokens are then decoded by the Mamba-based geometry
decoder to produce the dense point cloud. For the default 4× setting on ShapeNetPU, the model
upsamples 2048 input points to 8192 output points. For larger ratios, we follow the same model
rollout strategy used in the main experiments.
Following common point cloud upsampling protocols, we report Chamfer Distance (CD), Hausdorff
Distance (HD), and point-to-surface distance (P2F). CD and HD are computed between the predicted
and ground-truth point sets in the normalized coordinate frame. For P2F, predictions are transformed
back to the original object coordinate frame and evaluated against the retained mesh surface. Lower
values indicate better performance for all metrics.

F
Discussion
F.1
Failure Analysis
The sparse-to-dense mapping remains under-constrained when the input provides weak geometric
evidence or when the relevant structure is only partially observed.
The first pattern is weak evidence for thin structures, as shown in Figure 13(a). Narrow parts such
as airplane wings, chair legs, lamp supports, and similar elongated components may contain only a
small number of sparse input points. The latent flow can infer the coarse structural identity, but the
decoder may place points slightly off the true surface or recover an incomplete structure compared
with the ground truth.
The second pattern is ambiguity caused by limited local evidence, as shown in Figure 13(b). When
several dense surfaces are compatible with the same sparse observations, the model may generate a
23


Figure 13: Failure analysis of ControlFlow3D. Red boxes highlight challenging regions in the sparse
input, our output, and the dense ground truth (GT).
geometrically plausible averaged solution. This behavior can blur sharp local boundaries or create
small density leakage between adjacent semantic parts.
The third pattern is incomplete recovery of occluded or sparsely visible structures, as shown in
Figure 13(c). Multi-view distillation provides additional boundary and part-level cues during training,
and these cues are encoded by the learned velocity field. At inference, the model does not require
2D inputs. It predicts from the sparse point cloud and the distilled prior. If a structure is poorly
represented in the point input and not reliably encoded in the learned prior, the output may preserve
the main visible region while missing part of the occluded or narrow component.
F.2
Limitations
Our framework relies on pretrained visual and 3D foundation models. This provides the latent flow
with a structured representation space, but it also makes the method dependent on the coverage and
biases of these encoders. If the frozen 3D encoder under-represents a particular geometry family, the
corresponding latent targets may not faithfully preserve its fine structures. Similarly, if the rendered
multi-view images do not reveal important regions because of occlusion or limited viewpoints, the
prototype controller cannot recover information that is absent from the training signals.
Finally, the decoder is designed around a native 4× decoding path. Larger arbitrary ratios are handled
by repeated rollout or post-processing, which keeps the architecture simple but may increase inference
cost and affect density preservation at very high ratios. A future direction is to condition the latent
flow and decoder directly on the target density ratio.
G
Broader Impact
ControlFlow3D leverages multi-view and 3D foundation model knowledge during training while
requiring only sparse point clouds at inference. This setting is relevant to applications where dense
geometric observations are expensive or difficult to obtain, such as robotics, autonomous driving,
augmented reality, digital twins, and cultural heritage digitization. Denser and more surface-consistent
point clouds may benefit downstream perception, reconstruction, and visualization systems. However,
point cloud upsampling also carries potential risks because the generated points are inferred from
learned priors rather than directly measured by sensors. In safety-critical applications, such as
autonomous navigation or industrial inspection, upsampled geometry should therefore be treated
as model-assisted estimation rather than ground-truth observation. Although our method does not
use identity-related data, real-world 3D scans may still contain sensitive spatial information, and
responsible use should consider privacy, fairness, and uncertainty in practical use.
