# Abstract 

Flow matching has emerged as a powerful generative family for simulation-based infer-ence (SBI). However, sampling and density evaluation require numerical integration at inference time. This makes posterior sam-pling in neural posterior estimation costly. More importantly, it can make repeated like-lihood evaluation prohibitively expensive and impractical for Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling, which may require thou-sands of repeated density evaluations. Here, we develop conditional mean flows for poste-rior and likelihood approximation, combining few-step sampling with tractable density eval-uation. We show that the mean flow objective identifies conditional flow maps and that in-dependent masking enables direct estimation of marginal likelihoods. We evaluate both posterior and likelihood estimation across sev-eral tasks and show that conditional mean flows retain the flexibility of flow-based den-sity estimators while significantly reducing inference time. In particular, single-step con-ditional mean flow density evaluation yields estimation error comparable to 200-step flow matching while enabling about 200-fold faster downstream MCMC sampling. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Mechanistic simulators are used to describe processes ranging from cognitive science (Ratcliff et al., 2016) to disease modeling (Ye et al., 2025). Yet, while practically important, their likelihoods are often unavailable in closed form. Simulation-based inference (SBI; Sisson et al., 2018; Cranmer et al., 2020; Deistler et al., 2025; Arruda et al., 2026) addresses this challenge by using simulated parameter-observation pairs (θ, y) to infer unknown parameters, and amortized methods reuse the trained network across datasets (Bürkner et al., 2023). Two popular forms of SBI are neural posterior estima-tion (NPE) and neural likelihood estimation (NLE). NPE learns a posterior model qψ (θ | y) which can sample from the approximate posterior directly. NLE, instead, learns a likelihood model qψ (y | θ) which re-quires Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to sample from the posterior. NPE amortizes over datasets but is usually tied to the prior and data format used in training, whereas NLE needs MCMC for every dataset, but allows the prior to be changed after training and composes exchangeable likelihoods across any number of i.i.d. observations (Cranmer et al., 2020). Many SBI methods demand neural estimators that are both flexible and inexpensive to evaluate. In NLE, MCMC requires thousands of likelihood evaluations per posterior, making each evaluation a practical bottle-neck. Further, hierarchical approaches (Arruda et al., 2024) or self-consistency objectives that improve NPE (Schmitt et al., 2024a; Mishra et al., 2026) and enable model comparison (Kucharský and Bürkner, 2026) rely on density evaluation during training or inference. So far, normalizing flows have been a standard choice in these applications because they evaluate densities in a single pass (Papamakarios et al., 2021; Dinh et al., 2017; Durkan et al., 2019). However, their transforma-tions must remain invertible with tractable Jacobians, which restricts how inductive biases can be built into the architecture (Chen et al., 2025). Autoregressive flows based on causal transformers (e.g., TARFlow and STARFlow) relax this restriction (Zhai et al., 2025; Gu et al., 2025), but impose a fixed variable ordering and require sequential inversion for sampling. More-over, tractable joint densities do not generally imply tractable marginals, which are required with missing observations (Yalavarthi et al., 2024). Flow matching and diffusion models instead learn a velocity or score field with an unconstrained neural network (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2022; Wild-berger et al., 2023; Sharrock et al., 2024; Arruda et al., 2026), and diffusion transformers can flexibly represent arbitrary conditionals and marginals (Gloeckler et al., arXiv:2610.21657v1 [stat.ML] 15 Oct 2026 Conditional Mean Flows: Few-Step Densities for Simulation-Based Inference 

2024). Yet, their densities require solving an ordinary differential equation (ODE), which can take hundreds of network evaluations and makes repeated likelihood calls during training or within MCMC costly. Few-step generative models reduce the sampling cost, but consis-tency models, for instance, provide no tractable density (Song et al., 2023; Schmitt et al., 2024b). Thus, current SBI approaches either restrict the network architecture or make every density evaluation expensive .To overcome this limitation, we present conditional mean flows for SBI, which learn the average velocity between two times, so that one network evaluation defines a finite-time map based on mean flows (Geng et al., 2025, 2026). We first evaluate direct posterior sampling and density estimation, then focus on learned likelihoods for compositional inference, missing obser-vations, and gradient-based and delayed acceptance MCMC. Concretely, we develop and showcase 1. a conditional mean flow formulation for posterior and likelihood approximation, with exact stepwise Jacobian determinants, and a proof that the mean flow objective suffices to identify conditional flow maps (Section 3.1, Proposition 1); 2. marginal density estimation for missing targets with a masked transformer backbone, and a proof that the masked objective targets the correct marginal likelihood (Section 3.3, Proposition 2); 3. and a benchmark against normalizing flows and regular flow matching on four tasks covering both NLE and NPE, including a single-trial likelihood comparison in which one mean flow step matches the error of 200-step flow matching with two orders of magnitude faster sampling (Section 4); 

# 2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

Simulation-based inference. For conditionally in-dependent observations yn, simulation-based inference targets p(θ | yobs ) ∝ p(θ) QNn=1 p(yn | θ) when the likelihood is unavailable. Classical approximate Bayesian computation compares simulated and ob-served data directly, but becomes less effective as di-mension increases (Sisson et al., 2018). Neural pos-terior estimation (NPE) fits qψ (θ | y) to simulated pairs and draws approximate posterior samples with-out running MCMC for each dataset. Neural likelihood estimation (NLE), instead, fits a conditional density to prior-predictive pairs and uses it within MCMC (for an overview, see Cranmer et al., 2020). A per-observation estimator can be reused across dataset sizes, making single-trial likelihoods useful for mod-els with exchangeable trials (Boelts et al., 2022), and spline coupling flows are the standard tractable-density baseline (Durkan et al., 2019). 

Flow matching and diffusion-based inference. 

Flow matching learns a velocity field v(zt, t ) that trans-ports a Gaussian distribution to the target along a pre-scribed interpolation (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2022). It has been applied to both NPE and NLE, alongside score-based generative models (for a review, see Arruda et al., 2026). Sampling requires ODE inte-gration, and density evaluation additionally integrates the velocity divergence through the continuous change-of-variables formula (Chen et al., 2018), so repeated integration can dominate the cost of NLE. 

Few-step generative models. Consistency models learn transitions from intermediate noisy states toward data, enabling generation in one or a few steps (Song et al., 2023). They also support fast posterior sampling in SBI (Schmitt et al., 2024b; Arruda et al., 2026), although the standard formulation does not provide a tractable posterior density. Flow maps learn finite-time ODE transitions between arbitrary time pairs, and mean flows parameterize these maps through aver-age velocities and can be trained without distillation (Geng et al., 2025, 2026). Rehman et al. (2026) com-bined mean flows with a cycle loss that encourages invertibility to obtain few-step densities for Boltzmann generators. 

MCMC with learned and multi-fidelity likeli-hoods. The no-U-turn sampler (NUTS; Hoffman and Gelman, 2014; Betancourt, 2017) uses density and gra-dient evaluations to construct proposals, and probabilis-tic programming frameworks allow learned likelihoods to enter these calculations as model components (Car-penter et al., 2017). Delayed acceptance MCMC uses a cheap approximation to screen proposals while leav-ing the fine target invariant (Christen and Fox, 2005). Moreover, its multilevel variant organizes several ap-proximations (Lykkegaard et al., 2023), and related approaches in SBI use simulators at different resolu-tions (Prescott and Baker, 2021; Tatsuoka et al., 2026).

# 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate direct posterior approximation on two moons and likelihood approximation on the three fur-ther benchmarks, where the learned densities enter MCMC. The benchmarks show the following: • Two moons : few steps give accurate samples, so sampling has only a minor speed-accuracy trade-off, and densities improve with more steps (Sec-tion 4.1). • Cognitive model : exchangeable likelihoods yield precision comparable to 200-step flow matching with about 200 × faster MCMC (Section 4.2). • Epidemic renewal : a masked transformer marginalizes missing observations and is more ac-curate than a normalizing flow (Section 4.3). • g-and-k : at a shared budget, more draws beat more steps, and delayed acceptance MCMC is as accurate as NUTS without gradients (Section 4.4). We compare samples with exact references using the classifier two-sample test (C2ST; Lopez-Paz and Oquab, 2017), and densities through the Hellinger distance (Ap-pendix B). Reference-free evaluations use normalized root mean squared error (NRMSE), effective sample size (ESS) and predictive checks, and the calibration for every trained estimator is assessed using tests of accuracy with random points (TARP; Lemos et al., 2023) prior to any other evaluation (Table S1). Tasks, architectures and generative models are described in Appendix B to E. 

4.1 Few Steps Suffice for High Posterior Sample and Density Quality Setup and baseline. The two moons task has a two-dimensional parameter and observation, a bimodal crescent posterior, and an exact likelihood and posterior sampler (Greenberg et al., 2019; Lueckmann et al., 2021). All methods learn the posterior direction and are sampled directly with 5,000 draws per dataset, which we score against 32,000 exact reference draws. We compare a conditional mean flow at step counts S ∈{1, 2, 4, 8, 16 , 32 } with three baselines: a conditional normalizing flow, a conditional consistency model as the established few-step method (Song et al., 2023; Schmitt et al., 2024b), and a separately trained conditional flow matching model integrated with S Euler steps or an adaptive Tsit5 solver (Tsitouras, 2011). We integrate the velocity head of the mean flow with the same Euler or adaptive scheme, which repurposes the mean flow as a flow matching model. For both the consistency model and the mean flow, we tuned the sampling schedule for each step count on held-out observations, which are disjoint from the evaluation datasets. 

Sample quality. The conditional mean flow reaches a similar C2ST at every step count (Figure 1 and Figure S1A), so a single step suffices for sampling and already outperforms the normalizing flow ( 0.523 against 

0.543 ). In contrast, the consistency model needs at least 4 steps to approach this accuracy, and both flow matching variants need at least 32 Euler steps. The adaptive solver further improves the accuracy, but re-quires more than 300 solver steps. Hence, the mean flow with S=1 draws 5,000 samples about 23 times faster than flow matching with 32 Euler steps and 140 times faster than with the adaptive solver (Figure S2). It is also about 6 times faster than the normalizing flow and only reaches its sampling time at S=8 .Since the velocity head of the mean flow and the sep-arately trained model perform similarly under both solvers, mean flow training does not seem to degrade the learned velocity field. The consistency model is also sensitive to its discretization exponent, whose can-didates span a C2ST from 0.54 to 0.98 at S=4 , whereas the EDM schedule of the mean flow changes the C2ST by at most 0.015 (Table S3).

4.2 Single-Step Likelihoods Yield Error Comparable to 200-Step Flow Matching Setup and baseline. The cognitive model describes two-alternative decisions as a Wiener first-passage pro-cess with drift ν, boundary separation a and non-decision time t0 (Ratcliff et al., 2016), and is a standard benchmark for learned single-trial likelihoods (Fen-gler et al., 2021). We train one conditional mean flow on the signed response time y = (2 c − 1) t, with choice c ∈ { 0, 1} and response time t, and compose the learned trial likelihood into joint posteriors over 

N ∈ { 4, 16 , 64 , 256 } trials, which we sample with NUTS. Since a response cannot be faster than t0, we trun-cate the prior of t0 at the fastest observed response time of each run. We compare mean flow step counts 

S ∈ { 1, 2, 4, 8} with a flow matching model integrated with 200 Euler steps. 

Results. With S=1 , the NRMSE and posterior con-traction of the mean flow are comparable to the flow matching baseline at every N (Figure 2, Table S5 and Figure S3), while sampling is 182 to 216 × faster (Fig-ure S2D). Additional steps increase the cost and the number of convergence failures without clearly reducing the parameter error (Figure S4). From all MCMC runs, 

8.5% failed to converge, and all failed runs used S ≥ 4

(Table S5). We attribute this to the fact that the mean flow is trained for few-step sampling and evaluation only, and hence the intermediate maps might be poorly trained and additional steps, unlike in the discretiza-tion of an ODE, need not reduce the approximation error. However, for every method the coverage of the 95% cen-tral interval decreases and the worst posterior predictive discrepancy increases with N (Figure 2B). Composing many approximate likelihoods accumulates their errors while the posterior contracts, which makes even the 200-step baseline overconfident. With S=1 , the addi-tional discretization error further lowers the coverage at N =256 , whereas with S=2 coverage and predictive discrepancy are comparable to the baseline.

In contrast, the normalizing flow frequently generates the sentinel value −1 also at observed time points, which is reflected in a higher predictive RMSE (Table 1). In summary, the flexible architecture of mean flows allows a masked transformer to marginalize missing observations. This yields a lower posterior error than a normalizing flow with sentinel values, but at a higher cost per density evaluation.

# 5 DISCUSSION 

Neural density estimators for SBI have so far traded flexibility for fast density evaluation. Conditional mean flows relax this trade-off by allowing the step count to be chosen after training. In low-dimensional bench-marks, one or two steps matched the accuracy of flow matching while reducing MCMC time by up to two orders of magnitude. Few steps also yielded accurate posterior samples, and under a fixed MCMC budget, more draws were more valuable than more steps. 

Limitations. The main limitation is the cost of den-sity evaluation in high dimensions. Each step requires its full Jacobian and a log-determinant, whose cost grows cubically with the dimension, and differentiating the log-determinant for NUTS involves second-order derivatives. Together with the higher cost of each transformer evaluation, this made MCMC in the re-newal application slower than with the normalizing flow despite using fewer network evaluations. In addi-tion, the cycle loss encourages but does not guarantee invertibility, so the learned densities may not exactly be normalized. Finally, the coverage of every method, including flow matching with 200 steps, decreased at large trial counts, consistent with error accumulation. 

Outlook. Beyond MCMC, fast mean flow densities can support self-consistency objectives (Schmitt et al., 2024a) and, together with prior and likelihood evalu-ations, evidence estimation (Kucharský et al., 2026). Since these methods also rely on repeated density eval-uation, our work could make flexible flow-based estima-tors practical in any SBI setting that requires densities. A.2 Marginalization by Masking 

We show that the masked objective of Section 3.3 targets the marginal distribution of the retained target coordinates. For a set O ⊆ { 1, . . . , d }, we write zO for the retained coordinates of z, and uψ,O and ˆvψ,O for the outputs of the network on O.

Proposition 2 (Marginalization by masking) . Let the mask O ⊆ { 1, . . . , d } be nonempty with law π, independent of (z0, x, ϵ, s, t ), let the network outputs on O depend on the target only through zO , and let the unweighted loss be restricted to the coordinates in O. Assume (A1)–(A3) for each marginal target z0,O with π(O) > 0, and (A4) simultaneously for all such masks. Then every population minimizer satisfies, for every O with π(O) > 0 and 

p(x)-almost every x, ˆvψ,O = v∗O and uψ,O = u∗O , where u∗O is the average velocity of

Consequently, (9) , with the base density and Jacobians evaluated in |O| dimensions, equals log p(zO | x) for every schedule. Proof of Proposition 2. For a fixed mask, independence leaves the simulation law unchanged, and the coordinate-wise interpolation is 

zs, O = (1 − s)z0,O + sϵO , ϵO ∼ N (0, I|O| ).

Additional attention restrictions are covered only when they preserve the ability to represent v∗O and its average velocity. The independence of the synthetic training mask holds by construction and does not impose a missingness mechanism on the observed data. The marginal density uses only the observed coordinates. For p > 0, the residual and hence w depend only on the retained coordinates, so the reduction to the marginal targets still holds, but each summand then has the weighted minimizer discussed in Appendix A.1.

# C ARCHITECTURES, TRAINING AND HYPERPARAMETER SEARCH 

All networks and diagnostics are implemented in BayesFlow 2.0.14 (Radev et al., 2023; Kühmichel et al., 2026), where we added the conditional mean flow as an additional inference network. 

Architectures. 

• Mean flow: An MLP with 5 hidden layers of width 256. The condition x is embedded by a linear layer and concatenated with the target z at the input, and, together with a Fourier embedding with scale 0.3 of the time pair (s, s − t), it modulates every residual block (Perez et al., 2018). The auxiliary velocity head shares all but the last 2 layers. • Masked transformer: A transformer (Peebles and Xie, 2023) with 5 blocks of width 128 and four attention heads. It tokenizes each observation and parameter and receives the time pair (s, s − t) through adaptive layer normalization. During training, each observation of each simulation is dropped independently with probability 0.3, redrawn for every batch. Dropped tokens are excluded from attention and loss. A causal mask additionally restricts each observation token to parameter and observation tokens up to its own time step. The velocity head branches from the last 2 blocks and uses the same masks. Each observation and parameter token receives a learned embedding of its coordinate index, which for observations is the time step, and a Fourier embedding is used only for the time pair. • Consistency model: A continuous-time consistency model (Lu and Song, 2025) with an MLP of 6 hidden layers of width 256. • Flow matching: An MLP with the same depth and width as the mean flow, without the velocity head. • Normalizing flow: A spline coupling flow (Durkan et al., 2019) with 5 coupling layers, each with 2 hidden layers of width 256, and with 2 coupling layers, each with 2 hidden layers of width 128, for the renewal application. 

Training. We trained all networks offline on standardized parameters and observations with a batch size of 256, using AdamW with weight decay 5×10 −3, gradient clipping at norm 1.5, and a learning rate warmed up linearly over the first 5% of steps from 5×10 −5 to 5×10 −4 and then decayed to zero with a cosine schedule. We used 50,176 simulations for two moons and the DDM, 100,000 for g-and-k, and 8,192 for the renewal model to compare architectures under limited data, with 1,024 further simulations for validation. The conditional mean flow, the consistency model, and the flow matching model were trained for 1,000 epochs, and the normalizing flow for 200 epochs with early stopping (patience 10). 

Hyperparameter search. We selected the training hyperparameters of the conditional mean flow with a search in Optuna (Akiba et al., 2019) on two benchmarks: two moons and a three-dimensional variant of the Gaussian linear task with uniform prior. Table S2 lists the search space and the selected values, which all experiments use. The training times are s = u1+ αs and t = u1+ αt with us, u t ∼ U (0 , 1) , and for 0 unshared layers the velocity head reads the velocity from the boundary case uψ (z, s, s, x). Each trial trained one network per benchmark for 250 epochs of 100 freshly simulated batches of size 256 and evaluated it with S=4 steps on 256 held-out datasets with 500 posterior draws each. # D MCMC SAMPLERS 

NUTS. We sample with the NUTS implementation of nutpie (Seyboldt et al., 2026) through PyMC (Salvatier et al., 2016) on the JAX backend (Bradbury et al., 2018), with 4 chains, a diagonal mass matrix, a maximum Conditional Mean Flows: Few-Step Densities for Simulation-Based Inference 

tree depth of 10 and the default target acceptance rate of nutpie . We use the default of 400 warmup iterations, except for g-and-k, where the warmup is 25% of the requested draws of each method. 

Budget on g-and-k. A method whose density costs K network evaluations requests 8,000 /K draws per chain, so that the product of requested draws and network evaluations per density is constant across methods. The normalizing flow counts as K=1 . For delayed acceptance MCMC, the requested iterations are 96 ,000 /(JS c + 100) 

per chain, which accounts for the J=5 coarse evaluations and one fine evaluation per iteration. The budget is set on the requested draws and ignores the number of leapfrog steps per draw, which differs between methods, so the realized number of network evaluations varies. We count one network evaluation per forward pass of the network for one pair (z, x): a normalizing flow costs one evaluation per density, a mean flow with S steps costs S, a velocity field integrated with S Euler steps costs S, and a fixed-step Tsit5 integration costs 7 per step. Jacobians, divergences and gradients are obtained by automatic differentiation on top of these passes and are not counted; their cost is reflected in the wall times. 

Delayed acceptance MCMC. We implement two-level delayed acceptance MCMC (Christen and Fox, 2005) with the subchains of Lykkegaard et al. (2023). For any coarse posterior πc, its invariant distribution is the fine posterior πf (Section 3.2). Both levels use the same trained network: the coarse level evaluates the mean flow with Sc ∈ { 1, 2, 4, 8} steps, and the fine level integrates its velocity head with 100 Tsit5 steps of fixed size and the exact divergence in (1). Each iteration runs a subchain of J=5 random-walk Metropolis steps on πc, following Lykkegaard et al. (2023), and proposes its end point to the fine level with the acceptance probability (11). The random walk draws Gaussian increments with a step size per parameter, initialized at 10% of the prior width, and a global scale. A proposal outside the prior’s support is rejected without evaluating the network. If the subchain does not move, the proposal equals the current state and is accepted without evaluating the fine likelihood. Adaptation takes 25% of the requested iterations and is discarded. During adaptation, a Robbins-Monro update steers the global scale towards a coarse acceptance rate of 0.3, and in its second half, the step sizes are updated to the marginal standard deviations of the states visited in that half, after which the proposal is fixed. We run 4 chains started from prior draws.

E.4 Cognitive Model 

A trial is a Wiener process with drift ν that starts at βa (with fixed relative starting point β = 0 .5) and terminates at the first hit of 0 or a. The response time adds a non-decision time t0, and trials time out after 10 s . The priors are ν ∼ U (−3, 3) , a ∼ U (0 .8, 2) and t0 ∼ U (0 .1, 0.4) , and we simulate with Euler-Maruyama at dt = 10 −4.The network observes the signed response time y = (2 c − 1) t, with c = 1 for the upper boundary, which is bijective with the pair of response time and choice. Since the non-decision time cannot exceed the fastest observed response time, we sample it below this bound, which tightens as trials are added. Within each dataset, trial counts are nested, and every setting keeps 4 × 1,000 NUTS draws after the default warmup. For the first trial count N =4 , the chains start at the center of the prior support with the default jitter of PyMC . The chains for the next trial count start at the posterior median of the preceding trial count, clipped to the current support of t0,with independent jitter per chain.
