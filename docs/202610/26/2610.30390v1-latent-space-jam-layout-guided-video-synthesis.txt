Adding control to video generation often requires training, which demands
additional computational resources, data, or architectural changes. We pro-
pose a training-free approach that utilizes pretrained video diffusion mod-
els to control object layout and motion using coarse layouts. Our method
operates in two passes: first, it steers spatiotemporal placement through
prompt-guided cross-attention to produce a coarse visual guide; second,
this guide conditions the same model to generate a high-quality, layout-
consistent video. The approach enables coordinated motion with strong
temporal consistency, supporting diverse trajectories and object interactions
without additional training. Extensive experiments show that our method
consistently outperforms prior work in overlap and trajectory accuracy, as
well as visual quality across static, dynamic, and multi-object scene settings.

Introduction
Frame composition lies at the core of visual storytelling, defining
the spatial structure through which meaning, motion, and narrative
coherence emerge in video. Recent advances in text-based video
generation [HaCohen et al. 2026; Wan et al. 2025] have demonstrated
a remarkable ability to generate diverse and creative results from
free-form text prompts. Yet the absence of explicit mechanisms for
controlling spatial composition within and across frames continues
to limit their applicability in structured visual storytelling.
We consider the problem of layout-guided text-to-video genera-
tion. Given a pre-trained off-the-shelf video generator, our goal is
to steer its output to match a desired layout. Our method, Latent
Space Justified Attention Mapping (JAM), allows the user to align
the output video to a desired layout, guided by bounding boxes
across frames, without additional training or fine-tuning.
Training-free layout-guided video generation has been explored
in prior works [Huang et al. 2025; Jain et al. 2024; Lian et al. 2023;

Ma et al. 2024; Qiu et al. 2024]. However, existing methods fail in
scenarios involving complex motion, exhibiting limited dynamics
and poor temporal coherence. In contrast, our approach is able to
synthesize complex and dynamic motion while preserving the high
visual fidelity of the pre-trained video diffusion backbones, with-
out introducing additional training or degrading their generative
capacity. Furthermore, both subjective human evaluations and objec-
tive quantitative metrics demonstrate that our method consistently
outperforms these prior approaches (see Results).
There is an inherent trade-off between layout adherence and
visual quality: stronger layout regularization results in poorer visual
quality. Instead of trying to achieve both goals simultaneously, and
thus compromising on both, we take a different route. We split
the pipeline into a guide generation stage followed by a refinement
stage. Our key insight is that this allows us to push layout adherence
to the maximum in the first stage, and rely on the robustness of
modern video diffusion models in the refinement stage to fix the
coarse intermediate results, thus circumventing the tradeoff between
layout adherence and visual quality.
Inspired by attention-based control methods in text-to-image
generation [Chefer et al. 2023; Xie et al. 2023], we manipulate the
cross-attention maps of pre-trained video diffusion models to gener-
ate coarse guidance. However, extending these approaches to video
is not straightforward: the added temporal dimension makes it more
difficult to control object placement consistently across frames, and
the scale of video DiT models makes optimization across all lay-
ers impractical. In contrast, we show that effective control can be
achieved through selective intervention, modifying only a small
subset of cross-attention layers and a limited number of diffusion
steps. As a result, our method can be executed on a single GPU,
with minimal time overhead relative to the inference time of the
underlying backbone models.
In practice, we treat a pre-trained diffusion transformer (DiT)
[Peebles and Xie 2023] video generator as a fixed computational
graph and optimize its latent input rather than its weights. Given
a textual prompt and a spatiotemporal layout, we first steer an
intermediate video toward the desired structure by optimizing this
latent variable, using a loss applied to selected cross-attention layers

of the DiT to align them with the provided bounding boxes. Then,
we rerun the generator and inject the resulting video at an early
diffusion timestep, using it as a coarse prior that the model refines
into the final result.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce a training-free framework for layout-guided
text-to-video generation that enables explicit control over
spatial composition and motion using simple bounding-box
supervision.
• We present the importance of using a two-stage pipeline:
utilizing layout guidance videos as priors to the final result.
• We show that minimal intervention in a pre-trained video
diffusion model via a subset of its cross-attention layers is
sufficient to steer generation.
• We validate our approach through extensive experiments,
demonstrating superior performance over prior methods
across complex, dynamic, and multi-object scenarios.

Related Work
Our work falls within the line of research on training-free con-
trollability for diffusion models, which exploits internal attention
representations as test-time control signals. We address the problem
of spatiotemporal layout and motion control in video generation,
achieving structured control without training, fine-tuning, or archi-
tectural modification.
A growing body of work in text-to-image synthesis demonstrates
that cross-attention can be used as an interface for test-time control.
Several methods [Chefer et al. 2023; Epstein et al. 2023] interpret
attention as a form of semantic grounding and introduce latent
optimization or self-guidance strategies to enforce token presence
and object saliency during sampling. Others [Chen et al. 2024; Phung
et al. 2024; Xie et al. 2023] reshape attention maps to align text
tokens with user specified spatial layouts or regions, enabling box
and region constrained generation. Additional approaches [Agarwal
et al. 2023; Cao et al. 2023] focus on attention segregation, refocusing,
and mutual self-attention control to improve compositionality and
consistency. Collectively, these works establish that attention is not
merely an internal mechanism, but a controllable structure that can
be manipulated at inference time to impose semantic and spatial
constraints without retraining the model.
In video generation, most controllable methods rely on addi-
tional training or fine-tuning. A common strategy is to augment
pre-trained video diffusion models with external adapters or control
branches conditioned on structural signals such as edges or depth
[Hu and Xu 2023], pose or trajectories [Hu 2024; Karras et al. 2023;
Wang et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2024], a combination of these signals
[Jiang et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2023], or camera parameters [Bai et al.
2025; Yang et al. 2024]. These approaches enable explicit control
over motion, layout, and viewpoint. However, they require paired
supervision, dedicated retraining, and model modification, which
restricts their applicability and adds significant computational cost.
Recently, several works have explored training-free control for
text-to-video diffusion models. Peekaboo [Jain et al. 2024] and Trail-
Blazer [Ma et al. 2024] intervene in the cross-attention layers of a
pretrained model to align foreground motion with user-specified
guidance. FreeTraj [Qiu et al. 2024] adopts a similar strategy but
further improves controllability by imposing guidance on both at-
tention maps and the latent noise. LVD [Lian et al. 2023] focuses on
generating a layout guide with an LLM, producing bounding box
trajectories across scenes to steer object placement. SwitchCraft
[Xu et al. 2026] and TempoControl [Schiber et al. 2026] apply a
training-free approach to modify contemporary DiT video models,
but focus only on temporal control.
In contrast to prior training-free video control methods that rely
on direct attention and latent noise manipulation [Jain et al. 2024;
Ma et al. 2024; Qiu et al. 2024], our approach adopts an optimization-
based attention alignment strategy that scales effectively to complex
spatiotemporal layouts. This enables stronger layout adherence
while preserving motion diversity and visual fidelity, as confirmed
by both quantitative metrics and human evaluations (see Results).

Video Diffusion Models
Diffusion models generate data by learning to reverse a gradual
noising process. Given a clean sample 𝑥0, a forward process pro-
gressively adds Gaussian noise over 𝑇steps to obtain 𝑥𝑇. A neural
network 𝜖𝜃is trained to predict the noise at each timestep, enabling

Modern video diffusion models extend image diffusion to the

state-of-the-art approaches adopt a latent diffusion formulation,
where a variational autoencoder encodes frames into a compact

rather than pixel space.
Recent approaches replace convolutional U-Nets with Diffusion
Transformers, which operate on spatio-temporal latent tokens us-
ing self-attention. This design better captures long-range spatial
and temporal dependencies, improving temporal coherence and
scalability. In this work, we assume a latent video diffusion model

Test-Time Latent Optimization via Cross-Attention
Text-conditioned diffusion models incorporate conditioning infor-
mation through cross-attention layers that relate conditioning to-
kens (e.g., text) to visual latent tokens. The resulting attention maps
capture how semantic concepts influence spatial regions during the
denoising process.
Test-time latent optimization utilizes this structure to steer gener-
ation without modifying model parameters. Instead of updating the
network weights, the latent variable is optimized during sampling
with respect to a task-specific objective. Concretely, given a target
constraint 𝐵(e.g., a target layout map), a differentiable loss is defined
over model activations (e.g., cross-attention maps), and gradients
are backpropagated to the latent variable:

Latent Space Jam
Enforcing layout adherence conflicts with the visual quality of a
generated video. In the early stage of generating the layout-guided
video, more complex motion requires stronger layout regularization,
thereby reducing visual fidelity. Our main insight is that we can
circumvent this tradeoff via a two-stage process. We first allow better
layout adherence at the cost of visual quality, and then enhance this
low-quality (but layout-accurate) guiding video.
The enhancement is achieved using existing state-of-the-art video
diffusion models [HaCohen et al. 2026; Wan et al. 2025], which can
be adapted to accept a video input, turning them into robust quality
amplifiers that can correct visually poor videos while maintaining
object locations and motion.
Figure 2 provides an overview of our pipeline. We present a two-
stage training-free layout-guided video synthesis framework that
enables explicit control in both time and space over videos generated
by a pre-trained video diffusion model, without any finetuning of
model parameters. Given a text prompt and user-specified layout
constraints in the form of bounding boxes across frames, we enforce
the model to generate a guiding video that adheres to these layout
constraints. Then, we use this guiding video as input to a video-to-
video pipeline to generate the final video.
In the following, we motivate the need for a structural prior. Then,
we analyze the limitations of explicit guide generation using en-
sembles of text-to-image models. Finally, we propose an alternative
formulation in which the guide is generated implicitly by optimizing
the latent input of a frozen text-to-video diffusion model.
4.1
On The Importance of a Guiding Prior
At the first stage of our pipeline, we generate a coarse guiding video
prior, allowing us to focus fully on layout adherence.
Figure 3 shows such a guiding video and its corresponding out-
put, demonstrating the ability of existing video diffusion models to
produce high quality video that preserves the intended layout from
a low fidelity guide. This shows that the guide itself does not need
to be visually detailed, but should adhere as closely as possible to
the intended layout.
Following the first stage, we adapt the second stage into a video-
to-video pipeline by injecting the prior at an early diffusion time-
step. At early time-steps, the diffusion process primarily governs
the global layout, while later steps refine visual details [Wang and
Vastola 2023]. Specifically, similar to [Meng et al. 2022], we inject a
noisy version of the video prior at a selected time-step, matching its
noise level to the step, and continue denoising to generate the final
video with restored visual fidelity. Technical details are provided in
the supplemental.
This raises the question of how such a guiding video prior should
be constructed. In particular, is it enough to provide unrelated frames
with weak spatiotemporal coherence? We explore this question next.
4.2
Are Text-to-Image Models Suitable for Guidance?
A straightforward strategy to generate the low-fidelity layout prior
is to leverage the strong semantic and compositional capabilities of
recent text-to-image (T2I) models. Since the guide is only required to
encode coarse spatial layout and approximate object motion, rather
than high visual quality, it is natural to generate a sequence of im-
ages that satisfy the bounding-box constraints on a frame-by-frame
basis and treat this sequence as a guiding video. This can be imple-
mented by applying layout-guided and inpainting techniques [Labs
2024; Li et al. 2023] to independently generate a static background
image and the objects specified in the text prompt, and then plac-
ing and propagating these objects across frames according to the
provided layout constraints.
As explained in section 5.1 and table 1, this simple baseline (T2I)
surprisingly matches or outperforms previous layout-guided video
generation methods [Jain et al. 2024; Ma et al. 2024; Qiu et al. 2024]
on refined videos, achieving comparable or better performance
across all datasets in overlap accuracy (IoU, AP50) and trajectory
accuracy (CD, I., I-AP50).
However, this baseline has fundamental limitations. Each frame
is generated independently, with objects created separately before
being inserted into the scene. While this guarantees correct spatial
placement, it does not preserve stable object structure or realistic
interactions among objects and their environment. Figure 6 shows a
recurring failure mode of this method; further results are provided
in the supplemental.
4.3
Utilizing Video Models for Better Guidance
Instead of relying on external text-to-image models to generate
a prior, we leverage the video diffusion model itself to produce a
layout-aware guidance signal. Specifically, we exploit the powerful
generative capacity of video diffusion models to synthesize a guid-
ing video that exhibits coherent motion while respecting specified
layout constraints.
To enforce spatial correspondence between the generated video
and the target layout, we take inspiration from attention-based
image editing methods [Chefer et al. 2023; Xie et al. 2023]. At a high
level, we control the output of a video diffusion model by steering
its cross-attention maps towards the provided layout.
Given that the model’s intermediate cross-attention maps exhibit
strong spatial correspondence with the final video, optimizing the
latent inputs to minimize a loss between these maps and the target
layout compels the generated output to follow the intended arrange-
ment. By contrast, attempting to control the layout only via text
fails. We demonstrate this in Figure 4.
This optimization prioritizes structural alignment over visual
quality. Stronger attention constraints enforce the target layout
more accurately, but degrade appearance. As stated earlier, a coarse
video is sufficient to guide generation when injected at early diffu-
sion steps. This allows us to optimize aggressively for layout in this
stage and rely on a second diffusion pass to restore visual fidelity.
We therefore obtain a controllable tradeoff between structure and
appearance, which we demonstrate in Figure 5 and discuss further
in the Results section.

Results
In this section, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our method. The
main focus of our technique it to generate videos according to the
prompt and spatiotemporal layouts provided by the user.
We compare our method to the T2I-based method proposed above
and to Peekaboo [Jain et al. 2024], TrailBlazer [Ma et al. 2024], and
FreeTraj [Qiu et al. 2024], prior training-free methods that generate
videos using layout guidance.
For fair comparison of the guide generation stage, in addition to
the provided benchmarks we use their outputs as guiding videos
to the second stage of our method, and inject these outputs to a
video diffusion model (see the refined section of Table 1, right).
Furthermore, we implement TrailBlazer [Ma et al. 2024] on Wan 2.2,
the backbone that we use in our method, as it achieved the strongest
benchmark performance overall and is the only benchmark that
supports multi-object settings.
5.0.1
Datasets. To ensure a comprehensive evaluation across vary-
ing levels of difficulty, we consider three data categories: Static,
Dynamic, and Multi. These contain approximately 40, 40, and 20
layouts, respectively, for a total of about 100 layouts. The Static set
contains single-object layouts with limited motion, initially based on
videos from the Davis dataset [Pont-Tuset et al. 2017]. The Dynamic
set follows the same single-object structure with similar textual
prompts as the Static set, but introduces strong dynamic motion.
The Multi set represents the most challenging scenario, featuring
multiple objects with strong dynamic motion in a single video and
custom textual prompts. For all datasets, layout guidance is provided
in the form of binary video masks, which are created by the authors
and to be publicly released alongside the textual prompts.
5.1
Quantitative Results
We present results in Table 1, additional results are in the supplemen-
tal. To evaluate the control of the layout on the generated output,
we use a set of complementary metrics that measure both semantic
and spatial accuracy between the ground truth layout and bound-
ing boxes predicted by an open-vocabulary segmentation model
[Minderer et al. 2022] . Intersection over Union (IoU) measures the
average overlap between predicted and ground-truth object masks
across all objects and frames, providing an overall layout accuracy
score. Average precision@50% (AP50) evaluates frame-level correct-
ness by considering a prediction correct when it overlaps the ground
truth by at least 50%, indicating whether objects appear in the in-
tended regions per frame. To evaluate motion fidelity with respect
to the target trajectory beyond standard overlap accuracy, we also
report Centroid Distance (CD), Intersection, and Intersection-AP50
(I-AP50). CD measures how far the predicted object centers are from
the ground-truth centers across all objects and frames. Intersection
measures the raw overlapping area between predicted and ground-
truth masks, and I-AP50 applies the detection-style metric based on
the raw intersection area rather than IoU. Together, these metrics
quantify how faithfully the generated videos follow the provided
layouts. We employ Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) [Unterthiner et al.
2018] to assess video quality against a set of 150 videos from Davis
6


[Pont-Tuset et al. 2017], and CLIP Similarity (ClipSim) [Radford et al.
2021] to measure alignment with the textual prompts.
As shown in Table 1, our method strongly outperforms other
approaches in terms of alignment with the input and video qual-
ity across all datasets and metrics. Wan 2.2 performs better in the
guide stage, whereas LTX-2 excels after refinement, likely due to
its dedicated refinement model. Notably, even when implemented
on the same backbone (Wan 2.2) for a fair comparison, TrailBlazer
continues to underperform our method across all stages, datasets
and metrics.
5.2
Qualitative Results
Figure 7 demonstrate that our method outperforms prior approaches
in terms of both layout alignment and overall video quality. As
stated earlier, during the guide generation stage we enforce strict
layout adherence, which comes at the expense of visual fidelity, this
quality is later recovered during the refinement stage. The strength
of the optimization at the guide generation stage can be controlled
by adjusting the learning rate. Figures 3 and 5 illustrate the visual
difference between the intermediate video guide and the final refined
output. This observation is further supported by the quantitative
performance gap between stages reported in Tables 1, 3 and 4.
In our implementation of TrailBlazer on Wan 2.2, as well as prior
benchmarks on earlier models, directly manipulating cross-attention
maps leads to either artifacts or weak layout control, and these
artifacts are often not recoverable during refinement (see Figure 7,
supplemental). This further supports our claim that optimization-
based guidance, together with a refinement stage, is necessary.
Finally, we conduct a user study comparing our method with
previous approaches on a subset of fifteen videos (five from each
dataset). The study involves approximately sixty reviewers, includ-
ing individuals with visual arts backgrounds as well as participants
from non-visual backgrounds. In total, we collected 250 responses.
Participants evaluated the generated videos along three dimensions:
fidelity to the prompt, fidelity to the layout, and aesthetic quality.
The aggregated results are shown in Table 2. We observe that the
straightforward Text-to-Video (T2V) method achieves strong visual
quality (VQ) and prompt adherence, but performs poorly in adher-
ing to layout guidance. Our approach significantly outperforms all
baselines in layout adherence. In terms of VQ and prompt adherence,
it performs competitively, surpassing all other methods except T2V.
Notably, on certain datasets, our method also exceeds T2V in visual
quality or prompt alignment.
5.3
Ablation Analysis
We conduct a series of ablation studies to better understand the
design choices in our method. In particular, we analyze (i) which
layers are most influential for layout control generation, (ii) how
the number of optimized layers impacts output quality, and (iii) how
the resolution used at different stages affects the trade-off between
inference time and performance.
In Table 3 we study which layers to select and how many are
needed. Instead of fixing a single subset, we randomly sample a
subset from the chosen layer range at each diffusion timestep. This
strategy benefits from representations across the full layer set while
reducing computational cost for high-resolution inputs. Our results
indicate that early layers are especially beneficial. Specifically, dur-
ing the guide generation stage (left columns block), we observe
higher IoU and AP50 and lower CD for early layer ranges (0–10,
10–20, 0–20) compared to later ranges (20–30, 30–40, 20–40). This
advantage consistently propagates to the refinement stage (right
columns block).
Table 4 shows the benefit of generating the refined video at a
higher resolution, but inference time is greatly reduced when the
guide is generated at lower resolutions with only a slight drop in
performance. Due to its high compression rate, LTX achieves signifi-
cantly faster inference times. Wan attains the strongest performance
during the guide stage, while LTX achieves higher IoU and AP50
scores after refinement, along with comparable performance on
trajectory-related metrics (CD, Intersection, and I-AP50).
For all results in the paper, we adopt the setting with three random
layers selected from layers 0 −10 (see Table 3, first row). We use the
lowest guide resolution (160×49) and the highest refined resolutions
(480 × 81, 768 × 121) from Table 4 (rows 6 and 13), as they yield the
best overlap and trajectory accuracy in the refined videos. Due to
paper format limitations, we refer the reader to the supplemental
for video demonstrations and further experiments.
5.4
Additional Applications
We show more uses of our method in Figure 8. By generating the
prior from an existing video, we can edit its attributes. Applying
optimization to style-related parts in the prompt, produces different
styles in the same video. More examples and details are in the
supplemental.
6
Limitations
Despite its strong performance, the approach has several limitations.
It does not explicitly separate camera motion from object motion:
bounding boxes define where content should appear over time, but
they do not specify whether changes should arise from object move-
ment or from camera transformations, which can lead to ambiguous
or unintended behavior.
Performance may decrease in complex situations, such as scenes
with detailed motion, multiple interacting objects, or crowded scene
layouts. Although we demonstrate results in such settings, they
remain challenging and leave room for further improvement. An im-
portant direction for future work will be to address these challenges,
which may lead to more robust layout-guided video generation.
7
Conclusion
We present a training-free approach for layout-guided text-to-video
generation that enables explicit control over spatial composition and
object motion while preserving the generative strength of modern
video diffusion models.
Our results show that minimal intervention at inference time
is sufficient to steer pre-trained models toward user-specified spa-
tiotemporal layouts without compromising visual fidelity or requir-
ing additional training. Through extensive quantitative and qual-
itative evaluation, we demonstrate consistent improvements over
prior methods across static, dynamic, and multi-object scenarios.
