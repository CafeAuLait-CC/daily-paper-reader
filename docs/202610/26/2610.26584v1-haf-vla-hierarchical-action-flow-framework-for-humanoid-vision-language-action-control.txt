Abstract: Humanoid robots hold great promise as general-purpose agents in human-centered environments, yet adapting generalist vision-language-action (VLA) policies to real-world humanoid loco-manipulation remains challenging. Unlike fixed-base or wheeled manipulators, humanoids must coordinate locomo-tion, posture, gaze, and dual-arm manipulation over a high-dimensional whole-body action space, where inaccurate base or waist motions can easily induce unstable and aggressive upper-body compensation. We propose HAF-VLA, a hierarchical action-flow framework for humanoid VLA control. Built upon a pretrained flow-matching VLA, HAF-VLA inherits the vision-language prefix encoder and action expert weights, and adapts them to humanoid whole-body loco-manipulation with learnable stage embeddings and cross-stage KV-cache conditioning. Instead of generating the full action chunk in a single pass, HAF-VLA progressively denoises a shared whole-body action space: first locomotion and head commands, then waist refinement, and finally full-body manipulation. To preserve structural dependen-cies across stages, we introduce a cross-stage KV cache conditioning mechanism, enabling subsequent action generation stages to refine and augment their outputs based on the action representations from preceding stages. Experimental results across six real-world tasks demonstrate that our framework significantly enhances the performance of existing models in humanoid loco-manipulation tasks, demon-strating an effective pathway for adapting strong mobile-manipulation VLAs to humanoid whole-body loco-manipulation. 

Keywords: Humanoid Robot, Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, Imitation Learning 

## 1 Introduction 

Humanoid robots hold the promise of becoming general-purpose agents capable of operating in human-centered environments. Unlike fixed-base or wheeled robots, humanoids must coordinate locomotion, waist motion, gaze, and dual-arm manipulation to navigate large spaces, adjust posture, maintain balance, and physically interact with the environment [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. This whole-body nature makes humanoid loco-manipulation fundamentally different from conventional manipulation: inaccurate base or waist motions can force the upper body to produce compensatory movements, degrading precision, stability, and energy efficiency. Recent vision-language-action models and world-action models have achieved strong results on fixed-base, dual-arm, and wheeled mobile manipulators [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. However, most of them are not explicitly designed for the structured action space and unstable bipedal dynamics of

Directly applying a single-stage action generator to humanoid loco-manipulation may entangle locomotion, posture, and manipulation in an unstructured manner, making the policy prone to unstable base motions or aggressive upper-body compensation [5, 6, 15, 16]. Therefore, humanoid VLA control requires an action-generation structure that respects the kinematic dependencies among the legs, waist, head, and arms while preserving the semantic reasoning ability of pretrained VLA models. To this end, we propose HAF-VLA, a hierarchical action-flow framework for humanoid vision-language-action control. Built upon a pretrained flow-matching VLA [10], HAF-VLA reuses its vision-language prefix encoder [17] and action expert weights, and adapts them to humanoid whole-body loco-manipulation through progressive action generation. Instead of synthesizing the full action chunk in a single pass, HAF-VLA first generates locomotion and head commands, then incorporates waist refinement, and finally produces full-body manipulation actions. This design encourages locomotion-related motions to be planned before manipulation, reducing the tendency of the upper body to compensate for inaccurate base or waist movements [18, 19]. We further introduce cross-stage KV-cache [20] conditioning to propagate structured action informa-tion across the hierarchy (see Fig. 1(b)). After each non-final stage completes flow denoising [21], its clean action prediction is re-encoded by the shared action expert to form an action KV cache, which conditions the following stage. In this way, later stages refine and augment their actions based on earlier motion plans, while all stages remain grounded in the same visual-language prefix representation. We evaluate HAF-VLA on two full-sized humanoid platforms, Tian Gong 3.0 and Tian Gong 2.0, across six real-world loco-manipulation tasks spanning household and industrial scenarios (see Fig. 1(a)). HAF-VLA consistently improves normalized task scores over imitation-learning, VLA, and world-model baselines (see Fig. 1(c)), especially on tasks requiring long-distance locomotion, precise body positioning, and coordinated whole-body manipulation. Our contributions are: (i) a hierarchical action-flow formulation for humanoid VLA loco-manipulation over a shared whole-body 2action space; (ii) a cross-stage KV-cache conditioning mechanism for progressive whole-body action generation; and (iii) real-world validation showing that this structure effectively adapts pretrained mobile-manipulation VLAs to humanoid loco-manipulation. 

## 2 Related Work 

Vision-language-action models. Vision-language-action models (VLAs) aim to map visual observa-tions and language instructions directly to robot actions [22, 23, 24, 25]. Representative works such as RT-2 [26] and OpenVLA [23] show that vision-language pretraining can be effectively transferred to robotic manipulation, while π0 and π0.5 further move toward continuous generative action modeling with flow matching [9, 10]. However, most existing VLAs still focus on tabletop tasks or mobile manipulators, leaving high-dimensional humanoid whole-body action generation underexplored. 

Humanoid VLAs. Recent works have begun to extend VLAs to humanoid robots [27, 28, 29, 30]. GR00T N1.7 [11] studies general-purpose humanoid action generation from vision-language inputs, while WholeBodyVLA [8] explores latent VLA learning for large-space humanoid mobile manipulation. Recently, Ψ0 [31] introduced an open humanoid foundation model pretrained from egocentric human videos. In contrast to methods that rely on latent skills, cross-embodiment transfer, or learning from human videos, our method is directly built upon a pretrained generalist VLA and improves its performance on humanoid by generating structured whole-body actions grounded in the humanoid forward-kinematic chain, sparing the computational cost of training a new model. 

Whole-body manipulation. Recent advances in humanoid whole-body control have greatly expanded the motion repertoire of legged humanoids [6, 32, 33, 34]. With improved reinforcement learning and low-level control techniques, humanoid robots can now perform highly dynamic behaviors. Nevertheless, extending these capabilities to generalizable loco-manipulation remains substantially more challenging. Works such as TWIST2 [35] and AMO [36] demonstrate the potential of VR-based teleoperation for whole-body humanoid control and data collection. However, their focus is mainly on teleoperation interfaces and low-level control pipelines, rather than learning a generalizable policy for long-horizon loco-manipulation. 

## 3 HAF-VLA 

HAF-VLA is built as a structured adaptation of a pretrained action-flow VLA. Specifically, we build upon π0.5 [10], inheriting its pretrained vision-language prefix encoder and action expert weights. We further introduce learnable stage embeddings and cross-stage KV-cache conditioning, transforming the original single-stage action generator into a hierarchical whole-body action-flow model. This design provides a simple and effective way to adapt a strong generalist mobile-manipulation VLA to high-dimensional humanoid whole-body loco-manipulation without training the full model from scratch. The detailed framework for HAF-VLA is illustrated in Fig. 2.

3.2 Hierarchical Action Flow 

Instead of generating the complete action chunk in the full action space at once, HAF-VLA de-composes action generation into three cumulative stages. Let the effective action dimension set be 

D = Dmove ∪ D skill ∪ D head ∪ D waist ∪ D arm ∪ D hand . (3) We define the active action dimensions of the three stages as 

D1 = Dmove ∪ D skill ∪ D head ,

D2 = D1 ∪ D waist ,

D3 = D2 ∪ D arm ∪ D hand = D.

(4) Stage 1 predicts locomotion, skill mode, and head commands, establishing the coarse motion and gaze direction. Stage 2 additionally activates waist commands, allowing the model to refine the body posture and upper-body workspace conditioned on the Stage 1 motion plan. Stage 3 activates the full action space, including arms and hands, and produces the final whole-body action chunk for execution. This progressive design encourages the model to first stabilize locomotion-related motions before generating manipulation actions.

For non-final stages, the cache used by the following stage is generated from the ground-truth masked action chunk rather than from the model prediction. This stabilizes optimization while preserving the same sequential dependency structure used at inference time. During inference, the model executes the three stages sequentially. We deploy HAF-VLA with a receding-horizon execution scheme. The robot executes the first 40 steps before the next inference call. In our real-robot deployment, the complete three-stage inference takes approximately 0.12 seconds on a single RTX 5090 GPU, supporting real-time closed-loop humanoid loco-manipulation.

4.3 Q2: Which design choices contribute to the performance of HAF-VLA? 

We conduct controlled ablations on the Box Carry task shown in Fig. 4. Unless otherwise specified, HAF-VLA uses three cumulative stages—locomotion/head, waist, and arms/hands—with 10 flow-matching denoising steps per stage. All variants are trained on the same 120 demonstrations and evaluated using the normalized task score defined in Section 4.1. Results are summarized in Table 2. 

Stage design. We first examine the stage order by rearranging the action groups while preserving the three-stage structure. The proposed locomotion →waist →arms hierarchy achieves 86.7%, outper-forming Waist-First (70.0%) and Arm-First (60.0%). To account for unequal supervision frequencies across stages, Loss-Calibrated reweights the losses of locomotion/head, waist, and arms/hands by 

1/3, 1/2, and 1, respectively, achieving 76.7%. These results support the proposed stage ordering beyond differences in supervision frequency. We further evaluate generation without body-part-specific staging. All-Joint , which activates the full action space at every stage, achieves 73.3%. To control for the total denoising budget, we compare HAF-VLA with single-stage π0.5 using 30 denoising steps. Despite the same total number of steps, 

π0.5 achieves only 30.0%, compared with 86.7% for HAF-VLA, demonstrating that the improvement cannot be attributed to additional denoising steps alone. Notably, increasing π0.5 from 10 to 30 steps reduces its score from 70.0% to 30.0%, potentially due in part to increased inference latency (0.075 s to 0.115 s). In contrast, HAF-VLA maintains strong performance despite a comparable latency of 0.123 s, highlighting the benefit of hierarchical action generation.

## 5 Limitations and Conclusion 

We present HAF-VLA, a hierarchical action-flow framework for adapting pretrained VLAs to humanoid whole-body loco-manipulation. By progressively denoising a shared action space and propagating intermediate motion plans through cross-stage KV-cache conditioning, HAF-VLA provides structured whole-body action generation for humanoid loco-manipulation. However, the hierarchical generation process increases the total denoising budget compared with a single-stage policy, which may introduce additional deployment latency. Moreover, our method still depends on the semantic reasoning and action priors of the underlying foundation model, and may fail when these priors are insufficient for unseen tasks or environments. Future work will explore more efficient denoising and deployment strategies to improve real-time performance, while further strengthening the foundation model adaptation for broader humanoid tasks. 9Acknowledgments

## S2 Implementation Details 

S2.1 HAF-VLA Implementation Details 

HAF-VLA is implemented on top of the pretrained π0.5 model following the official openpi code-base [10]. We initialize the vision-language prefix encoder and the action expert from the pretrained 

π0.5 checkpoint, and add learnable stage embeddings, stage-wise action masks, cross-stage KV-cache conditioning, and the action input/output projection layers for hierarchical whole-body action gen-eration. The action horizon is set to 100. During inference, HAF-VLA performs three sequential denoising stages with 10 flow-matching steps per stage. The final action chunk is taken from Stage 3, and the robot executes the first 40 steps before the next inference call. For parameter-efficient adaptation, we use the gemma_2b_lora variant in openpi and apply LoRA [40] to the PaliGemma [17] visual language backbone. The LoRA rank is 16, the LoRA alpha is 16.0, and the resulting scaling factor is 1.0. We do not use LoRA dropout. Following the official implementation, LoRA is applied to both attention and feed-forward layers, including the query projection, key-value projection, output projection, gated/up projection, and down projection. The original weights of the PaliGemma language backbone are frozen except for the LoRA parameters. The action expert uses the gemma_300m variant and remains trainable. The newly introduced hierarchy-specific parameters, including stage embeddings, action input/output projections, and time MLP layers, are also trainable. The vision encoder is not frozen in our current configuration. We train HAF-VLA with AdamW [41] using β1 = 0 .9, β2 = 0 .95 , ϵ = 10 −8, weight decay 10 −10 ,and global gradient norm clipping of 1.0. The learning rate follows a cosine decay schedule with 1000 warmup steps, a peak learning rate of 2.5 × 10 −5, a decay length of 30k steps, and a final learning rate of 2.5 × 10 −6. Unless otherwise specified, the model is trained for 50k steps with a global batch size of 64. For state input, since we use the π0.5 configuration with discrete state input, the robot state is discretized and appended to the prompt tokens, with a maximum token length of 385 for Tian Gong 3.0 and 192 for Tian Gong 2.0. 

S2.2 Baseline Implementation Details 

For all baseline methods, we use the same teleoperated training data, humanoid action interface, and robot-specific deployment wrapper as HAF-VLA. The predicted action chunks are mapped to the same low-level locomotion, waist, head, arm, and hand control interfaces for fair comparison. 

π0.5 [10]. We use π0.5 as a strong single-stage VLA baseline. π0.5 is a large-scale general-purpose robot foundation model in which the vision-language model and the action expert share the same attention backbone. We fine-tune the full model on our humanoid demonstration data for better performance. The action horizon is set to 100, and the model is trained for 50k steps on 8 NVIDIA 16 A100 GPUs with a per-GPU batch size of 8. During deployment, we execute the first 40 steps of each predicted action chunk before the next inference call. 

ACT [39]. ACT is used as a compact imitation-learning baseline with a Transformer encoder-decoder architecture and action chunking. We set the action horizon to 200 and train the model for 37k steps on 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with a per-GPU batch size of 64. During deployment, the model outputs action chunks with temporal aggregation over a 60-step window, which smooths the final commands sent to the robot. 

GR00T N1.7 [11]. GR00T N1.7 is used as a humanoid foundation-model baseline. It predicts continuous action sequences with a vision-language backbone and a diffusion Transformer action head. We set the action horizon to 100 and fine-tune the model for 20k steps on 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with a global batch size of 160. During deployment, we use an action chunk size of 50, which achieves the best empirical performance in our experiments. 

Cosmos Policy [12]. Cosmos Policy is used as a world-model-based visuomotor-control baseline. It is built on the Cosmos framework and uses the Cosmos-Predict2-2B backbone together with a diffusion Transformer action head to predict continuous robot action sequences. We set the action horizon to 50 and train the policy for 15k steps on 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with a global batch size of 160. During deployment, we use an action chunk size of 40, which gives the best empirical performance in our experiments.

Denoising-budget control. To distinguish hierarchical generation from simply increasing the denoising budget, we compare the proposed model, which performs 10 Euler denoising steps at each of its three stages, with single-stage π0.5 using 30 steps. The latter generates the complete action chunk through a single denoising trajectory without intermediate action-stage conditioning. This comparison matches the total number of denoising updates but not necessarily the computational cost, since the hierarchical model additionally constructs cross-stage action caches and uses different attention-context lengths across stages. 

No-Cache-Train. We disable cross-stage action-cache construction and conditioning during both training and inference. Each stage conditions only on the shared vision-language cache Cvl and its own current action tokens. The stage embeddings, cumulative action masks, zero-target supervision, and stage-wise losses are unchanged. During inference, the stages use independent noise initializations, and the final Stage 3 prediction provides the executed action chunk. 

No-Cache-Deploy. We retain the normally trained checkpoint but disable attention to preceding action-stage caches during inference. Specifically, the attention mask prevents Stage 2 from attending to C1 and Stage 3 from attending to C1 and C2. The cached tensors, sequence lengths, and positional indices are preserved, while the vision-language cache and current-stage tokens remain accessible. Thus, this variant removes access to preceding action representations without changing the attention-token layout. 18 Zero-Cache-Deploy. Using the same normally trained checkpoint, we instead replace the keys and values of all preceding action-stage cache tokens with zeros at every transformer layer. The vision-language cache remains unchanged. Unlike No-Cache-Deploy , the zeroed action-cache tokens remain visible to attention and participate in softmax normalization. The two variants therefore introduce distinct inference-time disruptions to cross-stage conditioning.

## S3 Detailed Task Description 

Evaluation protocol. We evaluate HAF-VLA and the baseline methods on six real-world humanoid loco-manipulation tasks in the main benchmark (Q1). An additional task, Box Carry , is used for the controlled ablation studies in Q2. All seven tasks follow the same milestone-based evaluation protocol. Each rollout starts from a predefined region that matches the distribution used during data collection. For fair comparison, different methods are evaluated using the same set of initial configurations whenever possible. Since these tasks involve long-horizon execution and multiple intermediate objectives, we use milestone-based normalized task scores instead of binary success rates. Each task has a maximum score of 15, and the final normalized score is computed as the achieved milestone score divided by the maximum score. 19 Language instructions. For each task, the policy receives a task-level language instruction together with egocentric visual observations and proprioceptive robot states. The language instructions used in our experiments are: • Laundry Loading : “Pick up the clothes on the bed, walk to the washing machine, and put the clothes into the washing machine.” • Clothes Retrieval : “Pick up the clothes from the drying rod, walk to the wardrobe and hang the clothes in the wardrobe.” • Table Tidy : “Walk to the table, close the laptop, grasp the bottle, and throw it into the trash bin.” • Plant Watering : “Walk to the shelf, lift up the flower pot with the right hand, and water the flowers.” • Box Transfer : “Carry the box, turn around, walk to the table, and put it down.” • Ball Tossing : “Grasp the ball, walk to the table, and drop the ball into the box.” • Box Carry : “Carry the box to the table.” 

Laundry Loading. In the Laundry Loading task, the robot starts near the bed, reaches toward scattered clothes, grasps the clothes from the bed surface, walks to the washing machine, places the clothes into the washing machine, and finally closes the washing-machine lid. During data collection, the robot starts from three positions around the bed: approximately 30 cm to the left of the center, at the center, and approximately 30 cm to the right of the center. This task requires the robot to combine flexible-object grasping, spatial navigation between the bed and the washing machine, and final object placement into a constrained container. The major challenge comes from the deformable nature of clothes: their shape, contact points, and grasp affordances vary significantly across rollouts, making robust grasping and stable carrying difficult. The robot must also accurately align with the washing-machine opening before releasing the clothes. The maximum score is 15: placing one piece of clothing into the washing machine gives 5 points, placing two pieces gives 10 points, and successfully closing the lid gives an additional 5 points. 

Clothes Retrieval. In the Clothes Retrieval task, the robot approaches a drying rod, reaches upward to remove a piece of clothing, carries it to the wardrobe, hangs or places it inside the wardrobe, steps backward, and closes the wardrobe door. During data collection, the clothing is placed at four uniformly spaced positions along the drying rod, covering different relative positions between the robot and the target clothing. This task requires high-position object interaction, dual-arm coordination, and precise whole-body positioning. A key challenge is that the robot must stand at an appropriate distance from the drying rod and the wardrobe: if it stops too far away, the arms must overextend; if it stops too close, the arms and torso may collide with the environment. The task also requires the robot to coordinate backward motion and arm movement when closing the wardrobe door. The maximum score is 15: successfully removing the clothes from the drying rod gives 5 points, placing or hanging the clothes in the wardrobe gives 5 points, and closing the wardrobe door gives an additional 5 points. 

Table Tidy. In the Table Tidy task, the robot walks to a table, approaches the laptop, closes the laptop lid, identifies and grasps a bottle or piece of trash on the tabletop, and throws it into the trash bin. During data collection, the robot starts from six positions in front of the table: two rows at different distances from the table, with three lateral offsets in each row, approximately 15 cm left, centered, and 15 cm right. This task contains multiple heterogeneous subgoals within a single rollout. The robot must first perform a contact-rich manipulation action to close the laptop, then switch to grasping and throwing another object. The main challenges are precise tabletop alignment, object-level spatial understanding, and robust transition between different manipulation modes. Small base-position errors can make the laptop-closing action miss the lid or cause the trash-grasping motion to be misaligned. The maximum score is 15: closing the laptop gives 5 points, successfully grasping the trash gives 5 points, and throwing it into the trash bin gives an additional 5 points. 20 Plant Watering. In the Plant Watering task, the robot moves toward a shelf with plants at different heights, lifts and stabilizes the flower pot or watering container with the right hand, waters the upper plant, changes its body posture through kneeling or lowering the waist, and then waters the lower plant. During data collection, the initial robot position is randomly perturbed within a small range. This task emphasizes whole-body posture adaptation. The robot must coordinate locomotion, waist motion, arm motion, and skill-mode control to reach plants at different heights. The main challenge is that the robot cannot solve the task using arm motion alone: it must adjust its body height and posture to reach the lower target while maintaining balance and keeping the container oriented correctly. The maximum score is 15: successfully watering the upper plant gives 5 points, successfully entering the kneeling or lowered-body posture gives 5 points, and successfully watering the lower plant gives an additional 5 points. 

Box Transfer. In the Box Transfer task, the robot grasps a box from one table, lifts and carries it, turns around, walks to another table, re-aligns its body with the target table, and places the box down. During data collection, the relative positions of the robot and the source table are perturbed within a small range. This task requires stable object carrying during locomotion and accurate final placement after navigation. The main challenge is that the robot must maintain the box pose while walking and turning; otherwise, the box may slip, tilt, or collide with the robot body. After reaching the target table, the robot must also re-localize the placement surface and adjust its arm trajectory accordingly. The maximum score is 15: successfully lifting the box gives 5 points, moving to the target table while carrying the box gives 5 points, and successfully placing the box on the target table gives an additional 5 points. 

Ball Tossing. In the Ball Tossing task, the robot reaches toward a ball on the table, grasps it, moves toward the target box, aligns its body and arm with the box, and releases the ball into the box. During data collection, the ball is placed at nine annotated positions on the source table, and the target box is also placed at nine annotated positions; the paired ball and box positions are collected sequentially to cover different grasping and releasing geometries. This task is particularly sensitive to final-stage execution accuracy. In addition to grasping and locomotion, the robot must control the release timing, wrist pose, and body alignment so that the ball enters the box. Even small errors in base position or release direction may cause the ball to miss the box. The maximum score is 15: successfully grasping the ball gives 5 points; if the released ball does not enter the box but touches the box wall, the rollout receives 5 points for partial release accuracy; if the ball successfully enters the box, the release stage receives 10 points. 

Box Carry. In the Box Carry task, the robot picks up a box from a bed, carries it while walking toward a round table, and places it on the tabletop. This task evaluates the robot’s ability to coordinate whole-body locomotion and manipulation while transporting an object between different surfaces. The robot must maintain a stable grasp and keep the box balanced throughout locomotion, avoiding unintended contact with its surroundings. Upon approaching the target table, it must stop safely without collisions and adjust its body posture to prepare for placement. The main challenge lies in maintaining object stability during locomotion while achieving precise placement on the relatively small, circular tabletop. The maximum score is 15: successfully lifting the box earns 5 points, reaching the target table and stopping without collisions earns 5 points, and placing the box entirely within the tabletop boundary earns an additional 5 points.
