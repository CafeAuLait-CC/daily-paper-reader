Abstract
Co-speech motion generation remains challenging over long horizons due to the
need for temporal coherence and consistent style. Existing methods typically gener-
ate long motions segment by segment, either conditioning on a short seed of recent
frames, which gradually drifts, or retaining ever-longer explicit histories, which are
costly to maintain and hard to learn from limited data. We trace these limitations
to a key asymmetry between past and present: past motion carries slowly varying
style that should be compactly summarized, whereas the current segment exhibits
fast-changing local dynamics that require fine-grained modeling. This asymmetry
motivates a simple Past-As-State, Present-as-Attention principle for long-horizon

Introduction
Humans naturally accompany speech with expressive body movements that convey emphasis, emotion,
and communicative intent beyond words alone [53]. Co-speech motion generation aims to synthesize
realistic full-body motions that are temporally aligned and semantically consistent with speech [56]. It
is crucial to applications like digital humans [99], virtual assistants [87], and animated characters [24].
Early co-speech motion generation often relies on direct speech-to-motion mapping [86], but the
inherent one-to-many nature of speech-to-motion correspondence has made generative modeling
increasingly important [38]. Accordingly, diffusion models [27] and flow matching [42, 50], the
prevailing generative paradigms, have been widely adopted in this field.2 While they improve short-
horizon realism and controllability [48, 92], long-horizon co-speech motion generation remains
challenging, as it requires preserving temporal coherence [75] and speaker style [1] (see Fig. 1).
For long-horizon generation, most methods proceed segment by segment, making inter-block history
propagation the central challenge. A common strategy is to use only a seed (i.e., the previous
segment’s last few frames; Fig. 2a) [45, 89, 92] as condition. With such limited history, temporal
coherence and style gradually drift over time (see Fig. 1a) [75]. Recent streaming methods exploit
longer histories [8, 75, 77], but retain contexts like frames or tokens (see Fig. 2b). Maintaining such
explicit histories incurs growing memory and compute costs as horizons extend, especially with
attention-based backbones [74]. More critically, learning to exploit ever longer explicit histories is
difficult given the limited data in co-speech motion generation [45], often leading to unstable style
preservation (see Fig. 1b). These observations raise a fundamental question: is there a more effective
way to represent and propagate the past than explicitly attending to raw historical sequences?
We answer this affirmatively: the past and present play distinct roles in motion generation and should
be represented accordingly. Past history primarily carries slowly varying stylistic traits (e.g., gesture
amplitude and spatial preference) [1, 3], which should be compactly summarized rather than stored
verbatim. By contrast, local motion dynamics are fast-changing, making full-resolution modeling of
the current block more suitable for capturing fine-grained motion details. This asymmetry motivates
a simple Past-As-State, Present-as-Attention principle: the past should be compressed as a persistent
state that is carried across segments, while the present should be modeled with full attention for
expressive generation (see Fig. 2c). Guided by this principle, we propose PASPA, which achieves
temporally coherent and style-consistent long-horizon co-speech motion generation (see Fig. 1c).
In PASPA, past history and the current block are handled by two complementary mechanisms. For
past history, we design a block-level recurrent state mechanism built upon Gated DeltaNet [83, 84],
which progressively summarizes preceding blocks into a persistent state and provides a selective-
retention, temporal-decay inductive bias for slowly evolving motion style. For the current block, we
use blockwise self-attention [60, 74] to enable full bidirectional interaction for fine-grained modeling.
We integrate them into a hybrid PASPA module, which forms the core component of our model.
We build PASPA on a semi-autoregressive blockwise flow matching (BFM) framework inspired by
recent block diffusion paradigms [6, 95], and develop tailored training and inference protocols. For
training, we apply distinct noise levels to multiple blocks and supervise their denoising in parallel,
yielding dense multi-block supervision and exposing each block to states summarizing variable-length
histories. For inference, PASPA separates state prefilling from iterative decoding and reuses cached
historical states for efficient rollout. To improve generation quality, we introduce a hybrid classifier-
free guidance [26] that augments audio-conditioned guidance with history-drop guidance [66],
enabling explicit control over historical dependence while preserving speech alignment.

Our main technical contributions are: (1) We identify a key asymmetry in the roles of past and present

with intra-block attention. (3) We develop tailored training and inference schemes, including multi-
block supervision, prefill-decode decoupling with state caching, and hybrid classifier-free guidance.

Related Work
Co-Speech Motion Generation.
The task synthesizes speech-aligned body movements. Its one-
to-many nature motivates generative paradigms such as diffusion models (DMs) [27, 67] and VQ-
VAE-based generation [73] for realism and diversity [2, 3, 24, 93, 98]. Further enhancements for
semantic controllability include hierarchical designs and language-based guidance [9, 41, 49, 59, 91].
VQ-VAE-based methods [45, 51, 85, 89, 90] generate motions through learned codebooks, but often
suffer from limited diversity due to discrete code constraints. DM-based methods [5, 11, 13, 54, 92]
better support diverse motion synthesis and have become a dominant paradigm in this field.
Diffusion Models and Flow Matching.
DMs [27] and Flow Matching (FM) [42, 50] have achieved
strong results in image generation [14, 17]. Advances include novel architectures [20, 58, 78],
conditional guidance [26, 32, 37], and accelerated sampling [50, 65]. They also extend to sequential
domains (e.g., video) via autoregressive (AR) factorization for variable-length rollout [6, 10, 62]. Rep-
resentative approaches include Block Diffusion [6, 95], which combines intra-block denoising with
inter-block autoregression, and Diffusion Forcing [10], which assigns independent per-token noise
levels to bridge next-token prediction [61] and full-sequence diffusion. Related advances mitigate
exposure bias [29], enable real-time generation [47], and support flexible history conditioning [66].
This AR diffusion paradigm has also been adapted to motion generation [8, 25, 77, 96].
Long-Context Sequence Modeling.
Attention [74] dominates long-context modeling but scales
quadratically with sequence length [70] and lacks inductive biases for data-limited regimes [18].
Alternatives like state space models [16, 22] and linear attention [34, 82, 83] replace pairwise attention
with linear recurrent state updates, improving efficiency and introducing stronger temporal inductive
bias. Specifically, Gated DeltaNet (GDN) [84] is highly relevant to us, as its gating and delta-rule
updates yield a temporal-decay bias that we leverage to model continuous motion dynamics. Such
long-context designs have also been adopted in motion generation (e.g., Motion Mamba [94]).

Co-Speech Motion Generation.
This task synthesizes motion x ∈RT ×C and facial expression

of length Ka (i.e., a = [a1, . . . , aN]), where an ∈RKa denotes the n-th speech segment.

3.2.1
Inter-Block Persistent State.
Why Persistent State for the Past?
Past history
carries stable style and long-range tendencies. Such
information is abstract and sparse: its effective con-
tent lies in a few persistent components, and token-
level attention would waste capacity on transient
fluctuations. Thus, the past should be selectively
condensed into a persistent state, not indiscrimi-
nately preserved. We achieve this via Gated DeltaNet
(GDN) [84], whose gating [82] and delta-rule [63, 76] updates suppress fluctuations and accumulate
informative trends, yielding a strong inductive bias for history propagation in our data-limited setting.
Blockwise Gated DeltaNet (BGDN).
GDN is token-recurrent, but our generation proceeds block
by block. We thus propose a blockwise GDN (BGDN, Fig. 4a), driven by a tailored Block-Causal
GDN kernel. Inside the BGDN module (Fig. 4 (a.1)), we take the noisy current block as input and the
encoded clean history as the context. Following GDN, we derive queries qi ∈Rdk from the input,
with keys ki ∈Rdk, values vi ∈Rdv, and gates αi, βi ∈(0, 1) from the context, omitting GDN’s
temporal mixing to prevent cross-block leakage. Within the kernel, these variables update a persistent
state Si ∈Rdv×dk and yield outputs o ∈Rdv. o are then projected to produce final outputs.

3.2.2
Intra-Block Bidirectional Attention.
Why Bidirectional Attention for the Present?
The current block carries fast-changing dynamics,
such as velocities and fine-grained articulations, which demand dense modeling of local motion details.
Correspondingly, BGDN shares a common block-start state, leaving token-to-token interactions
within the current block unmodeled. We assign this role to a dedicated bidirectional self-attention
module [74] rather than a causal one: although frames within a block follow a micro-level temporal
order, each token still benefits from both preceding and subsequent local context within the segment.

Applying bidirectional self-attention [74] across
the entire sequence naively would break the block-level semi-autoregression. We therefore propose
BMHSA, which permits bidirectional interaction within each block while strictly forbidding any
cross-block interaction. Given an input sequence X ∈RNK×d, the output is computed as follows:

3.2.3
Network Architecture.
We instantiate our PASPA layer following the DiT design [58] (see Fig. 4) with two modifications.
Structurally, we replace the self-attention sublayer with two sequential sublayers: BGDN and BMHSA.
For adaptive modulation, we replace AdaLN with AdaRMSNorm [15], where the modulation
parameters are predicted from the Fourier-embedded timestep t [69] and the speaker ID embedding.
Our architecture follows GlobalDiff [92], but uses our PASPA layer. We first use a frozen audio-
to-expression network to obtain expressions from WavLM features [12] and speaker ID. Motions
are represented in global rotation space and split into torso (with translation) and hands branches.
For each branch, the noisy stream embeds body-part motion with full noisy motion, audio features,
and expressions as tokens for the stacked PASPA layers, while the history stream encodes clean past
motion into each BGDN module (eh, et in Fig. 4). The branch outputs are merged to the full motion.

3.4
Inference for Long-Horizon Rollout
Prefill-Decode Rollout with State Caching.
For long-horizon inference, PASPA employs a prefill-
decode rollout (see Algorithm 1). Prefill only encodes the clean segment to update the BGDN
state, bypassing the denoising sublayers. Invoked after each block iteration (line 8), it integrates the
freshly denoised block into the state. Decode (lines 4-7) treats S strictly as a read-only condition.
It iteratively denoises the current block (line 7), evaluating Fθ under varying conditions for hybrid
CFG (detailed below). Throughout the rollout, the fixed-size S is the sole persistent cache, initialized
by prefilling seed xseed and aseed (or zeros) and reused across all N blocks. Unlike linearly growing
token caches [36, 74], S guarantees an O(1) query complexity per step, regardless of history length.
Hybrid Classifier-Free Guidance.
Standard CFG [26] modulates only the condition cn, leaving
historical dependence uncontrolled. Motivated by [66], we introduce a learnable null state S∅for
hybrid guidance via four predictions (Algorithm 1, lines 5-6): unconditional (S∅, ∅), condition-only
(S∅, cn), history-only (S, ∅), and full (S, cn). They are combined via a multilinear composition (Al-
gorithm 1, line 7) that incorporates marginal and second-order joint terms, empirically outperforming
simpler alternatives (see Tab. 13). To prevent distortions at noise extremes [37], scales wcond(t) and
whist(t) are time-dependent, activating only during mid-noise intervals [0.1, 0.6] (otherwise 1). To
train Hybrid CFG, we independently drop cn with probability 10% and sample S as unchanged,
null S∅, or interpolated αS + (1−α)S∅with probabilities 70%/10%/20%, where α is uniformly
sampled from [0, 1]. This interpolation enables continuous inference-time control of history strength.

Asymmetric Past-Present Modeling.
We validate the PASPA principle by varying the past and
current modeling under a fixed training recipe in Tab. 3. Removing history (history branch output
set to zero) substantially degrades FGD, confirming the necessity of long-range context. Increasing
token-attention history from Attn8 to Attnall improves FGD, but still underperforms PASPA, showing
that retaining more raw history does not guarantee effective history utilization. Moreover, replacing
current-block attention with state-based recurrent modeling (i.e., State+State) yields worse FGD
than even the no-history variant. This shows that the current block benefits from dense bidirectional
interaction and that state-based current-block modeling hurts generation quality. These comparisons
validate PASPA’s past-as-state, present-as-attention division, which yields the best FGD.
Blockwise Training Formulation.
We show the build-up ablation of our training recipe in Tab. 4.
Naively switching from single-block (Single) to multi-block (Multi) supervision degrades FGD
despite denser supervision, due to unordered block timesteps and imbalanced losses across blocks and
noise levels. Weakly monotonic timesteps (Mono) and adaptive loss weighting (AdaW; see Eq. (8))
address these two issues, significantly reducing FGD to 5.614. Beyond the two critical stabilizers,
SG (i.e., sg(·) in Eq. (8)) reduces FGD to 5.216, and LogitN (i.e., logit-normal time sampling [20])
brings it to 4.450. We use the optimal LogitN parameters in Tab. 4; see Tab. 9 for the full sweep.
Classifier-Free Guidance.
Table 5 evaluates the CFG build-up, including training dropout and
inference guidance. Condition dropout, acting as data augmentation, already reduces FGD from 4.450
to 3.937, and condition guidance further improves it to 3.883. However, history dropout without
guidance slightly degrades FGD, emphasizing that it offers no inherent quality gain alone. Further
adding history guidance without the second-order joint term also remains worse than condition CFG.
Only the full Hybrid CFG substantially reduces FGD to 3.532, validating the explicit interaction
between condition and history guidance. The activation interval is fixed to t ∈[0.1, 0.6]. Detailed
CFG scale, composition, and activation-interval ablations are in Tabs. 11a, 11b, 13 and 14. An
end-to-end comparison with and without CFG under the main-experiment setting is in Tab. 15.

Since Fig. 7 benchmarks all methods without CFG, we measure

CFG improves quality with modest latency overhead and nearly unchanged memory scaling.
5
Conclusion
We present PASPA for long-horizon co-speech motion generation based on the observation that past
history and the current segment demand different treatments. PASPA realizes this via a hybrid design,
summarizing past history into a persistent state while modeling the current segment with bidirectional
attention. Built on blockwise flow matching, PASPA incorporates multi-block supervision for effective
training and a prefill-decode protocol with hybrid classifier-free guidance for stable, expressive
inference. Extensive public-benchmark evaluations show that PASPA achieves state-of-the-art FGD
performance, preserving style and preventing temporal drift during extended motion generation.

Observations and Implications.
Figure 3 reveals a clear temporal-persistence asymmetry between
the two feature types. The style ACF ¯rs(τ) remains substantially positive beyond 100 frames.
Conversely, the local ACF ¯rl(τ) decays to near zero within a few tens of frames, indicating that
frame-level pose dynamics are inherently short-term. The pattern persists when replacing hand-crafted
style descriptors with the windowed raw pose mean (inset), confirming the asymmetry is data-intrinsic
rather than descriptor-induced. This asymmetry motivates PASPA’s core design: past history mainly
carries slowly varying stylistic tendencies that should be summarized into a persistent state, whereas
the current block contains fast-changing local dynamics that benefit from dense intra-block attention.

Dataset Information
BEAT2.
BEAT2, proposed by EMAGE [45], is a public mesh-level, motion-captured holistic
co-speech motion benchmark derived from the earlier BEAT dataset [44], providing synchronized
3D body and facial motion paired with speech audio. It contains about 60 hours of 3D motion data
paired with speech from 25 speakers (12 female, 13 male), comprising 1,762 dialogue sequences that
each last around 65 seconds on average. Data representation includes SMPL-X [57] body parameters
(55 axis-angle joints, 3D translation, 4D foot-contact) and FLAME [40] facial expressions (100D
coefficients). Motion and speech are released at 30 FPS and 16 kHz respectively. Following prior
work [45, 54, 89, 92], we adopt the same data partition and report results under two protocols: (1) The
1 Speaker protocol trains and evaluates on the “Scott” subset, isolating style modeling within a single
individual and enabling direct comparison with prior works that report single-speaker performance;
(2) The All Speakers protocol uses the full speaker set jointly, probing cross-identity generalization.
SHOW.
SHOW [85] is used as an additional benchmark to complement our primary evaluation on
BEAT2. It contains about 26.9 hours of in-the-wild talking videos from four speakers with diverse
speaking styles. Unlike the motion-captured annotations in BEAT2, SHOW provides pseudo-ground-
truth motion reconstructed from monocular videos, represented as SMPL-X parameters covering body
poses, hand gestures, and facial expressions. Motion is provided at 30 FPS with synchronized speech
audio. We follow the gesture-evaluation protocol of DiffSHEG [11] for the SHOW experiments.

Implementation Details
E.1
Implementation Details of Main Results
Table 8 summarizes training and inference configurations on BEAT2, our primary benchmark. We
apply these settings to SHOW for supplementary evaluation of PASPA’s effectiveness beyond BEAT2.
Dataset and Motion Representation.
We train and evaluate on BEAT2 [45], comprising 25
speakers with synchronized speech, body motion, and expressions. Each motion frame consists of
55 skeleton joints (25 torso, 30 hands), a global root translation, and a 100-dimensional FLAME
expression vector [40]. Following common practice, native axis-angle rotations are converted to
rot6d [97]. The rot6d rotations [97] and global root translation form a 333-dimensional motion vector.
For SHOW [85], we expand the hand PCA coefficients into axis-angle rotations and align the poses
with BEAT2’s 55-joint ordering, followed by conversion to global rot6d. Translation channels are set
to zero, while the 100-dimensional expression coefficients are retained. For both datasets, speech is
processed at 16 kHz, yielding 1024-dim WavLM features [12].
Architecture and Hardware.
We adopt a 6-layer backbone shown in Fig. 4 with hidden dimension
384 and 8 attention heads. It primarily consists of a PASPA module (Sec. 3.2), with BGDN for history
propagation and BMHSA for current-block modeling. We use RMSNorm [88] for normalization and
RoPE [68] for positional encoding within BMHSA (see Sec. 3.2.2). The block size is set to K = 64
by default, the empirically optimal value in Tab. 10. Speaker IDs are one-hot encoded and mapped
to separate learned embeddings in the expression predictor and motion backbone. In the latter, the
speaker and timestep embeddings are concatenated into a 768-dimensional conditioning vector.

For both datasets, a speaker-conditioned network predicts 100-dimensional expression coefficients
from WavLM features. On BEAT2, we reuse GlobalDiff’s pretrained audio-to-expression net-
work [92]. On SHOW, we train a TalkSHOW-based face predictor [85] using WavLM features to
predict the expression. WavLM and expression predictors remain frozen during PASPA training.
The BEAT2 configuration has 65.158 M parameters and 16.633 G multiply-accumulate operations
(MACs) per forward pass. The MACs are measured via ptflops at batch size 1 and sequence length
256, with all Triton kernels replaced by their PyTorch fallbacks. Training is distributed via data
parallelism on 4 NVIDIA A800 GPUs using Float32 precision.
Training.
We train for 1,000 epochs on both datasets (316,000 steps on BEAT2 and 82,000 on
SHOW). On BEAT2, main experiments and 500-epoch ablations and analyses take approximately
30 and 15 hours, respectively, on 4 GPUs. Following the standard reporting protocol used by prior
work [89, 92], we save checkpoints every 10 epochs and report results from the checkpoint which
achieves the best FGD on each dataset. Each GPU processes 72 sequences of 256 frames (i.e., 4
blocks), yielding a global batch size of 288. As detailed in Sec. 3.3, we sample timesteps from a
logit-normal distribution [20] (µ = −1.0, σ = 1.0) and enforce a weakly monotonic order across
22


blocks, with both designs empirically validated in Tabs. 4 and 9. The training loss uses adaptive loss
weighting (see Eq. (8)) to balance contributions across timesteps and blocks, which is proven crucial
in Tab. 4. The condition (cond) is dropped with 0.1 probability, while the history state (hist) is
assigned three modes: kept (keep, 0.7), nulled (null, 0.1), or linearly interpolated to null (interp,
0.2). To avoid extreme gradient updates, we rescale gradients to unit L2 norm.
Optimization.
We optimize PASPA via a mixed Muon-AdamW optimizer [31, 35, 46] for fast
convergence, omitting weight EMA (exponential moving average). Following standard practice,
Muon [31, 46] optimizes the hidden matrix-shaped weights, while AdamW [35] handles the remaining
parameters, including the input embeddings, the final output projection, and the 1D gains and biases.
We further place BGDN under AdamW, as we find Muon ineffective on it. Muon uses momentum
0.95 with RMS-matched learning-rate adjustment to keep the Muon update scale comparable to
AdamW. AdamW uses β1 = 0.9 and β2 = 0.999. Both optimizers apply weight decay 0.01. The
shared learning rate is 10−3, dropping to 10−4 after 5,000 steps to accelerate early training.
Inference.
We use 10 denoising steps. Following common practice [45, 89, 92], we prefill an
8-frame seed motion (see line 1 of Algorithm 1) into the state to initialize the rollout. We adopt Hybrid
CFG under the Interaction mode (i.e., keeping second-order joint term in line 7 of Algorithm 1;
concrete form in Tab. 12). We use guidance scales wcond = 1.5 and whist = 1.5 for the condition
and the history, validated in Tab. 11. Guidance is restricted to t ∈[0.1, 0.6], tuned via Tab. 14.

F.1.2
Block Size and Training Sequence Length
Table 10 studies the sensitivity to the block size K and the training window length T. For the
block-size sweep, we fix T = 256 and vary K, while for the window-length sweep, we fix K = 64
and vary T. In the block-size sweep, K = 64 achieves the best FGD, whereas smaller blocks degrade
FGD and substantially increase MSE. This suggests that overly short blocks fragment the local motion
segment and limit the dense bidirectional interactions needed for modeling fast-changing intra-block
dynamics. In the window-length sweep, T = 256 gives the best FGD. A shorter window (T = 128)
remains competitive on some secondary metrics, but its worse FGD indicates that limited histories
hurt the primary motion quality. Conversely, increasing the window length (e.g., T = 512) does not
further improve FGD, which we attribute to the larger number of blocks in each training window,
introducing more inter-block dependencies and making multi-block denoising harder to learn. We
therefore use K = 64 and T = 256 as the default setting.

Classifier-Free Guidance Activation Interval.
Following the observation that classifier-free
guidance is mainly beneficial in a mid-noise interval [37], we restrict CFG to this range. Concretely,
this is implemented by setting the timestep-dependent scales (e.g., wcond(t)) to non-trivial values only
within the chosen interval, and to 1 otherwise (i.e., no guidance). Table 14 validates this design by
varying the activation interval. Among the tested cases, [0.1, 0.6] achieves the best FGD. Extending
the interval toward either the high-noise side (e.g., [0.1, 0.7]) or the low-noise side (e.g., [0.0, 0.6])
degrades FGD. We therefore use [0.1, 0.6] as the default activation interval.
Effect of CFG on Main Results.
Table 15 compares PASPA with and without CFG under the 1,000-
epoch main-experiment configuration, complementing the 500-epoch ablation in Tab. 5. Compared
with Hybrid CFG, PASPA without CFG yields worse FGD scores: 3.76 vs. 3.71 in the 1 Speaker
setting and 2.59 vs. 2.33 in the All Speakers setting. Hybrid CFG improves FGD, BC, and MSE in
both settings, with slightly lower Div. Since FGD is our primary quality metric, we use Hybrid CFG.

Limitations, Future Work, Failure Cases and Broader Impacts
G.1
Limitations and Future Work
While our method shows promising results in co-speech motion generation, it still has limitations.
Generalization to Other Motion Domains.
The proposed past-as-state, present-as-attention
principle is derived from observations on co-speech motion generation, and we have not verified
whether the same asymmetry holds in other long-horizon motion domains such as music-to-dance [39]
or text-to-motion [71]. Even if this asymmetry generalizes, the specific PASPA instantiation may
require adaptation, and its effectiveness in these broader settings remains to be validated. We leave
the empirical verification of PASPA on other motion generation domains to future work.

Bounded State Capacity.
PASPA propagates past history through a persistent state of fixed
dimensionality S ∈Rdv×dk, regardless of how much history precedes the current block. This
bounded memory imposes an intrinsic compression limit: for extremely long sequences, older context
may be gradually overwritten by new updates. The state is designed to retain slowly varying style
statistics (e.g., gesture amplitude and spatial preference), but may lose details of distant gestures or
long-range correspondences needed to reproduce earlier motions. This trade-off is fundamental to
the broader family of linear recurrent models [16, 22, 82, 84]. A promising direction is the recently
proposed log-linear attention [23], which replaces the fixed-size state with a logarithmically growing
set of states and directly applies to Gated DeltaNet. We leave this for future work.
Train-Inference Gap.
Like most autoregressive generation methods, PASPA is trained with teacher
forcing method (each block is generated conditioned on ground-truth history) [55, 64], while inference
must rely on the previously generated blocks, leading to exposure bias. This bias may lead to error
accumulation in long rollouts, potentially manifesting as gesture repetition, posture collapse, identity
drift, or global-translation artifacts. We do not explore recent techniques that explicitly bridge this
gap, such as Self Forcing [29], which performs autoregressive rollout during training to align the
training and inference distributions. Integrating such strategies is a natural direction for future work.
Evaluation Limitations.
Our quantitative evaluation uses automatic metrics, yet no universally
accepted metric fully captures long-term stylistic consistency and perceptual quality. We therefore
complement these metrics with a user study to assess PASPA’s effectiveness. Developing new style
or perceptual metrics is beyond our scope and is left for future work. Moreover, the generalizability
of these findings should be further validated across broader real-world deployment scenarios.
G.2
Failure Cases
Figure 10 shows a representative failure mode of our method. In the ground truth, the speaker
performs a large in-place circling motion, leading to strong and highly non-linear changes in global
translation. Although PASPA generates plausible local gestures, it fails to recover this circling
trajectory and produces motion that remains mostly around a fixed spatial region. We attribute
this limitation to the current motion generation pipeline [92], where global translation is learned
together with joint rotations (i.e., rot6d [97]) in the same motion stream. These two components have
different geometric structures: translation lies in the Euclidean space R3, whereas the 6D rotation
representation (i.e., rot6d [97]) parameterizes rotations through constrained orthonormal frames on
the Stiefel manifold, corresponding to the structure of SO(3). Learning them jointly, especially within

G.3
Broader Impacts
Positive Impacts.
PASPA aims to improve long-horizon co-speech motion generation, which
can benefit digital humans [99], virtual assistants [87], animated characters [24], education, and
entertainment by enabling more coherent and expressive nonverbal behavior. By reducing the manual
effort required for full-body animation, it may also lower the production cost of educational content,
virtual avatars, and interactive media.
Negative Impacts.
Like other generative models for human motion and appearance [45, 71, 85],
PASPA could be misused to create misleading speech-driven avatars, contributing to impersonation,
disinformation, or non-consensual digital likenesses. Improved long-horizon coherence and style
preservation may make synthetic embodied behavior harder to distinguish from real recordings.
Although our method generates motion rather than audio or appearance, this risk remains relevant
when motion is combined with separately synthesized voice, face, or identity generation systems.
Mitigation.
Our experiments are conducted in a research setting using public co-speech motion
datasets adopted in prior work [45], and our contribution focuses on improving motion generation
within this established benchmark setting, rather than identity recognition, surveillance, or high-stakes
decision making. We do not collect new identifiable personal data or deploy the model in user-facing
applications. When releasing code or checkpoints, we will use research-oriented license terms and
encourage users to avoid deceptive, non-consensual, or identity-misrepresenting applications.
