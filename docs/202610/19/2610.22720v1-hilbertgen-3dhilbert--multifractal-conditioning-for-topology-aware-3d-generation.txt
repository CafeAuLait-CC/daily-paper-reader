Abstract
High-fidelity 3D generative modeling underpins game asset creation, medical
imaging, and VR/AR environmental design, where geometric structure directly
influences perception and decision-making. Diffusion models represent a pow-
erful paradigm for point cloud generation. However, existing approaches rely
heavily on local voxel or patch features and often miss global structural organi-
zation, and much less work has studied how global conditioning can be used to
improve generation quality. In this paper, we introduce HilbertGen-3D, a novel
topology-aware conditional diffusion framework based on Hilbert–Multifractal
Conditioning (HMC). HMC computes multi-scale voxel measures, serializes them
via a 3D Hilbert curve to expose structured spatial dependencies, and estimates
the generalized fractal dimensions from scale-dependent partition functions. We
encode these signals as global embeddings and multi-scale tokens, and inject them
into a diffusion denoiser through an efficient bottleneck fusion module. Across
the Chair, Airplane, and Car categories of ShapeNet, our two conditioning vari-
ants reduce the absolute deviation of 1-Nearest Neighbor Accuracy (1-NNA)
from 50% by up to 8.90 percentage points and increase coverage by up to 5.88
percentage points relative to TopoDiT-3D [6]. This shows that combining local
density cues with global Hilbert–multifractal structure provides a simple and ef-
fective recipe for high-fidelity and diverse 3D generation. Code is available at
https://github.com/shixuanli01/HMC-DiT3D.

Introduction
Generative modeling of three-dimensional (3D) point clouds is a central problem in geometric
learning, with applications spanning shape synthesis, digital content creation, simulation, and 3D
perception [7, 14]. Diffusion models have recently become powerful tools for this task because they
can represent highly multimodal shape distributions and scale to expressive neural backbones [5].
Nevertheless, many existing architectures, such as DiT3D [18], are still primarily driven by local
geometric operators: they voxelize or patchify the shape, construct local tokens, and rely on the
denoiser to recover global point distribution. In practice, this can produce shapes with locally plausible
geometry but weaker global coherence, especially for objects with thin parts, holes, branches, or
strongly nonuniform mass distributions across scales.
This limitation suggests that global structural information should explicitly guide the denoising
process, rather than being left to emerge from local feature aggregation. In this paper, we pro-
pose HilbertGen-3D, a novel topology-aware framework for 3D point cloud generation based on
Hilbert–Multifractal Conditioning (HMC). Instead of constructing persistence diagrams, we start
from multi-scale voxel measures computed on a normalized point cloud. At each scale, we serialize

one-dimensional sequence. We then exploit the multifractal theory [17, 22, 30, 31] and evaluate
the partition function across scales and moments to estimate the generalized fractal dimensions
summarizing how mass concentrates, disperses, and varies across resolutions. Together with the
Hilbert-ordered sequences themselves, these statistics define an HMC representation of the shape.
HMC provides two complementary forms of conditioning. A compact global descriptor captures
the overall multifractal profile of the shape, while windowed embeddings of the Hilbert sequences
retain structured multi-scale context. We encode these signals into a global embedding and a set
of HMC conditioning tokens, then inject them into a diffusion denoiser via a memory-efficient
bottleneck fusion module. This design uses a representation built directly from voxel mass statistics
to preserve the key insight behind topology-aware conditioning, allowing for global shape information
to guide denoising. We also verify that HMC improves structural fidelity, sample diversity, and global
coherence relative to purely local baselines on standard point-cloud generation benchmarks.

Related Works
3D generative models.
Deep generative modeling for 3D shapes has advanced across a range of
representations, including point clouds, voxels, meshes, and implicit fields [25]. Early point-set
methods, such as PointFlow [26], modeled shape distributions with hierarchical continuous normal-
izing flows, while ShapeGF [2] learned gradient fields whose sampling procedure recovers surface
geometry. Diffusion models then became a dominant paradigm for 3D shape synthesis: PVD [32]
formulated denoising diffusion directly on point clouds, LION [29] moved diffusion to a hierarchical
latent space to improve fidelity and enable downstream manipulations, and DiT-3D [18] showed that
plain diffusion transformers can serve as competitive 3D generators. In parallel, conditional systems
such as Point-E [20] and Shap-E [9] expanded generation beyond unconditional shape synthesis,
while GET3D [4] demonstrated high-quality textured mesh generation from image collections. More
recently, topology-aware 3D diffusion has begun to incorporate persistent homology(PH) features as
global structural cues [6]. This paper introduces a new approach based on the Hilbert–Multifractal
Conditioning method, which injects Hilbert-serialized multifractal descriptors as multi-scale struc-
tural priors for point-cloud generation. Whereas persistent homology captures connected components,
loops, and voids, HMC captures voxel mass distribution, density heterogeneity, and local spatial
neighborhoods. Furthermore, persistent homology uses a dedicated topological encoding pipeline,
while Hilbert sequences can be directly processed by sequence models and bottleneck fusion modules,
which can exploit prior information about the reference density structure to improve generation
quality and diversity.
Hilbert curves and structure-preserving serialization for 3D data.
Space-filling curves provide
a principled way to analyze multidimensional geometry. Classical analysis showed that the Hilbert
curve has strong clustering behavior and can preserve locality better than the z-curve in common query
settings [19]. This idea has recently reappeared in 3D deep learning as a practical serialization tool
for irregular point clouds. OctFormer [23] uses octree-based ordering to form efficient local windows
for transformer processing. Point Transformer V3 explicitly serializes point clouds with z-order and
Hilbert curves to build efficient grouped attention, arguing that Hilbert ordering offers stronger locality
preservation [24]. PointMamba [13] similarly leverages space-filling curves to turn unstructured
point clouds into sequences amenable to linear-time state-space modeling. Point2Point [21] goes
further by directly learning on Hilbert-sorted point clouds and reports competitive results on both
analysis and generation tasks. These works use Hilbert order primarily as a computational device for
tokenization, grouping, or sequence modeling. In contrast, our method uses the Hilbert traversal as
part of the conditioning signal itself: we serialize multi-scale voxel measures along the curve, extract
both global multifractal summaries and local Hilbert-window embeddings, and feed these descriptors
into the denoiser as explicit global structural priors.

3.2
Hilbert Curves
Our denoiser uses the diffusion-transformer backbone of DiT-3D [18]. To construct a separate
sequence of geometric conditioning signals, we serialize a voxel mass field using a 3D Hilbert
traversal. This orders voxel cells, rather than the original points, and provides a spatially structured
input to the HMC condition encoder. A Hilbert curve provides such an ordering. In the continuous
setting, the Hilbert curve is a space-filling map
γ : [0, 1] →[0, 1]d.

For our purposes, we fix d = 3 in the rest of the paper. Hilbert curves are constructed recursively: a
unit cube is divided into 2d smaller subcubes, and at a finer level, each octant is recursively traversed
using an appropriately rotated or reflected lower-order pattern. Its orientation is chosen so that the exit
from one octant connects to the entry into the next; the same unrotated pattern is not simply repeated
independently in every octant.. For more detail on the construction, see Moon et al. [19]. As the
recursion depth increases, the Hilbert curve visits finer and finer spatial cells, and in the limit, it fills
the entire domain. For computation, we use the discrete 3D Hilbert traversal. At scale s, let Rs = 2s
and Vs = {0, . . . , Rs −1}3 be a regular voxel grid. A discrete Hilbert ordering is a bijection
πs : Vs →{0, . . . , R3
s −1},
which assigns each voxel a unique one-dimensional index and visits every voxel exactly once. The
inverse map π−1
s
maps a Hilbert index back to its corresponding voxel cell. Thus, Hilbert serialization
only permutes the voxel values and therefore introduces no additional information loss beyond
voxelization.
Locality preservation. The key property of the Hilbert curve is locality preservation. Although
no one-dimensional ordering can perfectly preserve all Euclidean distances in three dimensions,
Hilbert order tends to place spatially nearby voxels close to each other in the induced sequence [19].
Contiguous intervals of Hilbert indices often correspond to compact spatial neighborhoods, and the
recursive construction makes this property hold across multiple resolutions. Compared with simple
raster or lexicographic ordering, Hilbert ordering avoids many long spatial jumps and better preserves
clustered structure. This property is especially useful for point-cloud generation. After a point cloud
is normalized and voxelized, the Hilbert traversal converts its spatial mass distribution into an ordered
signal. Coarse recursion levels encode global layout, while deeper recursion levels resolve local
details such as thin parts, branches, and nonuniform density. Therefore, Hilbert serialization provides
a natural interface between unordered 3D geometry and sequence-based neural architectures. In our
method, this ordering is used to construct Hilbert–Multifractal Conditioning tokens that provide the
denoiser with locality-preserving, multi-scale structural information.

benchmark for 3D point cloud generation [3]. ShapeNet comprises 51, 127 shapes across 55 classes.
Following prior work, we train HilbertGen-3D on the Chair, Airplane, and Car categories. We show
further results on Lamp and Vessel in Section A.

Evaluation.
Following prior work [18, 6], we use Chamfer Distance (CD) and Earth Mover’s
Distance (EMD) to compute 1-Nearest Neighbor Accuracy (1-NNA) and Coverage (COV). The
1-NNA metric computes the leave-one-out accuracy of a 1-Nearest Neighbor classifier, with lower
scores signifying superior generative performance in both quality and diversity. Coverage (COV), on
the other hand, quantifies the proportion of ground-truth reference point clouds that correspond to
at least one generated shape. Although COV is highly sensitive to mode dropping and serves as an
effective measure of diversity, it does not assess the geometric quality of the generated point clouds.
Implementation.
To ensure consistency, we adopt the implementation from DiT-3D and TopoDiT-
3D [18, 6]. The input voxel size is set to 32 × 32 × 32 × 3(V = 32), and we train the models
for 10, 000 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10−4 and a batch size of
512. In our experiments, we set T = 1000 and use S/4 configuration with a patch size p = 4.
Specifically, we pick the scale set S = {3, 4, 5} and momentum set Q = {1, 2, 3}. We pick the
window length w = 64 to generate the HMC token and set the depth of the perceiver resampler to 4
with bottleneck latent dimension Kb = 128. After training the DiT backbone, we report metrics from
two versions of the proposed algorithm: in the first version, we construct a bank of HMC tokens from
the training data, which is then randomly sampled from during generation; in the second version,
we separately train a variational autoencoder on the training data and then use the VAE to generate
HMC tokens during inference. The VAE baseline uses latent dimension 64, encoder hidden widths
(2048, 1024, 512) with SiLU activations, and a decoder with the reversed hidden widths. Its objective
uses βVAE = 0.05 and unit reconstruction weights. VAE training uses AdamW with initial learning
rate 10−3, zero weight decay, batch size 256, cosine learning-rate decay, gradient clipping at 1, and
2000 epochs.

5.3
Ablation analysis.
We conduct ablation studies on the Airplane category to examine the contributions of Hilbert
serialization, generalized-dimension descriptors, and bottleneck fusion. Similar to the sensitivity
study, we use a batch size of 128 and train for 2000 epochs. Table 2 compares the full model with
variants that remove each component. The full model achieves the best 1-NNA-CD and both coverage
metrics, while the variant without generalized dimensions achieves the closest 1-NNA-EMD to 50.
Throughout this comparison, changes in 1-NNA are interpreted through the absolute distance from
50, rather than the raw accuracy alone.
Removing the HMC tokens worsens all four reported metrics. The 1-NNA-CD and 1-NNA-EMD
increase from 55.57 and 53.66 to 57.33 and 54.76, respectively, increasing their distances from
50 by 1.76 and 1.10 percentage points. Meanwhile, COV-CD decreases from 60.98 to 58.50, and
COV-EMD decreases from 59.27 to 57.67, corresponding to reductions of 2.48 and 1.60 percentage
points. These results support the use of Hilbert serialization for constructing spatially structured
conditioning signals. Among the tested ablations, removing this component produces the largest
deterioration in EMD-based 1-NNA.
Removing the global HMC embedding increases 1-NNA-CD from 55.57 to 55.84, moving it 0.27
percentage points farther from 50. COV-CD and COV-EMD decrease from 60.98 and 59.27 to 60.24
and 58.25, respectively, corresponding to reductions of 0.74 and 1.02 percentage points. However,
1-NNA-EMD improves slightly from 53.66 to 53.58, reducing its distance from 50 by 0.08 percentage
points. Thus, the global descriptor provides complementary benefits for CD-based distribution
matching and coverage in this experiment, but does not improve every reported metric.
Removing bottleneck fusion produces the largest deterioration in CD-based 1-NNA and both coverage
metrics. The 1-NNA-CD increases from 55.57 to 60.00, moving 4.43 percentage points farther from
50, while COV-CD and COV-EMD decrease to 55.92 and 55.83, representing reductions of 5.06 and
3.44 percentage points. The 1-NNA-EMD also worsens, increasing from 53.66 to 54.54 and moving
0.88 percentage points farther from 50. These results indicate that the fusion architecture is important
for using HMC conditions effectively in the evaluated configuration.
Overall, all three components contribute to the full model’s CD-based 1-NNA and coverage ad-
vantages over the ablated variants. Bottleneck fusion has the largest observed effect on these three
metrics, while the EMD-based 1-NNA results show a more nuanced pattern. The ablations therefore
support the combined design without establishing that every component improves every evaluation
criterion.
6
Conclusion
In this work, we presented HilbertGen-3D, a diffusion framework for point cloud generation based
on Hilbert–Multifractal Conditioning (HMC). Experiments suggest that HMC is a promising condi-
tioning strategy for improving global coherence and structural fidelity in generated 3D shapes. More
broadly, the proposed framework shows that locality-preserving spatial serializations together with
multi-resolution scaling statistics can serve as an effective alternative to purely local conditioning
schemes. On the other hand, it shall be noted that, while HilbertGen-3D can effectively generate
point clouds in settings when prior information of the overall density distribution can be exploited,
alternative approaches (such as persistent homology) may perform well otherwise. Future directions
include distilling the diffusion model to reduce computational cost [27] and using Bayesian techniques
to further improve generation accuracy [28].

Moment-order selection.
The baseline moment set Q = {1, 2, 3} achieves the best 1-NNA-CD
and COV-EMD within its group, at 55.57 and 59.27. Using only Q = {1} gives the highest COV-
CD of 61.31 and also yields a 1-NNA-EMD value closer to 50 than the baseline, at 53.11. The
set {1, 2, 3, 4} achieves the best 1-NNA-EMD of 52.65, improving its distance from 50 by 1.01
percentage points relative to the baseline, but reducing COV-CD and COV-EMD by 1.67 and 0.98
percentage points. Adding moment orders therefore does not produce a monotonic improvement.
The {1} configuration retains the information-dimension descriptor; it should not be interpreted as
removing the global HMC descriptor altogether.
Interpretation and scope.
The baseline provides a common reference, but the table does not
identify it as uniformly optimal. In particular, MLP ratio 3 improves all four reported means relative
to that reference. The groupwise comparisons also do not establish the performance of a configuration
that combines the individually favorable settings, since interactions between parameter choices are
not evaluated by these comparisons.
These results concern Ours-Bank on Airplane under the 2000-epoch training recipe. They do not
establish the same parameter preferences for Ours-VAE, other categories, or longer training schedules.
The four seeds are evaluation seeds; the table does not quantify variation across independently trained
models. Furthermore, without seedwise dispersion estimates, small differences such as 0.01 in
1-NNA-CD between 96 and 128 latents, or 0.02 in COV-CD between MLP ratios 3 and 3.5, should
be interpreted as numerical rankings rather than evidence of statistically significant performance
differences.

D
Computational Cost Analysis
We further evaluate the computational cost of our method and compare it with representative diffusion-
transformer baselines, including DiT-3D and TopoDiT-3D. Since all our experiments are conducted
under the S/4 configuration, we focus on computational efficiency under this setting rather than
performing an additional model-scale sweep. Following prior work, we report the sampling time in
seconds per iteration (s/it), and additionally measure the peak GPU memory consumption during
training. As shown in Table 6, our method achieves a sampling speed of 2.60 s/it, which is shorter
than the reported S/4 inference time of both DiT-3D and TopoDiT-3D. Meanwhile, our method also
uses less training memory than both baselines. These results indicate that the proposed design does
not introduce heavy computational overhead; instead, it maintains efficient inference while reducing
the practical memory burden during training.
We also compare the training throughput on the chair category, which is the main category used in
our controlled analysis. As shown in Table 7, our method requires 2.3 seconds per epoch, while the
baseline setting requires 11.8 seconds per epoch. This corresponds to a 5.13× training speedup, or
equivalently a 80.5% reduction in per-epoch training time. Together with the lower peak memory
usage in Table 6, this shows that our method improves computational efficiency not only during
sampling, but also during training.
