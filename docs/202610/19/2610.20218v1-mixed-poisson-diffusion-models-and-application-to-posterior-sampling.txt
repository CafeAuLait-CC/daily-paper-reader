Abstract 

Diffusion models are a powerful framework for learning complex probability distributions. While widely used diffusion models for continuous data rely on Gaussian noising processes, we instead develop diffusion models based on Poisson noise. More precisely, we introduce and study a new mathematical framework, called Mixed Poisson Diffusion Models (MPDM), with noising dynamics driven by multivariate counting processes. Our methodology is motivated by Poisson inverse problems. The success of Gaussian diffusion models has led to their use as prior models in a growing number of posterior sampling methods for inverse problems. However, most of these approaches focus on Gaussian inverse problems, in which observations are generated through a deterministic forward operator corrupted by additive Gaussian noise. By contrast, Poisson inverse problems, where observations are discrete and Poisson distributed, remain comparatively underexplored in this literature. Building on our Poisson generative dynamics, we develop a natural framework for posterior sampling in Poisson inverse problems and derive several posterior sampling algorithms based on the proposed model. We evaluate our approach on realistic Poisson inverse problems and show that the resulting framework is well suited to this class of problems. 

# 1 Introduction 

Diffusion models have emerged as a powerful and versatile framework for learning complex probability distributions, with striking empirical success in high-dimensional generative modeling (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021). Their central principle is to define a tractable forward noising process that progressively transforms the data distribution into a simple reference distribution, and then to learn the corresponding reverse-time dynamics in order to generate new samples. In their most widely used form, diffusion models rely on Gaussian perturbations, either through discrete-time Gaussian transitions or continuous-time stochastic differential equations (SDEs). This Gaussian structure is particularly appealing from both theoretical and computational perspectives, as it leads to explicit transition kernels and efficient simulation schemes. At the same time, recent works have demonstrated that the diffusion paradigm is not intrinsically tied to Gaussian noise. In particular, diffusion-type generative models have been extended to discrete state spaces through appropriately designed Markov chains and continuous-time jump processes (Austin et al., 2021; Campbell et al., 2022). These developments suggest a broader perspective in which the choice of the forward corruption mechanism can be adapted to the geometry or the observation model underlying the problem of interest. Yet, despite this flexibility, comparatively little attention has been devoted to diffusion models whose noising mechanism is driven by counting processes. In this work, we introduce and study a new mathematical framework, which we call Mixed Poisson Diffusion Models 

(MPDMs), in which the forward noising dynamics are given by multivariate counting processes, and in particular mixed Poisson processes (Grandell, 1997). Rather than progressively corrupting the data by additive Gaussian perturbations, MPDMs encode the data through the random accumulation of discrete events, leading to a fundamentally different family of intermediate distributions driven by Poisson noising dynamics. The resulting framework preserves the main conceptual structure of diffusion modeling: a simulation-free noising mechanism that defines a path of probability distributions, together with a Markov process sharing the same marginals and characterized by learnable dynamics, ultimately yielding a tractable generative model.

In a Bayesian inverse problem, the goal is to recover an unknown signal of interest x from a corrupted observation y. The central object is the posterior distribution πy of the signal, defined through Bayes’ rule, up to a normalizing constant, by πy (d x) ∝ g(y|x) π(d x), where 

π denotes the prior distribution on the unknown signal and (y, x ) 7 → g(y|x) is the likelihood relating the observation to the underlying signal. Diffusion models have recently been used as expressive data-driven priors in Bayesian inverse problems and have enabled high-quality reconstructions in a broad range of imaging applications. However, sampling from πy remains challenging because the diffusion prior is typically available only implicitly through a learned score or denoiser function. To address this difficulty, a large body of work has proposed modifying the reverse diffusion dynamics through an additional guidance term so as to generate approximate samples from the posterior distribution. Prominent examples include Diffusion Posterior Sampling (DPS) (Chung et al., 2023), Plug-and-Play Diffusion Models (PnP-DM) (Wu et al., 2024), and Decoupled Annealing Posterior Sampling (DAPS) (Zhang et al., 2025), among many others (Song et al., 2023; Kawar et al., 2022; Janati et al., 2025a); see, for instance, the review of Janati et al. (2025b). Most existing methodologies, however, are developed or theoretically justified primarily for linear inverse problems with Gaussian observation noise. In this setting, the Gaussian structure of the likelihood is naturally compatible with the Gaussian noising process underlying standard diffusion models. In particular, for an important class of such problems, this compatibility makes it possible to exploit explicit Gaussian identities and, in some cases, to construct exact poste-rior transitions; see (25) and Appendix C. This favorable structure generally disappears even for simple Poisson inverse problems, where observations are discrete and the likelihood is Poisson rather than Gaussian. Developing a diffusion framework that is intrinsically compatible with Poisson observations is therefore the main applicative motivation for the methodology introduced in this work. This class of problem is common in fields such as medical imaging, e.g., Positron Emission Tomography (PET) (Bailey et al., 2005), or astronomical observation (Donath et al., 2024). These Poisson inverse problems often arise from photon-counting measurements, where observed counts can be close to or equal to zero, and one cannot reasonably default to a Gaussian noise model. Indeed, in such settings, the observation noise is inherently discrete and signal dependent, making Gaussian-based generative dynamics less naturally matched to the underlying sta-tistical model. By building the generative process itself from Poissonian dynamics, MPDMs provide a framework that is naturally aligned with this class of observation models and, as we show, leads to new posterior-sampling methodologies for Poisson inverse problems. Our contribution is threefold: 

• In Section 2, we introduce Mixed Poisson Diffusion Models (MPDMs). We construct a stochastic noising dynamics based on the theory of multivariate counting processes (Protter, 2005; Daley and Vere-Jones, 2008), and notably mixed Poisson processes (Grandell, 1997; Zocher, 2005). This yields a rigorous mathematical framework for Poissonian generative mod-eling (Chen and Zhou, 2023; Bhattacharya et al., 2025), closely paralleling the Gaussian diffusion framework described in Section 2.2. We further establish quantitative sampling-error bounds that explicitly account for early stopping, denoiser estimation, and time-discretization errors, thereby providing a Poissonian counterpart to the error analyses commonly developed for other types of diffusion samplers. 

• In Section 3, we develop a framework for MPDM-based posterior sampling . To the best of our knowledge, this is the first posterior sampling methodology built upon Poissonian generative dynamics. We motivate this construction through Poisson inverse problems. For simple forward operators, Theorem 3 establishes a structural connection between MPDMs and Poisson inverse problems: in contrast to Gaussian diffusion-based approaches, posterior sampling with MPDMs can be carried out without introducing substantial approximations. For more general forward operators, we adapt several established diffusion-based posterior sampling methods to the MPDM setting. 

• Finally, we evaluate the proposed algorithms in Section 4. In particular we consider both synthetic and realistic Poisson inverse problems. Our experiments demonstrate the practical relevance of the framework and highlight the advantages of matching the generative noising dynamics to the Poisson structure of the observation model.

# 4 Poisson inverse problems: numerical experiments 

Despite our focus on inverse problems, we emphasise that MPDMs are generative models, and that prior works on Poisso-nian generative modelling suggest potential advantages over classical diffusion models for certain data distributions; see (Chen and Zhou, 2023; Bhattacharya et al., 2025). For the experiments below, we trained MPDMs on the BrainWeb dataset (Aubert-Broche et al., 2006; Schramm, 2021), and mock galaxy images from the EAGLE simulations (McAlpine et al., 2016); see Appendix E.5 for a gallery of MPDM-generated images, and Appendix E.1 for datasets details. 

Benchmark. Several diffusion-based posterior sampling algorithms are formulated for additive, homogeneous Gaus-sian noise, and do not directly apply to Poisson inverse problems, even with Gaussian approximation Pois( A(X)) ≈

N( A(X), diag( A(X))) . DPS can accommodate both the true Poisson likelihood and its Gaussian approximation; the lat-ter is the canonical version described by Chung et al. (2023). PnP-DM and DAPS likelihood steps can theoretically handle a Poisson likelihood. Our benchmark can be separated into three blocks. 1) Our MPDM-based algorithms adapting DPS (Algorithm 5), PnP-DM (Algorithm 6) and DAPS (Algorithm 8) for general inverse problems; or PDPS (Algorithm 4) for the Poisson denoising problems. 2) Diffusion-based models with the exact Poisson likelihood: DPS, PnP-DM and DAPS; or 3) with Gaussian-approximated likelihood. Each method uses the same number of NFEs ( i.e. , pre-trained denoiser calls). Computational costs nevertheless differ, notably because PnP-DM and DAPS require MCMC steps; see Appendix E for details. We train MPDMs and Gaussian diffusion-based models from scratch for each dataset, with identical architectures and training budgets; see Appendix E.3. The performance on realistic Poisson inverse problems is evaluated through several metrics: Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM), as well as Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS). These metrics evaluate the quality of a single reconstructed image, and Table 2 shows averages over the validation datasets. # 5 Conclusion 

We construct and study “Poissonian” diffusion models, named MPDMs, using the multivariate counting processes frame-work. We motivate the use of MPDM beyond generative modeling by adapting several diffusion-based posterior sampling algorithms; in particular, we demonstrate mathematically and empirically that MPDMs are well adapted to Poisson inverse problems, which remain relatively under-explored in this literature.

We provide a brief overview of diffusion-based posterior sampling. In particular, we explain the core ideas of the algo-rithms that are adapted to the MPDM setting in Section 3. We will refer to the diffusion model introduced in Section 2.2 and the associated notations. 

Gaussian inverse problems. Let A : Rd → Rdy denote the forward operator, with 1 ⩽ dy ⩽ d, and consider the posterior distribution πy of X ∼ π given the observation Y = y, where

During training, we add random lesions to slices 2. We augment the dataset during training by applying random translation ( ±10 pixels), horizontal flip, and small rotation ( ± 2°).

34 E.2 Training details 

While most of the literature relies on pre-trained models to evaluate Gaussian diffusion-based posterior sampling algo-rithms, our approach requires training models from scratch. The input z of the Poisson denoiser, (z, t ) 7 → Kπt (z), scales with t due to the nature of MPP. Thus, we use rescaled inputs for the network approximating Kπ , denoted by kθ , namely 

z/ (1 + t) and 1/(1 + t) instead of z, t . These design choices are similar to those of Bhattacharya et al. (2025). The net-work directly predicts the denoised image, rather than a residual correction to the input. We used the same strategy to train Gaussian denoisers approximating Dπ . The Gaussian diffusion formulation we considered, detailed in Section 2.2, is admittedly not the most common; however, it shares several similarities with MPDM: SNR increasing with t, process 

X scaling as Xt ≈ t, and such that Xt/t → X ∼ π almost surely. 

Architectures. For the toy example, we used a time-conditioned residual multilayer perceptron with a hidden dimen-sion of 128 and three residual blocks. The two-dimensional input was first projected to the hidden dimension, and the diffusion time variable was encoded using a 64-dimensional time embedding followed by a two-layer MLP. Each residual block consisted of two linear layers with SiLU activations and was conditioned on the time embedding through feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM), implemented as a learned scale and shift. A final linear layer mapped the hidden repre-sentation back to the two-dimensional output space, followed by a Softplus activation to enforce non-negativity. For the BrainWeb and EAGLE datasets, we used a U-Net architecture (Ronneberger et al., 2015) with 32 input channels, base width 256, and 5 resolution levels. The number of channels doubles at every level, with spatial downsampling by a factor of two. Each encoder and decoder level consists of two residual blocks. The residual blocks use Group Normalization and SiLU activations and are conditioned on the diffusion time through the same feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM) mechanism. The time variable is first mapped to a 128-dimensional sinusoidal embedding, followed by a two-layer MLP. The bottleneck consists of two residual blocks and contains no attention. The decoder uses nearest-neighbor upsampling followed by convolution and concatenates the corresponding encoder features through standard U-Net skip connections. A final 1 × 1 convolution produces the output channels, followed by a Softplus activation to enforce non-negativity. 

Training. We trained our models with the Adam optimizer and a 10 −4 learning rate. During training, each image is noised at a level t with a log-uniform distribution: log t is uniform on [−7, 7] . This non-uniform distribution for t is compensated with a Jacobian factor; see Appendix D.1. We use the usual l2 loss instead of the Bregman divergence (4): after comparing both choices, we observed no significant difference in our experiments. For the image datasets, we used a batch size of 32 and trained for 3500 epochs for the PET dataset and 1000 epochs for the astrophysics dataset. For the toy example, we used approximately 10 5 optimization steps, with batches of 1024 fresh samples from the Gamma mixture distribution defined in Appendix E.3. 

## E.3 Inverse problems: settings and detailed results 

Computational budget and hyperparameters. PDPS, DPS, PnP-DM and DAPS all require a time grid t0 < t 1 <

· · · < t N to approximately simulate the underlying Gaussian diffusion/MPDM. PDPS and DPS use one NFE (denoiser call) per iteration, hence N NFEs in total; to be precise, DPS requires vector-Jacobian computations. For DAPS and PnP-DM, there is an additional inner loop at each prior step: if we fix the total number of NFEs, we must allocate them between the outer loop on t0 < t 1 < . . . t N and the inner loop that simulates the diffusion from t onward. For fairness and simplicity, we use only one NFE for each prior step, and keep the same grid t0 < t 1 < · · · < t N for every algorithm. This design choice is present in DAPS: the authors observe that more NFEs in the inner loop lead to slightly better reconstruction metrics for linear inverse problems; see (Zhang et al., 2025, Appendix I). On the other hand, using only one inner-loop NFE is admittedly a slight modification of the original PnP-DM design. In the likelihood step of DAPS and PnP-DM, we set the number of Langevin steps to 128 and the step-size to δ = 0 .001 .While computationally cheap, this likelihood step is repeated at each iteration, thus N times. For simplicity, we choose to only match the number of NFEs between these methods and DPS/PDPS, ignoring these Langevin steps. Note that for Gaussian diffusion-based PnP-DM and DAPS, we use a standard Langevin MCMC scheme and clamp updates that have negative values, as the authors do not discuss the case where the density is supported on Rd

> +

. Meanwhile, our MPDM-based adaptations rely on Riemannian Langevin MCMC (Algorithm 7). The hyperparameter η mimics the empirical tuning of βy present in DAPS (Zhang et al., 2025, Appendix F.2): it effectively increases the weight of the likelihood term in the Langevin scheme. As in the original DAPS paper, we observe better reconstruction metrics when η > 1, so we keep this design choice in our implementations. This additional parameter is 35 not present in PnP-DM: we include it to ensure fairness, as we also observe better empirical performance with η > 1. In each experiment, we test several values of η for both DAPS and PnP-DM, ranging from 1 to 20 , and select the one that yields the best reconstruction metrics; see Table 10. ## E.4 PET: additional measurements 

The classical metrics we use to evaluate posterior sampling algorithms measure the quality of a given reconstructed image. They therefore provide only a partial assessment of sampling quality, as they do not directly account for the dispersion or diversity of the generated samples. Typically, in low-count regimes, posterior sampling methods should capture the dispersion of the true posterior. Moreover, the relevant notion of reconstruction quality may depend on the downstream task and the specific inverse problem at hand. In PET, for example, detecting lesions and accurately quantifying the associated uncertainty can be more relevant than the image quality measured by the aforementioned metrics. Figures 12 and 13 illustrate the detection of lesions with the BrainWeb dataset, in low-count regimes. To quantify this, we also evaluate reconstruction errors specifically within the lesions; see Tables 11 and 12. To do so, we compute the mean absolute error between the ground truth images and the approximate posterior samples, restricted to lesions (pixels with value > 0.9 on either image). If x denotes the ground truth and ˆx the reconstruction, we define it as
