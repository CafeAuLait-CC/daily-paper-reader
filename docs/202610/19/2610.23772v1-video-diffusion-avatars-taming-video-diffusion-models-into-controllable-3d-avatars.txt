ABSTRACT
Video diffusion models (VDMs) trained on internet-scale data encode a rich prior
on appearance, motion, and physics that is difficult to replicate with explicit 3D
rendering and costly 3D data capture. We introduce Video Diffusion Avatars
(VDAs), a method that creates photo-realistic, multi-view consistent avatars from
a single image by fine-tuning a VDM for precise control over camera viewpoint,
facial expression, and upper-body pose. Departing from 3D morphable models
(3DMMs), VDAs use a learned expression space that lifts the expressiveness ceil-
ing of 3DMMs, e.g. for the tongue and mouth interior. However, learned expres-
sion codes exhibit heavy viewpoint bias, as our DISTRACT benchmark reveals.
We show that this bias can be removed through disentanglement, enabling their
use as driving signals for multi-view consistent avatars. Camera and upper-body
pose are controlled jointly via proxy-mesh renderings, giving independent control
over expression, pose, and viewpoint. A camera-triplet training strategy makes the
approach efficient: our models train for 34 hours on a single A100 using only pub-
lic multi-view data. VDAs outperform state-of-the-art single-image 3D avatars on
novel-view reenactment, reducing face LPIPS by 29% and improving perceptual
quality (JOD) by 66% on BecomingLit. A static 3DGS reconstruction probe fur-
ther confirms that our generations are the most multi-view consistent. Built on a
video prior, VDAs produce effects beyond the reach of explicit renderers, includ-
ing physically plausible hair dynamics, shadows, reflections, and refractions.

est artifacts read as uncanny. In-the-wild use raises the bar further: accessories, complex lighting,
and hair dynamics must be handled convincingly from a single reference image.
The dominant paradigm builds explicit volumetric avatars, typically 3DGS (Kerbl et al., 2023) or
voxels (Lombardi et al., 2021; Cao et al., 2022), with expressions driven by 3DMMs (Blanz & Vetter,
1999; Li et al., 2017). This approach faces two fundamental limits. First, no large-scale 3D training
data exists, and, additionally, effects such as shadows, reflections, refractions, and physically plau-
sible hair dynamics are hard to render explicitly. Second, 3DMM-driven expression control suffers
fidelity loss from 2D-to-3D registration. Alternatively, learned expression spaces (Drobyshev et al.,
2022; Cheng et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025) avoid this ceiling but, as our proposed DISTRACT
benchmark reveals, carry a heavy viewpoint bias that must be addressed before they can drive multi-
view consistent avatars.
Video diffusion models (VDMs), trained on internet-scale data, offer a rich prior on 3D geometry,
photo-realistic appearance, motion, and physics. However, existing VDM-based avatar methods ei-
ther build 2D avatars without camera control (Tian et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024;
Zhao et al., 2025), or use the VDM only to synthesize multi-view images that are then distilled into
a 3DGS avatar (Taubner et al., 2025b;a), trading off rendering quality against speed.
We introduce Video Diffusion Avatars (VDAs), and depart from the two most common building
blocks for virtual avatars: 3DMM-control and 3DGS rendering. Following DH-STAR (Zielonka
et al., 2026), we define an avatar as a model that, conditioned on a single identity image, renders that
person under any requested facial expression, upper-body pose, and camera viewpoint. In our ap-
proach the VDM is the avatar: no 3D asset is ever built, and the prior is fully preserved. VDAs rests
on three cornerstones. Expression control: we remove the viewpoint bias from learned expression
encoders with an explicit disentangler, obtaining higher expression fidelity than FLAME-driven
control. Camera and pose control: a proxy mesh combining a FLAME head with a SAM-3D-
Body (Yang et al., 2026b) torso jointly encodes camera viewpoint and upper-body pose, while ex-
posing separate control for both. Training: camera triplets from curated multi-view data (Kirschstein
et al., 2023; Martinez et al., 2024), with reference, driver and target from different cameras, teach
multi-view consistency in 34 hours on a single A100.
VDAs outperform all state-of-the-art single-image 3D avatars on BecomingLit (Schmidt et al.,
2025), Multiface (Wuu et al., 2022), and VFHQ-test (Xie et al., 2022), improving LPIPS by 29%
and JOD by 66% over the strongest baseline. A 3DGS optimization against our generations outper-
forms prior diffusion-based avatars (Taubner et al., 2025b;a), camera-controlled VDMs (Lyu et al.,
2026), and 3DGS-based avatars (Kirschstein et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026). Our contributions are:
(a) VDAs replace the explicit 3D asset with a camera- and expression-controlled VDM, inheriting
shadows, reflections, refractions, and hair dynamics from its prior.
(b) We expose viewpoint bias in learned expression encoders with the DISTRACT benchmark,
debias it, and obtain higher expression fidelity than FLAME-driven control.
(c) Camera and pose conditioning via a Plücker-colored proxy mesh, that separates control over
camera viewpoint from neck pose.
(d) Affordable training strategy on public multi-view data using camera triplets in 34 hours on a
single A100, which preserves performance on challenging in-the-wild inputs.

RELATED WORK
Facial expression spaces and 3D morphable models. 3DMMs (Li et al., 2017; Blanz & Vetter,
1999) are the dominant expression parameterization, but they frequently miss micro-expressions
and lack a mouth interior, limiting the fidelity of any system built on them.
However, feed-
forward 3DMM regressors such as DECA (Feng et al., 2021), EMOCA (Danecek et al., 2022), and
Pix2NPHM (Giebenhain et al., 2026), and robust tracking (Giebenhain et al., 2025) are widely avail-
able and make 3DMM-based control practical for in-the-wild applications. Analogous body models
include SMPL (Loper et al., 2015) and SAM-3D-Body (Yang et al., 2026b). Implicit neural head
representations (Giebenhain et al., 2023; 2024a; Zheng et al., 2022) go beyond the linear 3DMM
expression space. Alternatively, several methods learn an expression latent directly from 2D data:
MegaPortraits (Drobyshev et al., 2022) relies on cycle-consistency, while WAN-Animate (Cheng
et al., 2025) and X-NeMo (Zhao et al., 2025) learn expression representations end-to-end for video-
driven animation. In Section 3.1 we compare the qualities of such learned expression spaces and
reveal a heavy viewpoint bias that must be addressed for multi-view consistent avatars.

Avatars. Most recent avatar systems use neural radiance fields (NeRFs) (Mildenhall et al., 2020)
and 3DGS (Kerbl et al., 2023), either for generalizable single-image creation (Kirschstein et al.,
2026; He et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026; Kirschstein et al., 2024b) or for per-identity optimization from
multi-view captures (Xu et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024; Giebenhain et al., 2024b). A separate line
distills the outputs of diffusion models into an explicit 3D representation: CAP4D (Taubner et al.,
2025b) and Joker (Prinzler et al., 2025) condition an image-diffusion model on 3DMM renderings
and distill its multi-view images into a 3DGS/NeRF avatar, and MVP4D (Taubner et al., 2025a)
uses a video-diffusion model instead. These methods trade the photorealistic diffusion prior for an
explicit 3D asset. We refer to DH-STAR (Zielonka et al., 2026) for a broader survey.
Video diffusion models for avatars. VDMs such as WAN-Animate-14B (Wan Team et al., 2025;
Cheng et al., 2025) synthesize temporally coherent, photorealistic human video at unprecedented
quality. Diffusion-based portrait-animation methods (Guo et al., 2024; Tian et al., 2024; Xie et al.,
2024; Zhao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025) condition these models on signals ranging from sparse
2D keypoints to dense 3DMM-derived renderings, but typically produce 2D avatars without explicit
camera control. DiffusionAvatars (Kirschstein et al., 2024a) trains a person-specific image-diffusion
model as the avatar. FaceCam (Lyu et al., 2026) adds camera control to a VDM, but lacks expression
control. DeX-Portrait (Wu et al., 2025) controls neck pose and expression, but not the camera. Fac-
torPortrait (Tang et al., 2025) is concurrent work that controls expression, head pose, and viewpoint
in a VDM. However, it trains on proprietary data using limited-quality 3DGS renderings as pseudo
ground truth, and no code or model is available.

3.1
EXPRESSION CONTROL AND THE DISTRACT BENCHMARK
3DMMs such as FLAME limit the detail and range of representable expressions, motivating the use
of learned expression encoders. A good encoder must preserve expression while discarding identity
and camera viewpoint. We introduce the DISTRACT benchmark, which directly measures how
strongly an encoder’s code is distracted by these confounders.
DISTRACT evaluates two invariance axes on NeRSemble (Kirschstein et al., 2023): viewpoint in-
variance (same identity and timestep, different camera) and identity invariance (same expression
across different identities, verified by manual inspection). Full details are in Section B.
Most encoders are heavily viewpoint-biased (Table 1 and Figure 3): X-NeMo reaches only 18%
Rank-1 on viewpoint invariance. Yet a small MLP probe on the same frozen latents raises this to
98%, confirming that expression information survives but is entangled with viewpoint, thus moti-
vating an explicit disentanglement module.

disentangler D splits it into an expression latent zex and a camera latent zcam, and an entangler E

combining a FLAME head with a SAM-3D-Body torso, that jointly encodes camera viewpoint and
body/neck pose. We color each pixel by the direction of its camera ray, i.e. the directional part of its
Plücker coordinates, blended with Lambertian shading. Crucially, head pixels express this direction
in the head’s local frame and torso pixels in a pelvis-aligned frame (Section F.2). The color contrast
between head and torso thus directly encodes neck pose, keeping camera viewpoint and neck rota-
tion separable. Rendering from the target camera πtar yields Sπtar and Mπtar, which are encoded by
Pskel and Pmesh (the latter zero-initialized) and added to the noisy video latents.

EXPERIMENTS
Implementation details. We fine-tune WAN-Animate-14B on curated multi-view data from
NeRSemble (Kirschstein et al., 2023) and AVA-256 (Martinez et al., 2024). Training takes 34 hours
on a single A100 GPU. The proxy-mesh head comes from monocular pixel3DMM (Giebenhain
et al., 2025) FLAME tracking; the body and neck from SAM-3D (Yang et al., 2026b). At inference
we perform 20 denoising steps. Full hyperparameters are in Section E.

4.1
EVALUATION SETUP
Baselines. We compare against the single-image 3DGS avatars LAM (He et al., 2025), Flex-
Avatar (Kirschstein et al., 2026), and UIKA (Wu et al., 2026), and against CAP4D (Taubner
et al., 2025b) and MVP4D (Taubner et al., 2025a), which distill FLAME-conditioned image- and
video-diffusion outputs, respectively, into a 3DGS avatar. For CAP4D, we report both the full
pipeline (CAP4D) and its diffusion model (MMDM). For computational reasons, we evaluate only
MVP4D’s video-diffusion model (MMVDM, labeled MVP4D in tables and figures). The camera-
controlled VDM FaceCam (Lyu et al., 2026) lacks expression control, so we include it only in the
multi-view consistency probe. Details in Section C.
Novel-View Reenactment Task. Our evaluation must simultaneously measure how well an avatar
preserves identity and expression and how multi-view consistent it is. We therefore make the pro-
tocol as viewpoint-challenging as possible: each evaluation triplet consists of a single reference
image Iref, a driving video, and a target video from different camera viewpoints, similar to the
camera-triplet setup of training (Section 3.4). We evaluate on BecomingLit (Schmidt et al., 2025)
(25 self-reenactment and 13 cross-reenactment clips) and Multiface (Wuu et al., 2022) (22 self-
reenactments), since no unified cross-method split exists for NeRSemble or AVA-256. Those held-
out datasets eliminate training-data leakage for all baselines. Details are in Section B.
3DGS Distillation. To explicitly measure multi-view consistency, we additionally fit a static 3DGS
scene from each diffusion-based method’s generations along a closed ellipse camera trajectory with
a frozen expression, and evaluate the reconstruction. We choose the static setting because dynamic
3DGS optimization is far less standardized and can itself introduce fitting flaws that would confound
the comparison. We render the fit at the 16 real rig cameras, none of which is part of the generated
trajectory, and compare against the captured images. Details are in Section B. Metric definitions are
given in Section A.

RESULTS
Human perception of faces is highly sensitive to temporal artifacts such as flicker, identity drift, and
unnatural motion, which single frames cannot convey. We therefore strongly encourage viewing the
supplementary video alongside the figures below.
Self-reenactment. VDAs outperform every baseline on every metric of both datasets (Table 2),
as well as on VFHQ-test (Xie et al., 2022) (Table 3, Figure 18 in the appendix), an in-the-wild
single-view benchmark testing generalization beyond the multi-view training distribution. Qualita-
tively (Figure 4), our results retain sharper identity detail and transfer subtle expressions and micro-
expressions, as well as rare expressions outside the FLAME space, which limits the baselines.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Unlike a 3DGS avatar, which renders in real time once built, VDAs pay the cost of VDM inference
for every clip (about 12 minutes for 77 frames at 576 × 1024 on one A100), and long sequences
are prone to temporal drift. Autoregressive video and world models (Liu et al., 2026; Samuel et al.,
2026; Zhu et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026), including a real-time variant of WAN-Animate-2 (Wang
et al., 2026), have shown that long, consistent generation can be made fast. Motivated by this, we
distill our model with Self Forcing (Huang et al., 2025) into a four-step auto-regressive VDM for
temporal consistency as a prototype (Section K). This unoptimized implementation generates 4.8
frames per second at 288 × 512 on one A100 and stays temporally stable over more than a thousand
frames, at the cost of a small loss in quality. Furthermore, lighting is baked into the reference image,
and we rely on a fixed expression space rather than training it end-to-end. Our recipe is anchored to
WAN-Animate-14B, although we expect it to transfer to other backbones, as well.

CONCLUSION
We showed that an avatar does not have to be a 3D asset: by making a video diffusion model con-
trollable in expression, pose, and camera viewpoint, VDAs turn its internet-scale prior into a single-
image avatar that is multi-view consistent and more faithful than both 3DGS-based and diffusion-

based avatars. Central to this is expression control through learned latent spaces: our recipe works
with different expression spaces, and those of image encoders capture expressions with higher fi-
delity than 3DMMs, but must first be disentangled from camera viewpoint for the best results. Train-
ing is affordable and accessible, taking 34 hours on a single GPU with public multi-view data only.
Because the prior is preserved rather than distilled into 3D, VDAs render what explicit renderers
struggle with (shadows, reflections, refractions, hair dynamics, and clothing that deforms with mo-
tion) and generalize to subjects, effects, and viewpoints absent from the training data. We see this
as evidence that controllable video models are a practical foundation for photorealistic avatars.
ETHICS STATEMENT
VDAs turn a single photograph into a photo-realistic, controllable video of the depicted person. The
same capability enables impersonation and non-consensual synthetic media. We train and evalu-
ate only on multi-view datasets whose participants gave informed consent and which their authors
released for research use (NeRSemble (Kirschstein et al., 2023), AVA-256 (Martinez et al., 2024),
BecomingLit (Schmidt et al., 2025), Multiface (Wuu et al., 2022)) and on the public VFHQ bench-

A
EVALUATION METRICS
We report PSNR and LPIPS over a face-only crop. We also report JOD, a perceptual video-quality
metric computed via pyfvvdp (Mantiuk et al., 2021) that captures temporal, video-level quality
beyond per-frame image metrics. We further report face-specific semantic metrics: identity preser-
vation as ArcFace (Deng et al., 2019) cosine similarity to the ground-truth target, and expression
similarity as the cosine similarity between the DECA (Feng et al., 2021) FLAME expression codes
of the generated and ground-truth frames, averaged over the clip. We deliberately score expres-
sion in a third-party space rather than in X-NeMo’s (Zhao et al., 2025) own motion embedding:
our method is driven by X-NeMo codes, so a cosine similarity in that same space would be a met-
ric we have a stake in. For pose accuracy we report keypoint distance normalized by inter-ocular
distance (AKD/IOD), and in the ablations additionally APD, the average rotational distance (in de-
grees) between the head poses estimated with the FAN3D landmark detector on the generated and
ground-truth frames.
To make these and other pixel-aligned metrics meaningful, we align the 3DMM present in ev-
ery compared model to the ground-truth 3DMM before computing them, removing an otherwise-
unresolved ambiguity. On the multi-view benchmarks this alignment is possible because a ground-
truth target view exists. On monocular VFHQ-test it does not, so there we follow the standard proto-
col for that dataset and align on detected facial keypoints instead, landmark-cropping every method’s
output to a common head scale and centre before scoring. That alignment is what makes the pixel
metrics comparable across methods whose outputs live in different frames, including methods that
render into the reference frame rather than reprojecting to a target camera. Multi-view consistency
is measured separately with the 3DGS probe (Section 4.2). Cross-reenactment has no ground-truth
target image, so the pixel metrics do not apply there. Identity is instead measured against the ref-
erence, and expression and pose against the driver’s own target-view video — the driver has the
correct expression and pose for that camera, only the wrong identity, which is exactly what those
two metrics need.

EVALUATION PROTOCOL DETAILS
DISTRACT.
DISTRACT is built on the FLAME-tracked sequences of NeRSemble (Kirschstein
et al., 2023) (all 16 cameras, sentence-reading sequences excluded). Each query has one positive
and four distractors. In the viewpoint-invariance task (500 queries), query and positive are two
cameras of the same identity, sequence and timestep. In the identity-invariance task, the positive is
the nearest frame of another identity in FLAME expression and jaw parameters; of 456 mined sets,
we kept the 126 whose positive we manually verified to show the query’s expression. Distractors are
random frames that share with the query its identity and camera (A), only its identity (B), only its
camera (C), or neither (D). Codes are compared by cosine distance; Rank-1 is the fraction of queries
for which no distractor is closer than the positive, and Err-X the fraction for which distractor X is.
The MLP probe (two layers, supervised contrastive loss (Khosla et al., 2020), all views of one frame
form a class) is fitted on each encoder’s NeRSemble embeddings, which include the benchmark
frames; it is thus transductive and measures how much view-invariant expression information the
frozen latents contain, not generalization to unseen subjects.
Reenactment evaluation.
BecomingLit (Schmidt et al., 2025) and Multiface (Wuu et al., 2022)
are part of neither our training data nor that of any compared method; we avoid NeRSemble and
AVA-256 because train/test splits differ between methods. Each case is a camera triplet, and all
methods receive the same reference image, driving video and target calibration; expression is es-
timated from the driving video only, and multi-view tracking only places the head under πtar. Be-
comingLit has 25 self-reenactment cases from 13 identities, with a near-frontal reference camera
and driving and target cameras 35–101◦apart in azimuth (median 77◦). Its 13 cross-reenactment

still below ours on all metrics. UIKA and FlexAvatar are rendered directly; FlexAvatar uses its feed-

TRAINING-FREE USE OF THE DISENTANGLED CODES
The disentangler of Section 3.1 splits an expression encoder’s raw code into an expression latent zex
and a camera latent zcam, and the entangler maps any pair back into the encoder’s native code space.
Both generators below stay frozen: we only change the code they receive.
X-NeMo.
Keeping zex fixed and sweeping zcam through the codes of other camera views rotates
the generated head while the expression is preserved, both for a constant and for an extreme driving
expression (Figure 12). X-NeMo cannot distinguish a camera change from a neck rotation, so the
camera code moves the head rather than the viewpoint. This is why our avatar model takes camera
and body pose from the proxy mesh instead (Section 3.3).
WAN-Animate.
WAN-Animate’s native expression codes carry the viewpoint of the frame they
are extracted from. When the target view differs from the expression source, rendering with the
raw codes produces subtle but visible expression artifacts (parted lips, a shifted mouth shape) that
vary along the camera path. Re-entangling our disentangled expression latent with the target view’s
camera code removes them (Figure 13). The remaining artifacts of this training-free variant, and the
coarse camera control it offers, motivate the camera-triplet fine-tuning of Section 3.

EMERGENT VIEW INVARIANCE
Camera-triplet supervision alone makes the expression code view-invariant. We compare the two
runs marked † in Table 5, which differ only in whether the driving expression code is read from a
different camera than the reconstructed frame (cross-view) or from the same camera (self-driven),
and track DISTRACT viewpoint-invariance rank-1 accuracy of the expression code at the face-
encoder output during training (Figure 14). Both start at 0.244; cross-view training climbs to 0.792,
whereas self-driven training stalls at 0.486. Since the proxy mesh already fixes the target camera,
viewpoint information in the expression code is never needed; under cross-view supervision it is
actively misleading, and training suppresses it. The gain carries over to generation quality (Table 5).

STREAMING DISTILLATION PROTOTYPE
As a proof of concept for fast and long generation, we distill our model with Self Forcing (Huang
et al., 2025) into a causal, four-step student. The student shares our architecture, weights, and
conditioning; we only train rank-128 LoRA adapters on the transformer and the face-fuser blocks,
and make attention block-causal over blocks of four latent frames (16 video frames). Training
follows the two stages of Self Forcing, an ODE initialization (Yin et al., 2025) on 1,500 teacher
trajectories followed by distribution matching distillation (Yin et al., 2024b;a) on self-generated
videos, at 288 × 512 and takes about 60 A100 GPU hours. At inference, the student streams the
video block by block with a rolling key–value cache and two frame sinks (Xiao et al., 2024; Yang
et al., 2026a), the reference latent and the first generated frames, so that memory is constant in the
video length.
On one A100, the student generates 4.8 frames per second at 288 × 512: a 41-second video (1029
frames) takes about four minutes. Table 9 and Figure 15 compare the student with our full model on
BecomingLit self-reenactment: it denoises a clip about 11× faster and matches identity, expression,
and keypoint accuracy at a moderate loss in image quality. On 41-second videos of 11 held-out sub-
jects, identity and appearance remain stable to the last frame, and expression fidelity stays constant
while chunked generation in overlapping windows degrades over time. The student is somewhat
behind our full model in image quality and runs below real time; training at higher resolution and
system-level optimizations are natural next steps.
