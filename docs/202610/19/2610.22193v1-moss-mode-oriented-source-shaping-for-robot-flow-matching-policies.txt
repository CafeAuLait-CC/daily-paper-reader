MOSS: Mode-Oriented Source Shaping for Robot
Flow Matching Policies
Zirun Ma*1,2, Jindou Jia*1, Tuo An1, Jiaqi Bai1, Jiahua Dong1, Jianfei Yang†1
1MARS Lab, Nanyang Technological University, 2Tsinghua University
* Equal contribution, † Corresponding author.
In robotic manipulation, the same observation often admits multiple feasible actions. Generative
policies such as diffusion and flow matching naturally capture such multimodal behaviors, yet which
mode is executed is left to random sampling and cannot be actively controlled. We propose Mode-
Oriented Source Shaping (MOSS), which turns mode selection into an explicit choice of where generation
starts. We first reveal, both empirically and theoretically, that deterministic flow transport induces
mode-dependent structure in the source space. Under ideal deterministic ODE transport, distinct
behavioral modes have distinct source-space preimages, while practical approximation errors can blur
their boundaries due to imbalanced data, limited model capacity, and few inference steps. Motivated
by this source-space structure, MOSS constructs semantically aligned mode-specific Gaussian sources
that encode mode identity in the initial noise. A modality-strength estimator predicts the local degree
of multimodality, which adaptively controls the geometry of the mode-specific sources. It contracts
them toward a shared Gaussian where behaviors are similar and separates them near behavioral
branching points. During deployment, the desired behavior is selected at inference simply by sampling
from the corresponding component. Across 6 simulated and 3 real-world manipulation tasks, MOSS
steers to the target mode up to 100% of the time with high task success, recovers minority modes at
over 90% guidance rate under a 10:90 imbalance dataset, and surpasses mode-conditioned flow policy
by 43.3 points of guidance rate on real robots.

Introduction
Generative models have recently become a prominent paradigm in robot imitation learning (Pearce et al.,
2023; Physical Intelligence et al., 2026). Unlike conventional policies that regress toward an optimal action,
representative approaches such as Diffusion Policy (DP) (Chi et al., 2023) and Flow Matching (FM) (Lipman
et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2026) generate action trajectories from random noise, allowing them to represent
multiple feasible behaviors under the same observation, i.e., multimodal behaviors (Zhai et al., 2025). Here, a
behavior mode denotes a semantically coherent family of action sequences that follow the same qualitative
strategy for accomplishing the task (Chi et al., 2023). For instance, a robot may approach the same object
from the left or the right, or interact with different targets in different orders, while still completing the task
successfully. However, in existing generative policies, which mode is ultimately executed is largely determined
implicitly by random sampling (Lipman et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2026). This limits their applicability in
settings that require user-specified preferences, test-time constraints, or human intervention (Zoellner et al.,
2026; He et al., 2025).
Existing solutions fall into two categories. The straightforward one explicitly conditions the generative
model on a mode indicator, in practice most often a language instruction, so that different conditions induce
different action distributions (Yang et al., 2026; Zhang and Gienger, 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Such conditioning,
however, often fails to steer the policy reliably when the training data are imbalanced (Fang et al., 2026),
since high-dimensional visual scenes are strongly correlated with task execution, the model tends to learn
scene-action shortcuts that bypass the weaker, low-dimensional language condition (Ren et al., 2026). The
other line instead operates on the source distribution of FM, controlling multimodal behaviors by implicitly

explicit semantic correspondence to behavioral modes, so aligning them with user-defined modes still demands
additional networks and laborious post-alignment (Zhang et al., 2026).
Toward a more reliable handle on mode selection, we first analyze how a well-trained FM policy behaves on
multimodal tasks. We find that, even with a standard isotropic Gaussian source, the final behavior does
not arise randomly from the entire noise space. Under deterministic Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE)
transport, different behavioral modes tend to occupy distinct, albeit noticeably entangled, regions of the source
space. We further show theoretically that, under ideal transport conditions, such mode-specific initial-noise
regions are guaranteed to be implicitly separated. In practice, however, imbalanced mode distributions, limited
model capacity, and a restricted number of inference steps leave these partitions interleaved, weakly separated,
or severely imbalanced, sometimes resulting in mode preference or even mode collapse (Zhai et al., 2025;
Mazza et al., 2026). This raises a natural question: can we make the existing partitioning more explicit, and
steer behavior directly through the initial distribution?
Based on this insight, we propose Mode-Oriented Source Shaping, termed MOSS, which enables controllable
multimodal generation by dynamically shaping the source distribution of FM, as shown in Figure 1. Specifically,
we first construct semantically defined mode-specific Gaussian sources, explicitly encoding mode identity
into the initial noise and making the resulting control more robust to imbalanced mode distributions, limited
model capacity, and limited inference steps. Since multiple modes typically arise only during certain critical
phases rather than throughout the entire task (Jia et al., 2026a), a modality-strength estimator then predicts
the local degree of multimodality from previous observation and action history, and uses it to continuously
modulate the separability of the Gaussian components. When demonstrations from different modes behave
alike, the components contract toward a shared Gaussian and maximize data sharing. As the robot approaches
a behavioral branch, they gradually separate and give the initial noise a clear, stable mode identity.
MOSS provides several practical advantages beyond explicit mode selection. First, when different modes
in the training demonstrations are highly imbalanced, the generation probabilities of standard FM tend to
inherit the empirical data distribution. In contrast, our method can directly adjust the deployment probability
of each mode by changing the source sampling distribution. Second, for data-scarce modes, we find that the
proposed strategy can effectively leverage shared trajectory data from other modes, allowing the policy to
learn more stable common motion structures while preserving clear mode boundaries, thereby improving both
sample efficiency and task success for minority modes. Finally, compared with methods that apply strong
guidance to shift the learned vector field at inference time, mode-specific sources still initialize transport
2


from regions already established during training. This can potentially reduce trajectory-quality degradation
caused by inference-time guidance (Yu et al., 2026), while naturally extending to currently emerging one-step
denoising algorithms that do not support guidance (Ho and Salimans, 2022; Jia et al., 2026b).
We systematically evaluate our method on multiple robotic manipulation tasks with multimodal structure,
including 6 simulation tasks and 3 real-world robot tasks. Compared with standard FM (Lipman et al.,
2023), explicit mode-conditioned FM (Lei et al., 2026), and source-based methods (Zhang et al., 2026; Luo
et al., 2026), MOSS achieves up to 100% target-mode steering while maintaining high task success rate (SR).
Even under a highly imbalanced mode distribution (10 : 90), our method can reliably recover the minority
mode, achieving over 90% guidance rate (GR). Ablations show that adaptive source geometry improves GR
over fixed Gaussian separation by 12-14 percentage points in average GR at the same training budget. On
real-world tasks, MOSS achieves an average GR/SR of 84.2%/85.3%, outperforming mode-conditioned FM by
43.3 and 12.0 percentage points in average GR and SR, respectively. These results validate the effectiveness
of source-space control under visual noise, limited demonstrations, and execution errors.

Related work
Multimodal generative policies.
Generative policies such as diffusion (Chi et al., 2023) and FM (Lipman
et al., 2023) naturally model multimodal action distributions through stochastic generation. However, different
behavioral modes can still become entangled or collapse during generation (Zhai et al., 2025; Mazza et al.,
2026). Recent methods improve multimodal modeling through additional structure. VFP (Zhai et al., 2025)
combines a variational latent prior, optimal transport, and a MoE decoder to better separate action modes,
while MARS (Jia et al., 2026a) adaptively switches between stochastic and deterministic generation according
to multimodality degree. None of these methods can actively control multimodality.
Source shaping for flow matching.
Standard FM-based policies (Lipman et al., 2023) commonly initialize
transport from a simple Gaussian source. Recent works instead design the source distribution to improve
efficiency or structural alignment. A2A (Jia et al., 2026b) initializes flow from action-history representations
to enable shorter transport and one-step inference; VITA (Gao et al., 2026) uses visual latents as the flow
source; and LAFM (Machado et al., 2026) selects learned source priors according to latent actions. These
methods primarily target efficient or better-aligned transport rather than explicit mode-level control. More
recently, SL-FM (Zhang et al., 2026) separates behavioral branches through orthogonal source lifting while
retaining a shared velocity field. MOSS similarly controls behavior through the source space, but explicitly
aligns source components with semantic behavior modes and adaptively adjusts their separation according to
local multimodality.
Explicit conditioning for behavioral control.
Explicit conditioning provides a direct mechanism for controlling
multimodal action generation by injecting the desired behavioral intent into the policy. Large VLA models
such as RDT and π0.5 (Liu et al., 2025; Physical Intelligence et al., 2026, 2025) use language and visual-
semantic conditions to guide action generation. Lightweight generative policies can instead introduce explicit
discrete mode variables; for example, PF-DAG (Lei et al., 2026) predicts a primary mode and generates
continuous actions with a mode-conditioned MeanFlow policy. While effective, such explicit conditioning may
introduce shortcut solutions, where the policy relies heavily on the injected mode signal rather than organizing
multimodal behaviors intrinsically. In contrast, MOSS controls behavior through the source distribution
without exposing the mode variable to the policy itself, and our experiments show source-based mode control
yields stronger controllability than directly injecting the mode variable into the velocity-field condition.

Every practical factor pushes in the same direction: finite demonstrations make velocity estimation unreliable
in sparsely covered regions, mode imbalance further removes supervision from minority behaviors, a network
of bounded Lipschitz constant cannot reproduce the sharp velocity variation that a clean mode separation
demands (Mazza et al., 2026), and finite-step integration adds numerical error. The resulting growth of ηo
widens the ambiguous region and manifests during sampling as mode intersection, mode preference, or even
local mode collapse. This exposes a fundamental limitation of leaving noise zoning to be learned implicitly
from a standard Gaussian source: the existence of an ideal mode partition does not imply that sufficiently
clear, stable, and controllable source regions emerge under finite data, limited model capacity, and finite-step
inference.
4
Mode-Oriented Source Shaping
4.1
Overview
Section 3 shows that FM naturally induces mode-dependent regions in the source space, but this separation is
implicit and can become weak or entangled in practice. Rather than recovering these implicit regions, MOSS
deliberately imposes a simple, semantically aligned source geometry to make mode separation explicit and
controllable. It enlarges the source-space separation between modes when it is actually needed, and selects
the mode by choosing where the transport starts. How much separation is needed depends on the state: when
the upcoming behavior is highly multimodal, MOSS places the mode-specific sources far apart; when the
behavior is essentially determined, it lets them contract back toward a shared Gaussian. Figure 1 depicts its
architecture.
Concretely, given an observation history o, a target action chunk y, and a mode label k, MOSS predicts a
modality strength q ∈[0, 1] for the current state and draws the source sample x0 from the corresponding mode-
specific component. In our implementation, we use σmin = 0. Training then follows standard FM (Lipman
et al., 2023), with a single velocity field shared by all modes:

Conclusion
We presented MOSS, a source-space approach for controllable multimodal FM policies. Our analysis shows
that ideal deterministic FM transport naturally induces mode-dependent noise regions, while practical
9


approximation errors can blur these implicit partitions and lead to mode preference or collapse. MOSS
makes this structure explicit by associating semantic behavioral modes with mode-specific source distributions
and adaptively adjusting their separation according to local multimodality, without conditioning the shared
velocity field on the requested mode. Across simulated and real-world manipulation tasks, MOSS achieves
strong mode controllability and task success rate, extends naturally to multiple behavioral modes, and
improves minority-mode learning under highly imbalanced demonstrations. Together, these results highlight
source-space geometry as an effective design dimension for controllable generative robot policies.
7
Limitations
MOSS has two main limitations. First, the current formulation assumes a fixed set of discrete behavioral
modes specified before training, and therefore does not directly support open-ended mode control or continuous
behavioral variation. Extending MOSS toward dynamic mode discovery and continuous mode spaces remains
an important direction for future work.
Second, although the source-space formulation is in principle
compatible with larger generative robot policies, its performance in such large-scale settings remains to be
evaluated.

Visualization of Initial-Noise Mode Separation
We investigate whether the initial noise of a standard FM policy is predictive of the behavioral mode ultimately
executed by the policy. We conduct the experiment on 2D Navigation, where a DiT-based FM policy can
reach the goal through four feasible routes, denoted as Mode 1 – Mode 4.
We perform 1,000 closed-loop rollouts using the standard Gaussian source distribution N(0, I). For each
rollout, we record the initial noise of the first executed action chunk, which contains 8 two-dimensional actions.
Since route selection is primarily reflected in vertical motion, we retain only the y-dimension, resulting in an
eight-dimensional noise feature for each rollout.
A rollout is considered successful if its final position is within 0.06 of the goal at (1.8, 0.5). Successful
trajectories are assigned mode labels according to their mean vertical coordinate in the wall-crossing region
x ∈[0.78, 0.85]:

Mode 4, respectively.
For visualization, the eight-dimensional noise features are standardized and projected to two dimensions using
LDA fitted with the trajectory-derived mode labels. LDA identifies directions that maximize between-mode
relative to within-mode variation. Each point in Figure 2 represents the initial noise of one successful rollout,
colored by its resulting behavioral mode, while the ellipses show the nominal 95% Gaussian covariance contours
of each mode.
We further quantify the semantic structure of this source-space partition. For each mode, we compute the
mean of its initial-noise features. The mean y-noise values for Mode 1 – Mode 4 are −0.303, −0.182, 0.135,
and 0.286, respectively, increasing monotonically with the vertical location of the corresponding route. This
suggests that the source-space organization learned by standard FM is aligned with the geometry of the
resulting behaviors.
Together, these results show that a standard FM policy already develops a mode-relevant organization of its
initial-noise space, despite being trained without explicit mode conditioning. However, the substantial overlap
between neighboring modes also shows that this naturally emerging partition is insufficient for reliable mode
control. This observation motivates MOSS to explicitly shape and separate mode-specific source regions for
controllable multimodal generation.
To test whether this structure can directly influence generation, we construct a Gaussian source centered at
each mode-specific mean while retaining the standard deviation of the original source, σ = 1, and perform
100 rollouts per source. The resulting mode distributions are reported in Table 4. Sampling around different
mode-specific means induces distinct biases in the generated behaviors. In particular, the sources centered at
the Mode 1 and Mode 4 means produce their corresponding modes in 62% and 56% of rollouts, respectively.
The intermediate means yield more overlapping mode distributions, indicating that the naturally emerging
source partition is semantically structured but not cleanly separated.

Tasks and Mode Definitions
We evaluate MOSS on 6 simulated tasks and 3 real-world tasks. The 4 bimodal simulation tasks are Push
Cube, Grasp Eyeglasses, Stack Cube, and Put Wine. The 2 multi-mode tasks are 2D Navigation and Place
Cube. The real-world experiments comprise Place Cups, Pick Veg, and Open Drawer. Mode annotations
identify distinct successful strategies rather than task success itself.

Mode-conditioned FM.
Our goal is to compare source-space mode control against direct mode conditioning
under the same FM policy backbone. PF-DAG (Lei et al., 2026) contains a mode-conditioned action-generation
stage, but its complete pipeline additionally predicts the primary mode, addressing the distinct problem of
automatically inferring multimodal structure. Since our setting assumes that the desired semantic mode
is explicitly specified at deployment, we isolate the mode-conditioned generation mechanism and directly
provide the requested mode label to the FM policy. Specifically, the mode label is embedded and injected
as an additional condition into the velocity network throughout transport. MOSS and Mode-conditioned
FM use the same visual encoder, velocity-field architecture, action horizon, optimizer, training data, and
evaluation protocol. The comparison is therefore intended to isolate where the given mode information enters
the generative process—through the velocity field or through the source distribution—rather than to compare
against the full PF-DAG system.

Imbalanced Training and Data Efficiency
For each task, mode 1 is treated as the minority mode. MOSS and Mode-conditioned FM are trained on
10 mode-1 and 90 mode-2 demonstrations, whereas unconditional FM-DiT is trained only on the same 10
mode-1 demonstrations. Evaluation always requests mode 1.
Each method is trained independently. At each checkpoint, we evaluate the requested mode and report its SR
and GR. The solid curves in Figure 7 show the mean GR across the 3 seeds, while the shaded regions indicate
±1 standard deviation computed from the seed-wise results.
The horizontal axis reports the cumulative number of sampled minority-mode training windows rather than
the epoch index. This normalization allows the methods to be compared according to their exposure to
target-mode data, while MOSS can additionally benefit from the majority-mode demonstrations.

Additional Ablation Studies
H.1
Ablation Variants
The ablations in Table 3 compare 4 source-distribution variants to the source distribution:
• Single Gaussian: a standard Gaussian is partitioned by the sign of its projection onto the mode axis at a
selected action position, without displaced Gaussian centers.
• Single-step separation: mode-specific Gaussian displacement is applied only at the branching point.
• Without adaptation: fixed mode-specific source separation is applied across the complete action chunk.
• MOSS: full-chunk separation is adjusted using the estimated modality strength.
The single-position variants leave most source dimensions shared, whereas the full-chunk variants maintain a
consistent mode identity across the predicted action sequence.
