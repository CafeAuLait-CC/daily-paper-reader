ABSTRACT
Scalable approaches to learning neural dynamical systems have placed diffusion,
flow matching, and few-step generators in flow maps at the forefront of generative
modeling. Such state-of-the-art models have limited access to explicit model densi-
ties despite evolving from classical generative modeling approaches that leveraged
tractable likelihoods, e.g., Normalizing Flows and Autoregressive models. In
this paper, we combine these threads of few-step generation and tractable sample
likelihoods and introduce NORMALIZING FLOW MAPS (NFM), a class of exactly
invertible pushforward maps that generalizes normalizing flows and flow maps.
More precisely, NFMs learn the integrator of an underlying dynamical system, but
crucially, they provide efficient exact likelihood evaluation by construction. To en-
able scalable training of NFMs, we derive two complementary axes of consistency
inherent to NFMs that yield (self) distillation objectives: (1) pointwise matching,
which unlocks ℓ2-based flow-map style regression losses, and (2) distributional
matching, which matches pushforward distributions to the marginals of a prescribed
forward process. On conditional image generation on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet-64,
NFMs achieve state-of-the-art sample quality among parameter-matched
normalizing flows, while supporting likelihood-based RLHF. For equilibrium
sampling in Boltzmann Generators, NFMs outperform prior state-of-the-art
normalizing flows on both single-system and transferable peptide benchmarks.

INTRODUCTION
Learning a transport map between a tractable reference distribution and the empirical distribution
of complex data has become the de facto paradigm in generative modeling, with varied application
domains spanning audiovisual media (Ho et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023a), biomolecular model-
ing (Abramson et al., 2024; Watson et al., 2023), and scientific simulation (Shu et al., 2023; Du et al.,
2024). Driving this widespread adoption is the ability to leverage standard alignment techniques such
as best-of-N selection (Ramesh et al., 2021), inference-time steering (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Ho &
Salimans, 2022; Bansal et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2025), and post-training algorithms (Black et al., 2024;
Liu et al., 2025a) which effectively sharpen the model distribution—by increasing sample rewards
assessed by an oracle. However, each generated sample necessitates computationally expensive
numerical integration of an ODE or SDE—producing a substantial inference tax that computationally
challenges the steering and post-training pipeline (Wohlwend et al., 2025; Scarpellini et al., 2026).
Indeed, while typical Autoregressive LLMs have a mature post-training stack—e.g., PPO (Schulman
et al., 2017), GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), etc.—corresponding algorithmic frameworks for diffusion and
flow model post-training are both vast and varied (Wallace et al., 2024; Prabhudesai et al., 2023; Clark
et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2023). The crucial distinction is that LLMs natively provide tractable sample
likelihoods, whereas evaluating exact densities for diffusion and flow-matching models is computation-
ally challenging. Specifically, evaluating sample likelihoods of flow models requires integrating the

Jacobian, and necessitates stochastic trace estimation (Chen et al., 2018; Meyer et al., 2021). Instead, it
is computationally more attractive to target the reward-tilted distribution, by modifying the drift of the
underlying dynamics through path-space objectives. These objectives, however, crucially rely on high-
variance value estimates of the terminal reward. More precisely, value function estimation necessitates
propagating terminal rewards through noisy intermediate states, which requires backpropagating
through time (Ben-Hamu et al., 2024)–or a noisy single-step denoising approximation (Blasingame
& Liu, 2025; Lee et al., 2026b). These algorithmic features combine and give rise to tilting objectives
that are inherently high variance, often leading to unstable post-training and mode collapse.
As generative models are deployed at scale, the cumulative cost of repeated sampling for generation,
steering, and post-training makes inference efficiency a central design consideration. This practical
motivation has led to the emergence of flow maps (Boffi et al., 2025a; Geng et al., 2025; Boffi et al.,
2025b), that directly learn the flow integrator, effectively compressing numerical simulation into few
network evaluations. Although the exact ODE flow is a diffeomorphism, standard parameterizations
are not invertible, nor do they provide tractable Jacobian determinants. While flow maps reduce the
inference tax, they do not resolve the crucial likelihood-access gap—limiting their usage in scientific
applications like Boltzmann Generators (Noé et al., 2019). As a result, classical Normalizing
Flows (NF) (Tabak & Vanden-Eijnden, 2010; Tabak & Turner, 2013; Dinh et al., 2017; Rezende &
Mohamed, 2015) trained with MLE remain an attractive exact likelihood-based model class. However,
their practical deployment critically relies on noise injection into the clean data distribution for stable
training, which unfortunately eliminates the promised access to cheap sample likelihood (Chen et al.,
2026). Taken together, these considerations motivate a new generative model that combines few-step
sampling of flow maps with exact sample likelihoods for scalable, likelihood-based post-training.
Current work. In this paper, we introduce NORMALIZING FLOW MAPS (NFM), a new generative
model family, depicted in fig. 1, that is purpose-built to deliver two objectives of interest: 1.)
Exact sample likelihoods and 2.) Few-step inference; thereby generalizing classical invertible normal-
izing flows and endowing flow maps with tractable likelihoods. An NFM defines a time-conditioned
generative transport operator that is tied to the law of a known stochastic interpolant. However, unlike
existing model classes, NFM by construction defines a family of tractable invertible maps connecting
arbitrary points along a prescribed probability path. Consequently, NFMs provide exact sample
likelihoods without expensive numerical integration. Importantly, restricting this family to the prior
and data endpoints recovers a normalizing flow, and querying arbitrary time pairs yields the few-step
transport characteristics of a flow map. As a result, NFMs are naturally suited for applications that
require sample likelihoods, such as Boltzmann generators (Noé et al., 2019) and RLHF post-training.
To train NFMs, we operationalize two complementary matching principles inherent to NFMs, which
in turn lead to self-distillation training objectives. In particular, our key insight is that NFMs must
satisfy a known stochastic interpolant at a pointwise level and/or match its marginal distribution.
The former condition unlocks regression-based training common in flow maps, but adapted to
NFM, while the latter is an altogether new algorithmic framework that trains few-step generators
by exploiting NFMs family of (end-point) invertible maps. We summarize our main contributions:

METHOD
We introduce NORMALIZING FLOW MAPS (NFM), a new generative model class built from
two-time pushforward maps that learn generative transport along the probability path of a stochastic
interpolant. More precisely, NFMs generalize classical normalizing flows across time, combining
the large transport-step capabilities of jump operators while endowing previous flow maps with
tractable inverses that enable exact sample likelihoods, i.e., p0(x0). We first define the model and
its parameterizations (§3.1), then expose the inherent computational properties at the particle and
distributional level. These principles yield (self)-distillation objectives (§3.3) resulting in NFM-R
that is constructed from pointwise matching of the probability flow and NFM-D that leverages
distributional matching of its marginals. Finally, we demonstrate RLHF-based post-training in §3.4.

RELATED WORK
Normalizing flows. NFs (Tabak & Vanden-Eijnden, 2010; Tabak & Turner, 2013; Rezende &
Mohamed, 2015; Dinh et al., 2017) are invertible generative models whose exact likelihood can be
evaluated via the change-of-variables formula. Despite this appealing property, scaling to tasks like
image generation remains challenging due to numerical instabilities (Xu & Campbell, 2023; Andrade,
2024) and architectural constraints (Chen et al., 2019). Recently, TARFlows (Zhai et al., 2025) have
demonstrated impressive capabilities in image (Gu et al., 2026a; Shen et al., 2026) and video genera-
tion (Gu et al., 2026c), as well as in sampling from Boltzmann distributions (Midgley et al., 2023; Tan
et al., 2025a;b). While TARFlow is trained via noise augmented MLE (Loaiza-Ganem et al., 2023;
Zhai et al., 2025), alternative training strategies include training over a probability path (Kim et al.,
2020; Chen et al., 2026; Gu et al., 2026b), and direct regression-based training (Rehman et al., 2026b).
Stochastic interpolants and flow maps. Diffusion (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; Ho et al., 2020; Song
et al., 2021) and Flow Matching (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023b; Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden,
2023; Tong et al., 2024) models have achieved state-of-the-art sample fidelity across numerous
modalities (Rombach et al., 2022; Abramson et al., 2024; Zeni et al., 2025). Generating samples and
evaluating likelihoods with these models typically require costly numerical integration (Salimans
& Ho, 2022; Chen et al., 2018; Grathwohl et al., 2018), motivating the development of few-step
models (Song et al., 2023; Song & Dhariwal, 2024; Frans et al., 2025; Geng et al., 2025; 2026), and the
flow map framework (Boffi et al., 2025a;b; Sabour et al., 2025). While flow maps accelerate sampling,
likelihood evaluation remains costly (Rehman et al., 2026a). Alternatively, proxy likelihood evaluation
can be amortized using an auxiliary divergence predictor (Ai et al., 2026; OuYang et al., 2026).
6
CONCLUSION
In this paper, we introduce NORMALIZING FLOW MAPS, a family of invertible transport maps
that combine few-step sampling with exact change-of-variables densities for one-step endpoint
samples. We demonstrate the application of these cheap likelihoods in Boltzmann generators through
importance sampling, and straightforward RL-based post-training of image generation models.
Furthermore, we demonstrate superior sample quality to existing NF methods and avoid the use
of Tweedie denoising which requires spatial gradients and breaks invertibility guarantees. While
NFM-R obtains the highest sample quality, it is prone to becoming numerically non-invertible, which
impedes RL methods relying on the score-function or KL-regularization. This points to the importance
of designing training schemes that enjoy the benefits of both pointwise and distributional consistency.

ETHICS STATEMENT
Our method is a general purpose generative modeling approach. In the image experiments, we post-
train class-conditional generators using text–to–image reward models. These rewards are imperfect
proxies for human preferences: optimizing them may amplify biases in the reward models or favor
images that score well without matching a user’s intent. In the molecular experiments, we evaluate
equilibrium conformation sampling on existing peptide benchmarks using molecular dynamics data.
The molecules studied in this work are considered safe and non-toxic. Applying the method to other
molecular systems would require evaluation specific to those uses.

EXACT LIKELIHOOD WITHOUT TWEEDIE DENOISING
In Figure 5, we compare guided MNIST samples from an iTARFlow trained via MLE without
additive Gaussian noise and an iTARFlow trained with Gaussian noise augmentation and Tweedie
denoising at inference time. In this experiment, training without noise produces visibly poor samples,
despite retaining the flow’s exact invertibility condition. Noise augmentation followed by denoising
substantially improves sample quality. However, the flow’s tractable density describes its outputs
before denoising. The subsequent Tweedie transformation is not guaranteed to be invertible, and its
Jacobian determinant is not accounted for by the flow’s likelihood calculation. Consequently, the
likelihood of the denoised output distribution is not provided by the original flow. This comparison
illustrates the practical difficulty, in our setting, of obtaining both high-quality samples and tractable
exact likelihoods for the same output distribution.

EXTENDED BACKGROUND ON BOLTZMANN GENERATORS
Sampling from equilibrium distributions is a fundamental problem in statistical physics (Frenkel &
Smit, 1996; Tuckerman, 2023), since equilibrium configurations provide access to thermodynamic
observables and characterize the macroscopic behavior of physical systems (Pathria, 2017). For
a configuration x with energy E(x) at temperature T, the equilibrium distribution is given by the
Boltzmann distribution

A particularly important instance of this problem is the sampling of molecular configurations (Zucker-
man, 2011). Equilibrium ensembles are required to characterize thermodynamic and conformational
properties of molecular systems and are relevant to applications including biomolecular modeling
and drug discovery (Frenkel & Smit, 1996). Sampling such systems is challenging because their
energy landscapes are typically high-dimensional and multimodal, with metastable regions separated
by large free-energy barriers. As a consequence, local sampling methods such as molecular dynamics
(MD) (Alder & Wainwright, 1959) and Markov chain Monte Carlo can exhibit long autocorrelation
times and slow transitions between metastable states (Wirnsberger et al., 2020; E et al., 2002).
Boltzmann Generators (BGs) (Noé et al., 2019) address this challenge by learning a tractable
generative model that approximates the target Boltzmann distribution. In particular, the model defines
a distribution pθ(x) such that

while providing both a mechanism for efficiently drawing samples x ∼pθ and a tractable evaluation
of their density pθ(x). The former enables direct generation of global proposals in configuration
space, while the latter enables the use of Monte Carlo correction methods to account for discrepancies
between the learned proposal distribution and the target distribution.
One such correction is self-normalized importance sampling (SNIS) (Liu, 2001). For an observable

D.1
TRANSFORMER AUTOREGRESSIVE FLOWS
NFMs are compatible with any NF architecture, however, we focus on Transformer Autoregressive
Flows (TARFlows) as they have been established as the state-of-the-art NF architectue in terms of
sample fidelity and scalability. TARFlows construct exactly-invertible networks by composing affine

D.2
NFM ARCHITECTURE
Both NFM-D and NFM-R adopt modified versions of the iTARFlow architecture (Lu et al., 2026b).
NFM-D introduces time-conditioning via a sinusoidal timestep embedding module and injects this
conditioning along with the class embedding into both the attention and the adaptive layernorm (Pee-
bles & Xie, 2023) modules within each block.
NFM-R adapts iTARFlow to more closely follow the iMF architecture. The major changes are
completely eliminating adaptive layer norm modules in favor of in-context conditioning and adopt-
ing general-purpose Transformer improvements: SwiGLU (Shazeer, 2020), RMSnorm (Zhang &
Sennrich, 2019), RoPE (Su et al., 2024) and QK-normalization.
In-context conditioning. Following iMF we abandon adaLN-zero (Peebles & Xie, 2023) in favor
of in-context conditioning with multiple tokens for each condition. This change is particularly
helpful given the need for our image models to condition on class, two timesteps and several CFG
related conditions (c.f. § A.1), something which is better handled by introducing all conditioning into
attention than simply summing their embeddings in an adaLN-zero module. Furthermore, adaLN-zero
is known to be parameter-heavy (Geng et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026b); so this change potentially
enables NFM-R to be more parameter efficient. Key to making in-context conditioning competitive
with adaLN-zero is the use of multiple tokens per condition. Given a condition α ∈R, we jointly
learn its embedding map gθ : R 7→Rn and m embedding offsets {βi}m
i=1, βi ∈Rn. Then α’s

D.3.3
NUMERICAL INVERTIBILITY
Noise annealing and FP64 inference. Theoretically, NFM-R should be exactly invertible; however,
in practice, its regression objective combined with the denoiser parametrization can lead to a loss
of numerical invertibility. As a mitigation strategy, we apply additive Gaussian noise to x0 before
computing the interpolant and anneal its contribution to zero over the course of training (see §D.4).
Through this strategy, it is possible to recover numerical invertibility in FP64 at inference. This
strategy enabled the NFM-R CIFAR-10 to be finetuned with REINFORCE to preferentially produce
green frogs (§ E.1.1).

Multi-step NFM-D. For two-step generation on CIFAR-10, we train the gated dual-time
parametrization using equally weighted one-time and composed likelihood objectives,
L = 1
2LD + 1
2LD,2.
Both objectives are evaluated on the full minibatch from the start of training. In the composed
objective, the terminal intermediate-to-prior map uses detached current parameters, while retaining
gradients through its input. For the one-time objective, we sample u ∼U[0, 1]. For the composed
objective, we independently draw two uniform times and order them as 0 ≤t < s ≤1. The two
objectives share the same class-dropout mask, with labels removed independently with probability 0.1.
At inference, we use the final checkpoint after 1000 training epochs with EMA decay 0.9999. Sam-
pling follows 1 →0.75 →0: the first step is unconditional, while the second uses a CFG scale of 3.2.
