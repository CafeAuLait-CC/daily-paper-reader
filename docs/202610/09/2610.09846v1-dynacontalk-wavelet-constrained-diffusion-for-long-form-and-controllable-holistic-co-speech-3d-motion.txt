Holistic co-speech animation is prone to averaging in both motion rep-
resentation and speech conditioning. In coordinate-space diffusion, slow
body posture, mid-frequency gesture strokes, and fast hand or facial de-
tails are entangled in one prediction target, often producing low-variance,
over-smoothed motion. Meanwhile, dense rhythmic and acoustic cues can
dominate sparse content-specific information under fixed multimodal fu-
sion. We present DynaConTalk, a wavelet-constrained diffusion framework
for long-form and controllable holistic co-speech motion generation. Dif-
fusion operates in stationary wavelet transform (SWT) coefficient space,
whose temporally aligned bands separate coarse posture evolution, gesture
strokes, and fine expressive details. Our dynamic gating network preserves
HuBERT and speaker identity as a base and selectively adds rhythm, mel,
and transcript features through motion-state- and noise-aware residual
gates. Attention pooling and learned depth routing deliver complementary
conditions to each denoising stage, while a frame-resolution rhythm path
preserves precise timing. A signed proposal–consensus update then rec-
onciles these conditions with the evolving motion state. Matched-noise
constraint injection uses the same sampling interface for history continua-
tion and localized keypose repair, and extends to reference-guided control.

Introduction
Holistic co-speech generation is inherently prone to averaging,
largely because the motion signal itself is layered rather than a
single homogeneous pose sequence. While the torso evolves slowly
to anchor the speaker, arms and hands produce mid-frequency ges-
ture strokes, and fingers and facial expressions carry fast expressive
details [Alexanderson et al. 2023; Ao et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2024b;
Liu et al. 2024b; Pavlakos et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2023; Yi et al. 2023;
Zhang et al. 2025]. This layered structure makes holistic co-speech
generation especially vulnerable to averaging. When all body, hand,
face, and trajectory variables are diffused as one coordinate vec-
tor, slow posture, gesture strokes, and high-frequency details are
optimized in the same space. The model can then reduce loss by
producing safe and over-smoothed motion, where rare expressive
gestures are absorbed into common beat-like movements.
A second source of averaging is rooted in the way speech condi-
tioning is structured. Speech provides multiple heterogeneous cues:

rhythm and acoustic energy are dense and frequent, while semantic
and textual cues are sparse but crucial for content-specific gestures.
Fixed concatenation or static fusion tends to let frequent rhythmic
cues dominate the generated update, causing rare semantic gestures
to be weakened or replaced by generic motion.
To cope with these problems, we propose DynaConTalk, a wavelet-
constrained diffusion framework for long-form and controllable
holistic co-speech motion generation. Our approach tackles both
sources of averaging by explicitly structuring the motion represen-
tation and conditioning signals according to their temporal and
semantic properties.
First, to address motion-level averaging, we perform diffusion in
the stationary wavelet transform (SWT) space, where aligned bands
separate coarse posture, mid-frequency gestures, and fine expressive
details. This decomposition enables scale-specific learning through
dedicated prediction heads, preventing low-amplitude expressive
dynamics from being overwhelmed by dominant coarse motion
without resorting to a single entangled coordinate-space target.
Second, we introduce state-aware dynamic gating and proposal–
consensus conditioning. HuBERT features and speaker identity form
a persistent base; independent residual gates select rhythm, mel,
and transcript cues using both the current noisy motion and the
diffusion timestep. Learned attention pooling and modality-to-depth
routing adapt these signals to each denoising stage, while a separate
frame-level rhythm path preserves timing within temporal patches.
The consensus branch controls a signed motion update, allowing a
proposed change to be accepted, attenuated, or reversed according
to the current motion state.
Third, we introduce matched-noise constraint injection for long-
form continuation and flexible user control. A clean motion history
or reference target is re-noised to the current diffusion timestep
before being injected into the constrained region. This provides
a shared sampling interface for window continuation, localized
keypose repair, and reference-guided control. The released window
sampler also uses a short velocity-based blend at joins, and the
interactive editor repairs only clips surrounding the requested edits.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• SWT coefficient diffusion. We formulate motion genera-
tion in temporally aligned wavelet space, decoupling coarse
posture, gesture strokes, and fine hand and facial details
through band-specific decoding.
• Dynamic conditioning. We combine a persistent base,
sparse residual gates, attention pooling, depth routing, and
a frame-level rhythm path with signed proposal–consensus
injection into the denoiser.
• Matched-noise constraint injection. We turn window
continuation, keypose editing, and reference control into
a single in-distribution denoising process by injecting con-
straints at the matched diffusion noise level.
2
Related Work
2.1
From Neural Regression to Holistic Diffusion
Early speech-driven animation mapped linguistic and prosodic cues
to authored gesture libraries [Cassell et al. 2001; Kipp 2005; Kopp
et al. 2006; Prendinger et al. 2004]. Neural methods replaced these
discrete pipelines with direct speech-to-motion regression using
convolutional and recurrent models [Ferstl and McDonnell 2018;
Ginosar et al. 2019; Hasegawa et al. 2018; Takeuchi et al. 2017; Yoon
et al. 2019], but deterministic regression tends to average over the
one-to-many mapping between speech and valid gestures [Cassell
et al. 1999; Kucherenko et al. 2021; Loehr 2007].
Generative models address this ambiguity through adversarial
learning [Bhattacharya et al. 2021; Ferstl et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2021],
variational models [Li et al. 2021; Pang et al. 2023; Petrovich et al.
2021], and normalizing flows [Alexanderson et al. 2020; Henter
et al. 2020; Ye et al. 2022]. Diffusion models [Ho et al. 2020; Song
et al. 2021] further improved stochastic motion generation [Chen
et al. 2023; Tevet et al. 2023] and speech-driven gesture synthe-
sis [Alexanderson et al. 2023; Ao et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2023; Zhu
et al. 2023]. Recent systems have expanded from upper-body ges-
ture to coordinated body, hands, face, and trajectory [Chen et al.
2024b; Liu et al. 2024b,a; Ng et al. 2021, 2024; Pang et al. 2023; Xu
et al. 2024; Yi et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2025]. Most generate motion
in pose-coordinate or learned latent spaces; DynaConTalk instead
uses an explicit temporally aligned multiscale representation as the
diffusion target.
2.2
Content, Rhythm, and Dynamic Speech Conditioning
Co-speech motion depends on complementary speech cues: prosody
and acoustics capture rhythm and emphasis, self-supervised features
such as wav2vec and HuBERT encode phonetic and contextual infor-
mation [Baevski et al. 2020; Hsu et al. 2021], while text and speaker
identity provide semantic and behavioral context. Multimodal meth-
ods combine these signals or learn cross-modal associations [Liang
et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2022b; Zhi et al. 2023], and DisCo separates con-
tent and rhythm representations for diverse gesture generation [Liu
et al. 2022a].
Semantic conditioning has also been introduced through text
features [Kucherenko et al. 2020], language-aligned diffusion la-
tents [Ao et al. 2023], retrieved gesture concepts [Zhang et al. 2024],
and holistic semantic-motion modeling [Zhang et al. 2025]. Dyna-
ConTalk instead dynamically regulates multiple speech conditions
according to the evolving motion state.
2.3
Frequency and Multiscale Motion Representation
Frequency structure has been used to model periodic and long-
range motion [Holden et al. 2017; Starke et al. 2022]. In gesture
synthesis, DisCo uses motion analysis to disentangle content and
rhythm without generating directly in the frequency domain [Liu
et al. 2022a]. More recent work incorporates Fourier or wavelet
structure into motion modeling [Fan et al. 2025; Feng et al. 2024],
while wavelet-domain diffusion has also been explored for image
synthesis and restoration [Huang et al. 2024; Phung et al. 2023].
Co-speech motion additionally requires temporal alignment be-
cause speech cues, gesture events, continuation boundaries, and
user controls occur at specific times. Unlike global Fourier repre-
sentations or decimated wavelets, the stationary wavelet transform
(SWT) retains coefficients at every frame and scale. Following dif-
fusion inpainting methods such as RePaint [Lugmayr et al. 2022],
known content can be re-noised to the current diffusion level and

3
Method
Co-speech animation must turn rapidly varying speech into physi-
cally coherent body, hand, face, and root motion over long durations.
DynaConTalk addresses this by performing conditional diffusion in
a stationary wavelet motion space, where multi-scale motion coeffi-
cients are generated by stream-specific denoisers and reconstructed
into SMPL-X [Pavlakos et al. 2019] motion by inverse SWT. We first
define the motion representation and wavelet diffusion (Secs. 3.1
and 3.2), then describe the structure-aware denoisers (Sec. 3.3), state-
aware dynamic gating and consensus injection (Sec. 3.4), matched-
noise continuation and control (Sec. 3.5), and the training objectives
(Sec. 3.6).
3.1
Problem Formulation
Given an arbitrary-length speech signal, we generate holistic co-
speech motion including body-hand pose, facial expression, and
root trajectory. Training sequences are cut into 𝑁-frame clips. For
each clip, the audio is encoded into a frame-aligned feature bank
containing a three-channel rhythm descriptor (amplitude envelope,
short-time energy, and onsets), a 128-band mel spectrogram, and
1024-dimensional HuBERT-Large features. A 512-dimensional CLIP
transcript embedding provides window-level text context. Speaker
identity is represented by the speaker index, a learned speaker
embedding, and the first ten SMPL-X shape coefficients. The local
motion consists of body-hand 6D rotations 𝐵∈R𝑁×330 and facial
expression coefficients 𝐹∈R𝑁×100.
We model body-hand and face motion in SWT coefficient space.
With a 3-level SWT, each channel is represented by four aligned
bands (cA3, cD3, cD2, cD1), giving body-hand targets 𝑋𝐵
0 ∈R𝑁×1320
and face targets 𝑋𝐹
0 ∈R𝑁×400. We find that diffusing the root tra-
jectory jointly with the body motion is ineffective for two reasons.
First, audio is largely uninformative for root trajectory prediction:
the root trajectory is mainly correlated with lower-body motion.
Joint training may produce plausible trajectories conditioned on the
audio, but these trajectories are not necessarily consistent with the
generated motion. Second, the root trajectory is only 3-dimensional,
whereas the body representation is 330-dimensional, making its
loss easily underweighted during training despite its strong visual
impact. We therefore attach a separate pose-driven root-translation
regressor. It uses pelvis-centered joint positions, their temporal
derivatives, and root yaw rate extracted from the generated body
pose. A convolutional patch encoder, bidirectional GRU, and tem-
poral upsampling decoder predict translation relative to the first
frame. Root translation is added after local pose generation; it is not
part of the body denoiser’s 1320-dimensional target.

3.3
Structure-Aware Denoiser
The body-hand stream and the face stream share the same diffu-
sion formulation and conditioning interface, but use stream-specific
backbones. The body-hand stream predicts high-dimensional SMPL-
X joint rotations and uses structural motion tokens, while the face
stream predicts compact expression coefficients and uses a lighter
temporal Transformer without joint tokenization.
Joint- and band-aware motion encoder. The body target is re-
shaped to 𝑁× 55 × 4 × 6: each of the 55 SMPL-X joints has a
six-dimensional rotation in each of four aligned SWT bands. A band-
specific temporal convolution embeds each joint-band sequence.
Learned joint and band embeddings identify the tokens. Within
each frame, a spatial Transformer shared across bands models inter-
joint coordination; a second Transformer mixes the four bands of
each joint. Band fusion followed by joint fusion produces a frame-
level feature. A linear projection of the input provides a parallel
bypass:
𝐻= Linear(𝑥𝑡) + 𝜌LN(TokenEncoder(𝑥𝑡)) ,
where 𝜌is initialized to zero. This allows training to start with the
linear representation and progressively incorporate the structured
token branch. The face stream uses a direct linear projection instead
of joint tokenization.

stages. A stage combines two local convolutional modules with
a patch-level temporal mixer. Pointwise and depthwise temporal
convolutions preserve local detail. The mixer downsamples motion

Experiments
We first evaluate the complete DynaConTalk system, then ana-
lyze the evidence for each of its three core components: SWT mo-

4.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets. We evaluate on two public holistic co-speech bench-
marks, BEAT2 [Liu et al. 2024b] and SHOW [Yi et al. 2023]. BEAT2
provides motion-captured holistic body, hand, and facial motion,
whereas SHOW contains SMPL-X motion reconstructed from in-
the-wild talking videos with synchronized speech. We represent all
motion at 30 fps.
Evaluation metrics. For all main comparisons, we use the official
training, validation, and test splits. For body motion, Fréchet Ges-
ture Distance (FGD) [Yoon et al. 2020] measures the distribution of
learned gesture features, beat consistency (BC) measures speech–
motion rhythm alignment, and L1 diversity measures motion vari-
ation. Facial output is evaluated with vertex MSE and face-vertex
velocity distance (LVD), using the released EMAGE evaluation tools.
In Table 1, the test-set ground truth (GT) provides reference values
for BC and diversity; generated values closer to these references are
preferred.
The speech-only body evaluation generates complete test se-
quences without ground-truth motion history, keypose targets, or
root-trajectory inputs. FGD uses 6D joint rotations; BC and diversity
use SMPL-X joint positions with neutral body shape. BC excludes
the first and last two seconds of each sequence. For face evaluation,
the first eight expression frames initialize the history. SMPL-X ver-
tices use the predicted expressions with the ground-truth jaw pose
and body shape. Thus, facial metrics assess expression generation
under this stated initialization and geometry protocol.
Implementation details. The body denoisers have four stages with
1024 channels and use 256-dimensional joint-band tokens, four spa-
tial Transformer layers, two cross-band layers, and temporal at-
tention with 16 heads. The face denoiser has four stages with 512
channels, uses temporal attention with eight heads, and omits joint
tokens. The condition embedding width is 256 for body and 128 for

with 1000 training steps, 64-frame windows, and temporal patches
of eight frames.
We optimize with AdamW, learning rate 2 × 10−4, zero weight
decay, effective batch size 192, gradient clipping at 0.5, and mixed
bfloat16 precision. An exponential moving average with decay 0.995
starts at optimizer step 1000. The per-epoch cosine learning rate has

a 56-frame window stride, and a four-frame dead blend. HuBERT

4.4.2
Representation-Space Error Analysis. The previous subsection
established that SWT separates motion into distinct temporal scales.
A natural question then arises: does this structured representation
also make the generation process more robust to prediction errors?
To answer this, we conduct a controlled perturbation experiment
that isolates the effect of the representation space itself, independent
of the denoiser or conditioning.
We take ground-truth motion sequences from the BEAT2 test set
and add a small amount of Gaussian noise to either: the Raw6D
coordinate space (direct perturbation of 6D rotations), or the SWT
coefficient space (perturbation applied to each wavelet band, fol-
lowed by inverse SWT to reconstruct the motion). The noise mag-
nitude is calibrated so that the resulting motion exhibits a similar
overall root-mean-square error (RMSE) in pose space across both
conditions. This ensures a fair comparison: we are not asking which
representation preserves the pose better, but which representation
produces more natural motion dynamics from the same level of pose
error.
Let 𝐸= e
𝑀−𝑀denote the difference between the perturbed and
original motion. Since Raw6D and SWT are matched to have the
same pose RMSE, we focus on how this error changes over time.
We measure temporal distortion using the second-order temporal
difference of 𝐸, where a larger value indicates stronger frame-to-
frame jitter.
Under this matched-error setting, Raw6D yields a temporal resid-
ual of 44.10, while SWT yields only 4.67, corresponding to an 89.4%
reduction. This shows that, even with the same overall pose er-
ror, perturbations in SWT space lead to much smoother and less
disruptive motion dynamics than perturbations in Raw6D space.
Dynamic-distribution validation. To evaluate whether SWT bet-
ter preserves the brief, high-acceleration events characteristic of
natural motion, we compare the distribution of upper-body joint-
acceleration kurtosis, denoted by 𝜅acc. We report its log-transformed
value log(1 + 𝜅acc) and compare generated and GT distributions
using the 1-Wasserstein distance (𝑊1) and Kolmogorov–Smirnov
(KS) statistics. Under the same DGN conditioning, DynaConTalk
closely matches the GT distribution, with 𝑊1 = 0.0887, compared
with 1.1217 for Raw6D—a 92.1% reduction (Table 9, Fig. 9). A two-
sample KS test also does not reject distributional agreement with

Why FGD Underrepresents Fine-Scale Improvements. SWT substan-
tially improves fine-scale dynamics, yet its removal changes FGD
only modestly. We therefore examine whether FGD is less sensitive
to high-frequency motion. First, we apply perturbations of the same
magnitude at different temporal frequencies and find that FGD re-
sponds about 20× less to 4–12 Hz perturbations than to 0.25–2 Hz
perturbations. Second, in a band-replacement test, replacing the
predicted 3–15 Hz bands with GT leaves 99.51% of FGD unchanged,
whereas replacing only the 0–1 Hz band reduces the remaining FGD
to 9.14%. These results show that FGD is mainly determined by
coarse, low-frequency motion and is relatively insensitive to the
fine-scale dynamics improved by SWT.
4.5
Evaluation of Long-Form and Controllable Generation
Table 10 evaluates matched-noise constraint injection for long-
form continuation, keypose editing, and reference-guided control.
For window continuation, matched-noise history injection reduces
boundary position error by about one third and roughly halves
velocity and acceleration jumps relative to overlap replacement, im-
proving continuity across generation windows without a separate
transition network. For keypose editing, SWT repaint uses the same
editing protocol as Raw6D repaint but reduces edit spill by 42%
while improving boundary continuity, indicating better localization
of the edited motion as shown in Fig. 10. The same constraint mech-
anism supports reference-guided style, pose-hold, and follow-action
control without retraining, reducing style distance by about one
third and nearly halving both pose-hold and motion-envelope errors
relative to uncontrolled generation.
Additional style and follow-action results are shown in Fig. 11
and the supplementary video.
5
Limitations
Most holistic co-speech datasets provide limited global locomotion,
making root-trajectory generation weakly constrained by speech
alone. Future work will investigate larger-scale training and stronger
trajectory priors for more expressive whole-body motion. General-
ization to more diverse speakers and locomotion-heavy sequences
also remains to be evaluated.

Conclusion
DynaConTalk addresses the loss of multiscale motion detail, static
fusion of heterogeneous speech cues, and discontinuities introduced
by windowed generation. It combines aligned SWT-domain diffu-
sion, dynamic state-aware residual gating with attention pooling
and depth routing, signed proposal–consensus conditioning, and
matched-noise constraint injection for holistic speech-driven mo-
tion. Experiments support improved generation quality, while the

sentation, activity calibration with DGN, and smoother window
transitions and localized control with matched-noise constraints.
