CORRECTING GUIDED DIFFUSION TRAJECTORIES
WITH SPECTRAL ALIGNMENT
Gihoon Kim and Taesup Kim
Graduate School of Data Science, Seoul National University
ABSTRACT
The practical success of conditional image generation hinges on fine-grained
differences in condition alignment and visual fidelity. Classifier-free guidance
(CFG) is central to this success, but its lack of an explicit criterion makes it
difficult to assess whether the guided trajectory is progressing as intended. To
address this gap, we show that spectral alignment provides a principled criterion for
understanding guidance behavior and improving guided diffusion sampling through
adaptive correction. Our analysis identifies the spectra of intermediate states as an
indicator of consistency with the expected spectral evolution of the forward process.
Based on this observation, we introduce Spectral Correction Guidance, a method
that corrects deviations from an analytic reference spectrum during sampling. The
proposed method is training-free and applicable across diffusion backbones and
conditional generation tasks without modifying the underlying model. Experiments
demonstrate consistent gains in preference-based metrics over baseline guidance
methods in text-to-image generation and improved generation quality over CFG on
ImageNet. These improvements persist across a range of guidance scales and with
fewer denoising steps. Our analyses and ablations provide insight into guidance
behavior and how the proposed method affects generation quality.

INTRODUCTION
Denoising diffusion models (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2020a;b;
Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021) have shown strong generative capabilities across diverse domains (Ho
et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Chi et al., 2025), including image synthesis (Nichol & Dhariwal, 2021;
Karras et al., 2022; Peebles & Xie, 2023). They exhibit stable convergence during training while
modeling the reverse of a forward noising process that perturbs clean data toward a noise distribution.
The learned reverse process captures rich data distributions, enabling sample generation through
denoising from high to low noise levels. Building on this capability, diffusion models support open-
ended conditional generation tasks such as text-to-image synthesis (Rombach et al., 2022; Saharia
et al., 2022; Podell et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; 2024; Xie et al., 2024; Esser et al., 2024).
Classifier-free guidance (CFG) (Ho & Salimans, 2022) has become a standard technique for steering
sampling toward the target condition in diffusion models. As illustrated in Figures 1a and 1b,
conditional sampling without guidance often fails to capture the target condition, whereas CFG
improves both condition alignment and perceptual quality. These benefits depend on how guidance
is applied along the denoising trajectory (Hong et al., 2023; Karras et al., 2024; Sadat et al., 2024).
However, standard CFG uses a fixed guidance scale, limiting its ability to provide appropriate
guidance at each denoising step (Kynkäänniemi et al., 2024).
Recent guidance methods aim to enhance the benefits of CFG while mitigating its adverse effects.
They adjust the guidance strength across timesteps (Kynkäänniemi et al., 2024; Malarz et al., 2025;
Jin et al., 2025a), modify the direction of guidance with geometric constraints (Sadat et al., 2025a;
Chung et al., 2024; Kwon et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2025b), or modulate guidance within predefined
frequency bands (Zhang et al., 2024; Sadat et al., 2025b; Song et al., 2026). However, these benefits
may not extend beyond the settings these methods are designed to address, such as high-guidance
regimes. In Section 6.1, experimental results show that these methods yield limited gains across a
range of generation settings, where vanilla CFG remains a strong baseline.

In this paper, we argue that these limitations arise from reliance on constraints or suppression rules
without a criterion to guide correction along the denoising trajectory. However, defining such a
criterion is challenging in the conventional spatial domain, since coordinate-based representations
encode local structures and reflect sample-specific characteristics. To address this difficulty, we
identify alignment between the power spectra of guided intermediate states and the expected spectra
under the forward process as a key indicator of effective guidance. This provides a complementary
perspective, capturing global patterns of diffusion dynamics along the denoising trajectory.
Building on this observation, we propose Spectral Correction Guidance, a training-free method
for correcting guided denoising trajectories according to the spectral dynamics of the diffusion
process. We derive a criterion from the power-law spectral statistics of natural images (Ruderman,
1997), yielding a distributional reference that is not tied to individual samples or specific failure
patterns. Based on this criterion, our method corrects deviations from the expected spectral behavior
in intermediate states. The proposed correction enables adaptive adjustments across frequencies and
timesteps without relying on a prescribed correction pattern.
We further validate our method through extensive experiments on diverse conditional image generation
tasks. In text-to-image generation, the proposed correction improves preference-based metrics across
multiple backbones and guidance settings, indicating better text alignment and visual quality. In class-
conditional generation, our method achieves lower FID (Heusel et al., 2017) and higher Inception
Score (Salimans et al., 2016) than CFG, even with fewer sampling steps. These results demonstrate
that correcting spectral deviations during sampling can improve generation quality without additional
training. Ablation studies examine how correction strength and the timestep range over which
correction is applied affect generation outputs. We also discuss how the proposed method relates to
existing guidance approaches from a spectral perspective.

SPECTRAL DYNAMICS IN THE DIFFUSION PROCESS
In this section, we examine how the guided diffusion trajectory evolves during sampling from a
spectral perspective. To generate plausible samples from the data distribution, the reverse marginals
pθ(zt) defined in Eq. 3 should remain close to the corresponding forward marginals q(zt) in Eq. 2
across timesteps. We thus examine the spectral statistics of intermediate states to assess this marginal
alignment. The power spectra of intermediate states provide a tractable summary of how their energy
is distributed across frequency components. Agreement between their expected power spectra is a
necessary condition for matching the forward and reverse marginals.
We compare the expected power spectra of forward and reverse states across guidance scales under the
same noise schedule, as detailed in Appendix E. Let ˆzt = F(zt) be the Fourier representation of zt,
and define its power spectrum as Pt(ω) = |ˆzt(ω)|2, where ω > 0 denotes the radial frequency index.

SPECTRAL CORRECTION FOR CLASSIFIER-FREE GUIDANCE
To enable correction without access to corresponding forward states, we derive an analytic reference
from the statistical structure of natural image spectra. Using the forward process in Eq. 1 and the
spectral properties of Gaussian noise, we extend this reference to the expected power spectra of
intermediate states along the forward diffusion trajectory.
Proposition 1. Following the power-law model of natural image spectra (Ruderman, 1997), suppose
that the expected power spectrum of z0 takes the form1
E[P0(ω)] = Cω−β + c,
ω > 0, C, β > 0, c ≥0.
(7)
By definition, white Gaussian noise has a flat power spectrum,
E[Pϵ(ω)] = cϵ,
ω > 0, cϵ ≥0.
For the forward process zt = αtz0 + σtϵ in Eq. 1, z0 ⊥ϵ and E[ϵ] = 0, so the cross term vanishes in
expectation. Therefore, the expected power spectrum of zt has the form
E[Pt(ω)] = λtω−β + γt,
ω > 0, λt, γt ≥0, β > 0,
(8)
where λt and γt are determined by αt, σt and the constants in Eq. 7.
Proposition 1 shows that the expected power spectrum at each timestep can be specified in an analytic
functional form. This formulation allows us to compute the reference spectrum for assessing spectral
alignment without simulating the corresponding forward states.

Algorithm 1 summarizes how the correction is incorporated into the denoising process. The proposed
correction preserves the phase of the current latent and modifies only its Fourier magnitude to match
˜St(ω). This adjustment enables correction of spectral discrepancies while retaining the spatial
information encoded in the Fourier phase. Figure 3 visualizes this correction process, showing that
our method aligns the corrected spectrum ˜St(ω) with the target Tt(ω) in both overall energy level
and frequency-dependent shape. Our method therefore employs the expected spectral evolution of
the forward process as a reference for correcting guided denoising trajectories.
In practice, we estimate the coefficients in Eq. 7 from the spectral statistics of real image samples (Lin
et al., 2014) rather than through model-specific performance tuning. We perform this estimation
in the z0 space of each backbone without selecting samples to match its training data (Deng et al.,
2009; Schuhmann et al., 2022). We compute radial power spectra by averaging two-dimensional
Fourier power over radial frequency bins. Given the estimated coefficients, Eq. 8 determines the target
spectrum Tt(ω) at each timestep through the corresponding noise schedule. To retain fine-grained
frequency components (Karras et al., 2021), we relax the correction in the late denoising stage as
discussed in Section 6.2. This procedure applies a common correction principle across diffusion
models with different spectral statistics and noise schedules.
5
A SPECTRAL VIEW OF EXISTING GUIDANCE METHODS
In this section, we discuss related guidance strategies. From a spectral perspective, we also examine
how these strategies control distinct aspects of the guidance signal during denoising.
Scheduled guidance scaling.
Scheduled guidance scaling replaces the constant guidance scale
w with a predefined timestep-dependent schedule. Kynkäänniemi et al. (2024) restrict guidance to
selected denoising intervals, while Malarz et al. (2025) and Jin et al. (2025a) adjust the guidance
strength according to predefined functional forms. By Parseval’s theorem, adjusting the guidance
scale at each timestep also modulates the Fourier-domain energy of the guidance term. In this sense,
our method provides fine-grained scaling through spectral correction at each timestep and frequency.
Geometry-aware guidance.
Geometry-aware guidance methods constrain the magnitude (Jin et al.,
2025b) or direction (Sadat et al., 2025a) of guided updates, or adjust sampling to reduce deviations
from the data manifold (Chung et al., 2024; Kwon et al., 2025). Our method shares the goal of
keeping guided trajectories consistent with the data distribution, but approaches it through spectral
alignment with the forward marginals encountered during training.
Frequency-based guidance.
Frequency-based methods modulate guidance using predefined fre-
quency partitions (Zhang et al., 2024), applying different guidance strengths to separate bands (Sadat
et al., 2025b) or suppressing selected frequencies (Song et al., 2026). Although these methods also
consider spectral information, their adjustments follow band-specific rules. In contrast, our method
adapts the correction at each frequency based on spectral deviations from an analytic reference.

Effect of correction strength.
The ablation shows that an appropriate correction strength improves
visual fidelity and prompt alignment. As shown in Figure 7b, a small correction leaves the output
close to standard guidance with little visible effect, suggesting that weak spectral modification has
a limited effect on the guided trajectory. At moderate strengths, Figure 7c shows richer colors and
clearer details, indicating that stronger correction better restores spectral components affected by
guidance. However, excessive correction introduces artifacts and degrades perceptual quality as
shown in Figure 7d. These results suggest that the correction strength controls the balance between
correcting deviations from the expected spectrum and preserving individual sample characteristics.
Correction relaxation.
The previous ablation shows that increasing correction strength improves
fidelity and prompt alignment but can introduce artifacts. We thus study how to relax the correction
across the denoising process to retain its benefits while reducing artifacts. Motivated by the role
of late denoising steps in fine-grained detail formation (Karras et al., 2021), we vary the relaxation
parameter ρ, which controls when the frequency-domain constraint is relaxed during denoising.
Figure 8 shows that applying correction until the end can retain artifacts, whereas applying it only in
the early stage weakens its effect. An intermediate relaxation point provides the best balance between
spectral correction and natural detail formation. We therefore adopt this setting in our final method.
The relaxation analysis clarifies where spectral correction improves generation quality and where its
continued application can degrade quality (Kynkäänniemi et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2025a).
7
CONCLUSION
In this paper, we demonstrated that spectral alignment can serve as a practical criterion for understand-
ing guidance behavior and improving guided diffusion sampling through principled correction. This
perspective provides a basis for assessing whether guided trajectories remain consistent with the spec-
tral dynamics induced by the forward process. We derived an analytic reference from natural image
statistics and used it to correct spectral deviations along guided denoising trajectories. Building on
this criterion, Spectral Correction Guidance is a training-free method applicable to diverse diffusion
backbones and conditional generation tasks without modifying the underlying model. Experiments
show that the proposed method improves generation quality across model architectures, guidance
strengths, and reduced sampling budgets, while prior guidance strategies provide limited gains in
common guidance settings. This work highlights how diffusion dynamics and domain-specific
statistics can be combined to establish a principled criterion for trajectory correction.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
The proposed method demonstrates that spectral alignment based on power spectra can provide
a useful criterion for guiding diffusion trajectories and improving generation quality. However,
this criterion only captures marginal frequency-domain statistics and does not fully characterize
the generated distribution. Matching power spectra can align the overall spectral energy across
frequencies, but it does not account for phase information or spatial structure. Future work could
extend spectral correction beyond power spectra by using wavelet representations (Mallat, 1989) to
capture localized frequency structure and by incorporating constraints that account for phase and
individual sample structure. Another limitation is that our correction is applied with a shared rule
across samples and conditions. In practice, the amount and type of guidance needed may depend on
the input condition, prompt, or class. Guidance strategies that adapt to the input condition (Galashov
et al., 2026; Papalampidi et al., 2026) suggest a promising direction for extending spectral correction
to account for differences across samples and conditions. Combining spectral alignment with
condition-aware guidance control could enable more flexible adjustment of the denoising trajectory.

image generation backbones, including SiT and JiT.
SiT.
We first evaluate our method on SiT-XL/2 (Ma et al., 2024). We use 32 radial bins for power
spectrum estimation, with λ = 0.005 and ρ = 0.5. Figure 10 shows qualitative results. The results
suggest that our method can improve generation quality also on SiT. Our method produces a more
object-centric image for the European fire salamander class and reveals a clearer object shape for the
Indian cobra class.
JiT.

correction is not uniform across samples, as it can introduce artifacts in some cases while producing
only marginal changes in others. This behavior may be related to JiT operating directly in the

ADDITIONAL ANALYSIS OF POWER SPECTRUM MODELING
Our method extends a spectral principle from pixel space to latent space. To empirically validate this
assumption, we examine whether the power-law behavior of natural image spectra is preserved after
encoding images into the latent spaces of the models used in our experiments. Figure 12 shows the
mean power spectrum of encoded COCO val 50K images (Lin et al., 2014) in the latent space of
each model, along with the fitted curve based on Eq. 7. The fitted curves achieve R2 > 0.99 across
models, indicating that the z0 spaces of the image generation models considered in our experiments
can be well characterized by this power-law structure. This result provides empirical justification for
applying the proposed spectral correction in these spaces.
We also analyze the effect of binning resolution when estimating the power spectrum for the proposed
correction. The binning resolution determines how many frequency components are grouped into
each radial bin. To make the effect of this design choice more visible, we vary the number of radial
bins under a relatively strong correction strength of λ = 0.5. Figure 13 shows the qualitative results.
Coarse binning, such as 8 radial bins, covers a broad range of frequencies within each bin and mainly
produces global changes, such as changes in overall brightness. By contrast, finer binning, such as
128 radial bins, produces more noticeable changes in image structure. These results suggest that
binning resolution is one of the design choices that can affect the behavior of spectral correction.

ADDITIONAL ANALYSIS OF CORRECTION RELAXATION
We further analyze the effect of the relaxation factor ρ used in the proposed correction. Figure 14
shows the results on SDXL, where we vary ρ and evaluate PickScore, FID, and sFID. The value
ρ = 0.5 provides the most balanced results across the three metrics. This provides quantitative
support for the role of correction relaxation in balancing alignment and fidelity. We also observe that
the benefits of relaxation are often more apparent in visual quality than in metrics, with corrected
samples showing a more natural appearance and fewer visible artifacts.

Analysis of Failure Cases.
Despite the overall effectiveness of the proposed correction, the ad-
ditional qualitative results also reveal two common failure patterns. These include cases where the
correction only partially mitigates artifacts observed in CFG, and cases where improvements in visual
quality or prompt alignment are accompanied by undesired changes in other aspects of the image.
First, in the first example of Figure 16, our method produces a cat with a more natural composition,
but the generated cat still exhibits an abnormal tail structure. Similarly, in the fifth example of
Figure 18, the face of the person near the center is improved, while some details such as the hands
remain unnatural. In these examples, our method retains much of the quality already achieved by
CFG while refining some fine-grained details, though certain artifacts persist. In the last example
of Figure 19, our method improves visual quality over CFG, but some artifacts remain. Second,
some cases show a trade-off between different aspects of generation quality. In the last example of
Figure 22, the overall color tone is improved, but the number of people increases, making the result
less consistent with the text condition. In the last example of Figure 24, the luggage shape becomes
more natural, but the interaction between objects is weakened during the correction. These examples
suggest that, while the proposed method can improve generation quality in many cases, there remains
room for further improving its robustness.

ADDITIONAL DISCUSSION
The proposed method improves guided generation by normalizing spectral deviations from the
expected denoising dynamics. This correction keeps the guided trajectory closer to the spectral
statistics induced by the forward process, which can improve image quality and prompt alignment. At
the same time, the correction does not provide explicit feedback toward a desired semantic direction.
As a result, its ability to control generation toward a specific target remains limited. More direct
semantic control may require additional learning signals, such as external rewards or task-specific
supervision, but the use of such signals introduces separate challenges. These observations suggest
that more fundamental improvements may require aligning the denoising dynamics from the training
stage. This direction calls for a deeper understanding of the representation space and generation
dynamics formed by diffusion models.
