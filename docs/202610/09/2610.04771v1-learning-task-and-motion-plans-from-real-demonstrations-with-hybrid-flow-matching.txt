Learning Task and Motion Plans from Real
Demonstrations with Hybrid Flow Matching
Zuleika Redondo Garcia∗,1, Andreu Matoses Gimenez∗,1, and Javier Alonso-Mora1
Abstract—Long-horizon mobile manipulation requires a task
plan and the motion that executes it. Generative planners trained
on demonstration produce both in one pass, requiring neither a
symbolic domain nor search. To date, however, they have relied
on thousands of scripted demonstrations of fixed-base arms and
executed open loop. This paper presents a hybrid flow matching
planner: a single network generates the symbolic plan with
masked discrete flow matching and the motion trajectory with
continuous flow matching. Unlike prior generative planners, we
aim to learn from a much smaller set of demonstrations and
to execute the plan in closed loop. Two properties of the data
compensate for the small dataset. A demonstration resumed from
any of its intermediate actions is itself a demonstration, which
multiplies the training samples and enables replanning after
every action. Objects of the same kind are interchangeable, which
turns demonstrations of one goal into demonstrations of every
permuted goal. Our base implementation produces valid plans on
68% of held-out scenes; a training and generation scheme for the
discrete plan raises this to 76%, and replanning after every action
raises the task completion rate from 40% to 53% in a kinematic
simulation. The planner matches the task completion rate of
motion-only flow matching policies while additionally providing
the symbolic plan, and it outperforms previous hybrid diffusion
formulations on both task completion and plan validity. We vali-
date the planner on a real mobile manipulator. Videos and project

I. INTRODUCTION
Mobile manipulation tasks such as tidying a room or as-
sembling a structure require a robot to decide what to do and
how to move. To stack three cubes on a target table (Fig. 1),
a robot has to choose which cube to pick first, from which
side of which table, in which position to place it. It also has
to produce the base and arm motion for each choice. The two
levels depend on each other: a cube can only be reached from
certain sides of a table, and a covered cube cannot be picked.
Task and motion planning (TAMP) produces both the action
sequence and the motion that realizes it while keeping them
consistent [1].

Classical TAMP methods search over symbolic action se-
quences and call a motion planner to check each candidate [2]–
[5]. They need a hand-written model of the task: predicates,
action operators, and samplers for continuous parameters such
as grasps and base placements. The search is also expensive,
which limits replanning when the scene changes. Learning
can guide the search [6], [7] or distill planner solutions into
policies [8], but both still depend on the planner and its model.
Generative models trained on demonstrations take a differ-
ent route. The task knowledge enters through the demonstra-
tions, so no planning domain is written by hand. A plan then
costs one generation pass with a fixed number of network eval-
uations instead of a search over candidate plans. Diffusion and
flow matching policies capture the multimodal distribution of
demonstrated motion and generate whole trajectories [9]–[12].
On long tasks with several distinct decisions, however, the
model blends different behaviors and the trajectory commits to
none of them. Høeg et al. [13] address this with a discrete and
a continuous diffusion process in one network that generate a
symbolic plan together with the trajectory, and show that the
joint generation outperforms trajectory-only generation.
The space of task and motion plans is combinatorial, as each
possible action sequence must be grounded in the geometry

of the scene. Prior joint symbolic and continuous generative
planners have therefore relied on hundreds to thousands of
demonstrations per task, often generated by scripted experts on
fixed-base manipulators and executed open loop [13]. For real
mobile manipulators, however, demonstrations are typically
collected through teleoperation and cannot be generated at that
scale. This limited coverage raises three challenges: general-
ization to unseen scenes and plans, generation of variable-
length symbolic plans, and error accumulation during open-
loop execution.
We address these challenges with a hybrid flow matching
planner for mobile manipulation trained on about one hundred
real demonstrations. Given an observation of the scene, a
single network simultaneously generates a discrete symbolic
plan and the continuous trajectories of both the mobile base
and the end effector. To improve data efficiency, we augment
each demonstration with its suffixes, the remaining plan and
trajectory after each of its actions, which also enables the
planner to replan after every action. We also exploit object
interchangeability through object permutations, a consistent
renaming of the objects in plan and observation, to obtain goal-
conditioned demonstrations. We further constrain the gener-
ated plans to well-formed action sequences, adapt training to
variable-length plans, and automatically balance the discrete
and continuous losses.
We evaluate the method on a mobile manipulator building
a three-cube tower from 109 teleoperated demonstrations.
Generated plans are validated with a symbolic model, executed
in kinematic simulation, and evaluated on the real robot. We
compare against motion-only flow matching and the hybrid
diffusion planner of Høeg et al. [13], and ablate the proposed
components.
The contributions of this paper are:
• A hybrid flow matching planner that jointly generates the
symbolic plan and the motion of a mobile manipulator,
with a training and generation scheme that improves
symbolic plan validity and task completion.
• Two augmentations that exploit the structure of task
and motion demonstrations, which allow replanning after
every action and goal conditioning without new demon-
strations (Section IV-D).
• An evaluation on real demonstrations against motion-only
flow matching and hybrid diffusion [13], with ablations
and a deployment on the real robot (Section V).
II. RELATED WORK
Task and motion planning: Task and motion planning
(TAMP) integrates symbolic task planning with geometric
reasoning to ensure that high-level action sequences are phys-
ically feasible [1]. Existing approaches couple these levels
in different ways. Hierarchical methods progressively inter-
leave task and motion planning [2], while planner-independent
frameworks allow symbolic planners to invoke black-box ge-
ometric samplers [3], [5], [14]. Logic-geometric programming
instead optimizes the action sequence and its associated mo-
tion jointly [4]. Learning has also been used to guide the plan-
ning process, for example by ranking candidate refinements
[6] or predicting promising action sequences before geometric
verification [7]. Other work distills planner-generated solutions
into visuomotor policies [8]. These approaches generally retain
an explicit planning model, either during execution or when
generating training data. In contrast, we consider learning
symbolic plans and their associated motions directly from
teleoperated demonstrations.
Generative models for robot motion and planning: Dif-
fusion models generate multimodal trajectories from demon-
strations, for planning [9], [15] and for visuomotor control
[10]. Flow matching trains the same class of models with
a regression objective and supports sampling in only a few
integration steps [11]; it has since been adopted for robot
policies [12], [16]. For long-horizon tasks, however, task
structure is often provided separately. Generative skill chaining
composes diffusion models according to a given skill sequence
[17], SkillDiffuser learns discrete skill abstractions from lan-
guage instructions [18], and LTLDoG constrains sampling
with temporal logic [19]. Inside classical TAMP, diffusion
models have served as samplers for continuous parameters
[20], [21], while language models can provide high-level plans
that are subsequently grounded by a diffusion model [22].
In these approaches, the symbolic task structure is provided,
inferred, or planned separately from the continuous motion.
Our planner instead generates both jointly.
Joint discrete and continuous generation: Discrete dif-
fusion and flow models generate token sequences by pro-
gressively unmasking an initially masked sequence [23]–[26].
Campbell et al. [27] combine such a masked discrete flow with
a continuous flow to co-design protein sequences and struc-
tures, while Diffusion Forcing assigns individual noise levels
to tokens so that a single model can support both full-sequence
and next-token generation [28]. Closest to our work, Høeg
et al. [13] jointly generate a symbolic plan and continuous
trajectory using discrete and continuous diffusion processes
within a single transformer, with independent noise levels
for the two modalities. On simulated and real tabletop tasks,
the joint generation outperforms trajectory-only diffusion. Our
work builds on this hybrid formulation, replacing diffusion
with flow matching and studying the more data-limited setting
of teleoperated mobile-manipulation demonstrations.
Augmentation and replanning in imitation learning: Re-
labeling and symmetry provide complementary ways to reuse
demonstrations without collecting additional data. Hindsight
experience replay relabels trajectories according to achieved
goals [29], while equivariant policies incorporate scene sym-
metries into the policy architecture [30]. Receding-horizon
execution similarly improves robustness by repeatedly replan-
ning over fixed-length action chunks [10], [31]. This strategy
has also been applied to real mobile manipulation using rela-
tively small per-task demonstration sets supplemented by co-
training data [32]. Our work exploits object interchangeability
for symbolic relabeling and performs replanning at symbolic
action boundaries rather than at a fixed temporal horizon.

three cubes R, Y, and G can lie on three tables, and the goal
is a tower in a given order on a given table.

IV. HYBRID FLOW MATCHING

discrete and a continuous one. The symbolic plan is generated
with masked discrete flow matching, from a fully masked
sequence. The trajectory is generated with continuous flow
matching, from noise. Both flows run in parallel through the
same network (Fig. 3), so each modality conditions the other
while it is generated. Each flow has its own time, td and tc,
from 0 (noise) to 1 (data).

V. EXPERIMENTS
The experiments answer three questions. Does the planner
produce valid plans and successful executions on scenes it
has not seen, and how does it compare with the two closest
alternatives, motion-only flow matching [12], [16] and hybrid
diffusion [13]? What does each part of the training and
generation scheme contribute? Does the planner run on the
real robot?

predicted from the per-action success rates on demonstrated
scenes, so the states reached during replanning are as usable
as the demonstrated ones. Moreover, 8–10% of rollouts recover
after a failed action. The goal-conditioned policy shows a
similar improvement.

D. Real robot
The policy runs on the robot from the motion capture poses,
with the end-effector waypoints converted to joint targets by
inverse kinematics and the base waypoints sent to the base
controller, and it replans after every action. Figure 7 shows a
run in which every action succeeds, and Fig. 9 a run in which
a missed grasp is retried. The robot was tested in 30 unseen
cube placements: the tower was built in about 30% of the runs
from short plans (d ≤6) and in 10% from long ones (d ≥8).
The failures are the same as in the kinematic simulation, grasps
missed by a few centimeters and placements off the support.


Perception: The results above take the cube poses from
motion capture. To check that the policy does not depend
on them and works from image perception, we trained it on
image features instead: the object pose tokens are replaced by
one token per object from a DINOv2 keypoint estimator [39],
trained on images from a fixed camera recorded alongside the
demonstrations, with the same held-out split. The policy keeps
its plan validity (78% against 76%) and loses 6% of open-loop
task completion rate.
VI. CONCLUSIONS
We presented a hybrid flow matching planner, trained on
109 teleoperated demonstrations of a mobile manipulator,
that jointly generates symbolic plans and continuous trajec-
tories. Plan-suffix and object-permutation augmentations im-
prove data efficiency, enabling action-level replanning and goal
conditioning. The proposed training and generation scheme
increases the plan validity on held-out scenes to 76% while
closed-loop replanning after every action raises the task com-
pletion rate from 40% (open loop) to 53% in a kinematic
simulation, and allows recovery from failed actions on the real
robot. Compared with the two closest alternatives, the method
matches the task completion rate of motion-only flow matching
while providing an explicit plan, and improves trajectory
accuracy over hybrid diffusion.
