## Abstract 

Diffusion models trained on increasingly large datasets sampled from the same data distribution will converge to the same learned distribution. However, in practice, a model trained on finite data depends on the particular training dataset; this depen-dence is reflected in properties of the model’s outputs, such as memorization and sample quality. To understand this dependence through an information-theoretic lens, we study model information , a computationally tractable measure of how much samples from the trained model depend on the particular training dataset. We explore its scaling behavior as model size and training dataset size vary. Specifi-cally, we uncover two qualitatively distinct scaling regimes: (1) a nonlinear model information regime marking the transition from pure memorization to imperfect generalization, and (2) a generalization regime characterized by linear scaling of the model information and progressively improved generalization and sample quality. We then posit an information-theoretically principled scaling law to quantify these two regimes, and show that this law yields induced scaling laws for memorization and sample quality. We finally demonstrate that these induced scaling laws extrap-olate well outside the fit’s domain. Our results demonstrate information-theoretic principles for memorization and sample quality scaling laws in diffusion models. Our code is publicly available on GitHub. 

## 1 Introduction 

Diffusion models [Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015, Ho et al., 2020, Song et al., 2021] have emerged as one of the most successful approaches to deep generative modeling, achieving remarkable results across diverse domains, including images, videos, audio, and scientific applications [Rombach et al., 2022, Ho et al., 2022, Chen et al., 2021, Watson et al., 2023]. Their training objective, denoising score matching [Vincent, 2011], ensures that when sufficiently powerful models are trained with arbitrarily large amounts of training data, their output distribution converges to the true data distribution, independently of the exact training data used, and such trained models generalize [Yoon et al., 2023, Gu et al., 2023] — they generate realistic samples not contained in the training data. However, when trained on finite datasets, these models can memorize and reproduce individual training examples [Carlini et al., 2023, Somepalli et al., 2023], producing realistic samples without capturing the underlying data distribution [Marion and Wu, 2026]. A fundamental methodological question is: can we characterize the occurrence of memorization and generalization in diffusion models? 

Early empirical studies found that memorization diminishes with increasing training-set size and that this transition from memorization to generalization depends on the underlying denoiser neural network capacity, architecture, and training procedure [Yoon et al., 2023, Gu et al., 2023]. Theoretical work has further investigated why these models may generalize despite training objectives that favor memorization, highlighting the roles of model capacity [Buchanan et al., 2025], implicit regularization [Bonnaire et al., 2025], and architectural inductive biases [Kamb and Ganguli, 2024].

In the generalization regime, the trained models have nearly indistinguishable output distributions, so a sample bXcarries little information about which model (or training dataset) produced it. In the memorization regime, each trained model’s outputs concentrate around its own training data, so generated samples reveal the training dataset.

A complementary perspective on generalization emerges from studying the dependence of trained models on their training datasets: memorizing models are highly sensitive to the particular examples encountered during training, whereas generalizing models tend to capture the underlying data distribution more independently of the training set. For example, Kadkhodaie et al. [2023] related generalization to the convergence of independently trained models to the same output distribution, while other studies showed that the influence of individual training examples and the variability of learned denoisers decrease with dataset size [Dai and Gifford, 2026, Wang et al., 2026]. However, the quantitative behavior of this dependence of the learned distribution on the training dataset across dataset sizes and model capacities remains under-explored. 

Our contributions. We define the model information , an information-theoretic measure of how much a generated sample reveals about the identity of the model that produced it (and thus, under deterministic training, the exact training dataset used). The idea is sketched in Figure 1. We show: 1. Theoretically, model information characterizes statistical measures of bias, variance, memo-rization, and generalization (Section 3). 2. Empirically, model information is relatively cheap to compute and follows a predictable scaling law, the form of which we derive via solving a simple and analytically tractable linear-Gaussian model, as training-set size and model capacity increase (Section 4.1). 3. Model information is strongly correlated with empirically designed measures of memoriza-tion, generalization, and sample quality (Section 4.2) to the extent that it induces scaling laws for these quantities that extrapolate well to new parameters (Section 4.3). Together, these results establish a quantitative framework for information-theoretically principled predictable scaling of memorization, generalization, and sample quality in diffusion models.

## 4 Scaling Laws for Model Information, Memorization, and Sample Quality 

We now study how model information depends on the model size P and the training-set size N , and how it relates to memorization and sample quality. We train unconditional EDM diffusion models [Karras et al., 2022] with DDPM++ backbones [Song et al., 2021] (a UNet with, say, L layers in each of the encoder and decoder) on ImageNet [Deng et al., 2009]. We report the following findings: 1. Model information follows a scaling law whose form we derive from the Marchenko–Pastur law for the spectrum of an empirical covariance (Section 4.1). We can measure it from a few trained models per (P, N ) pair. 2. We show that model information is strongly correlated with memorization when N is small, and with FID when N is large (Section 4.2). 3. From these two findings, we extrapolate model information, memorization, and FID to a new P and N and show that these predictions are accurate (Section 4.3). In Appendix D we repeat the analysis on CIFAR-10, Fashion-MNIST, and FFHQ.

From the linear-Gaussian model to the scaling law. In the linear-Gaussian model, all d directions stop memorizing at the same training-set size N = d, so the transition from memorization to generalization is sharp. In our measurements the transition is gradual, extending over more than a decade of N (Figure 3). We reconcile these observations with the model by positing that different parts of the data are memorized more easily than others. To model this, we split the coordinates into independent groups of different sizes. Each group is modeled using the linear-Gaussian model, so a group of size dz stops memorizing at N = dz , and model information is the sum over the groups. We want the distribution of group sizes to have only two parameters: one for the location of the transition and one for its width. The transition is spread over a range of log N , so we take log dz to be normal with mean log Nc(P ) and standard deviation σ. This is the distribution of largest entropy for a given mean and variance of log dz . Then Nc(P ) is the training-set size at which a typical group stops memorizing; we call it the critical training-set size. A larger model can memorize a larger training set, so Nc(P ) increases with P . We take Nc(P ) and the number of groups to be power laws in P .Finally, the estimate is positive even when all models train on the same data, because of the variation between training seeds and the bias of the estimator (Appendix D.4). Model informations are mutual informations, which are both computationally and sta-tistically intractable to estimate, especially in high dimensions [McAllester and Stratos, 2020]. We pro-vide no formal explanation for the accuracy of these estimations. However, our understanding is that our score representation (Proposition 3.1) reduces the problem, therefore pushing the computational and statistical difficulty of estimating this mutual information, into training a small number of neural networks. This latter problem is itself computationally and statistically intractable in the worst case, yet very tractable in the average case (i.e., all of modern deep learning). This is one possible reason why this particular mutual information is surprisingly cheap to estimate.

4.3 Predicting Memorization and Sample Quality for a New Model 

With the scaling law and the two fits of Figure 6, we can predict memorization and FID before we train a model: we predict model information with the scaling law, and then predict memorization and FID from model information. We test this on a new pair, depth L = 5 and N = 250 ,000 ,which is outside the range of our experiments in both P and N . We fit the scaling law and both regressions only on the models at L ∈ { 2, 3, 4}. Thus the critical training-set size at L = 5 , and with it the range of N in which we predict FID, is also a prediction. We then train four models in the new setting. In this extrapolation, the empirically estimated model information is within 7% of the prediction, no sample is memorized, as predicted, and the FID is within 15% of the prediction. In Figure 7 we compare the predictions with the measurements at all values of N for the new value of P

corresponding to L = 5 . We observe that the predicted memorization ratio is close to the measured memorization ratio, and likewise for predicted and measured FID, for all values of N .

Our model information quantifies the variability of their learned distributions, connecting naturally to their bias–variance perspective. More recently, Hunt et al. [2026] used information theory to characterize memorization–generalization transitions in Bayesian diffusion models. Most closely related to our work, Yi et al. Our work addresses these questions through an estimator that uses a few independently trained models, an analytically tractable model, and empirical scaling laws. 

Information-theoretic approaches to diffusion models. Diffusion models can be viewed as noisy communication channels through which information is progressively lost. The evolution of mutual information under Gaussian noising can be expressed through denoising and score-based identities [Guo et al., 2005, Kong et al., 2023a, Franzese et al., 2024], enabling practical mutual-information estimation using diffusion models. This perspective has been used to study information about clean samples [Kong et al., 2023a, Stancevic et al., 2026], latent abstractions and semantic classes [Franzese et al., 2025, Handke et al., 2026], and relationships between images and text [Kong et al., 2023b]. Building on this line of work, we extend score-based mutual-information estimation to ensembles of trained generative models by approximating the ensemble with finitely many trained models, and demonstrate stable estimation using only a few independently trained networks. 

Scaling laws. Scaling laws have played a central role in the development of large language models, providing quantitative predictions of how performance improves with increasing dataset size, model capacity, and compute [Kaplan et al., 2020, Hoffmann et al., 2022]. More recently, similar relation-ships have been established for diffusion models, showing that both training loss and generative quality exhibit predictable scaling behavior [Li et al., 2024, Liang et al., 2026, Chickering et al., 2026]. Our work establishes scaling laws for model information across memorization and generalization regimes, enabling predictions of both memorization and generative quality. 

## 6 Conclusion 

In this work, we show that model information can be efficiently estimated and exhibits predictable scaling behavior across training-set sizes and model capacities. An analytically tractable model motivates a scaling law that captures the transition from memorization to generalization observed in neural networks. We show that this transition closely tracks the disappearance of memorization, while decreasing model information is associated with improvements in generative fidelity in the generalization regime. These relationships allow us to predict memorization and sample quality for previously unseen combinations of training-set size and model capacity. Our results thus provide a quantitative connection between dataset dependence, memorization, and generative fidelity, offering a new perspective on how generalization emerges in diffusion models. 9Several questions remain open. A deeper theoretical understanding of the scaling law is needed. Understanding how its parameters, including the critical training-set size Nc(P ) and the width σ of the transition, emerge from network architecture and learning dynamics remains open. Another direction is to investigate how random initialization and stochastic optimization contribute to model information independently of training-set variability. Finally, empirical improvements to the estimation procedure such that it can be applied reliably or efficiently to pretrained models would also be a way to expand this methodology to frontier settings.

B.3 Extensions: alternative hypotheses 

We briefly discuss the broader viewpoint from which the model information construction arose. The definition of model information does not require diffusion models: for any random generative mechanism Φ and any sample ˆX ∼ Φ, the mutual information I(Φ; ˆX) measures how much the sample reveals about the mechanism that produced it. Diffusion is useful here because it supplies a canonical noising channel ˆX 7 → ˆXt and turns this quantity into a time-resolved information curve with a tractable score-based production rate. From this perspective, the starting point is to view diffusion as a noisy communication channel through which information about a random source hypothesis Φ is progressively erased. The main modeling question is then which hypothesis Φ one chooses to track. In this paper, we take Φ to be the learned model induced by data set resampling, because this choice turns the resulting mutual information into a measure of finite-data dependence and, hence, into a measure of generalization and memorization. Other choices of Φ may reveal different structures erased by diffusion, and remain interesting directions for future work. ## C A Linear-Gaussian Model 

In this section, we study a linear-Gaussian model in which model information can be characterized explicitly. The purpose of this example is to provide a tractable analogue of the scaling behavior observed empirically. When the number N of training samples is small relative to the dimension d,model information is approximately constant in N and generated samples remain strongly dependent on the particular training dataset. Around N = d, this dependence decreases rapidly. With sufficiently many samples, model information enters a generalization regime in which it decays as 1/N . An example of the resulting model-information curve is shown in Figure 8. The connection with neural networks is limited, but we use this toy model because its model information is analytically tractable. For example, the trained model does not literally reproduce individual training examples. Instead, for N < d , the learned covariance retains a strongly dataset-specific subspace, which leads to divergent model information in the zero-noise limit. The fact that even this simple mechanism is sufficient to produce a high-information regime, a capacity-dependent transition, and a low-information scaling regime suggests that these phenomena may be more general. We therefore hope that this toy model provides some insight into the qualitative shape of the scaling laws observed in more complex generative models.

In the sigmoid–power form this exponent is a fitted parameter; on ImageNet the fit makes the decay much flatter than the measured slope near −1, and its F (P ) is far from the estimates at N = Nfull .When we withhold the largest model size, the Marchenko–Pastur form has the smallest error on ImageNet, CIFAR-10, and FFHQ. When we withhold the training-set sizes above the median, it has the smallest error on ImageNet and CIFAR-10.

D.9 Sensitivity of the Model Information to the Number of Trained Models 

The number of reference models Ktrain and the number of held-out models Kval are parameters of the estimator and not of model information, so the fitted scaling law should not depend on them. We check this in two ways on CIFAR-10, Fashion-MNIST, and FFHQ. First, we sweep Ktrain over 

{2, 3, 4, 5} on Fashion-MNIST and over {1, 2} on CIFAR-10 and FFHQ, with Kval fixed at five on Fashion-MNIST and two on the other two datasets. Second, since we train Ktrain + Kval models per 

(P, N ) pair, we sweep the two numbers together on Fashion-MNIST, as they would change with the number of trained models. We refit (14) independently at each value. Unlike the fits in the rest of this appendix, these refits include the estimates at N = Nfull as measurements of F (P ). This keeps 

F (P ) fixed across values of Ktrain , so that the comparison isolates the effect of Ktrain on the other parameters. On ImageNet we compare Ktrain = 1 with Ktrain = 2 on the same two held-out models, without the estimates at N = Nfull .Fewer reference models give slightly larger estimates (Figure 13). This is the sign that (155) predicts, since the mixture-score error is nonnegative and decreases as Ktrain grows, and it means the main-text numbers at Ktrain = 2 are upper bounds on the estimates with more reference models. The fitted parameters change much less than the estimates do (Table 12). To first order, fewer reference models multiply the estimates by a nearly constant factor, which changes the prefactor k and not the exponents. The total number of trained models has the same effect (Figure 14 and Table 13). This matters because the datasets differ in it: on Fashion-MNIST we train ten models per (P, N ) pair, and on the other datasets four.
