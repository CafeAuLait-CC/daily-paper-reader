Abstract
Recovering an object’s 3D structure and camera motion from a few uncalibrated
views is a fundamental problem in computer vision. Classical structure from mo-
tion methods recover 3D geometry consistent with the input images but cannot
recover unseen regions; native generation methods produce plausible 3D objects
which are related but not necessarily faithful to the input images. Methods sim-
ply concatenating reconstruction and generation allow error to cascade across the
two stages, instead of producing a 3D object consistent with both the distribution
and the input. We propose GENSFM, which integrates image-based 3D recon-
struction with a native 3D diffusion model. In particular, we use a multi-modal
diffusion transformer to jointly denoise the shape latent and per-view pose latents
under shared self-attention, grounding generated geometry in the observed views
while using object-level priors to infer unobserved regions. A compact 3D-native
UV-volume tokenization keeps the joint sequence small as views are added, and
a pose-aware stage refines geometry and texture by sampling per-voxel image
features at the recovered camera poses. On Toys4k and GSO, for 4 uncalibrated

Introduction
Recovering 3D structure and camera motion from images is a foundational problem in computer
vision. Classical Structure-from-Motion and Multi-View Stereo pipelines [1, 2, 3] estimate camera
poses from multi-view correspondences and reconstruct geometry through photometric or geometric
consistency, often regularized by hand-crafted priors. While effective when input views provide
sufficient texture, overlap, and coverage, this geometric paradigm becomes brittle in sparse object-
centric settings, where only a few uncalibrated views are available and large object regions go
unrecovered.
Such settings are common in practice, from robotic manipulation to AR capture and content-creation
pipelines, all of which need a holistic 3D representation to reason about occluded surfaces and contact
regions. Sparse-view 3D object recovery therefore requires more than triangulating visible evidence:
it must infer a complete 3D explanation that is both consistent with the input images and plausible as
an object, jointly answering what the object is and where each view was taken from.
Recent learning-based methods relax the texture and overlap assumptions of classical pipelines by
introducing large-scale data-driven priors. Feed-forward reconstructors [4, 5, 6] predict camera
parameters and pixel-aligned 3D representations jointly, while pose-centric works [7, 8, 9, 10]
estimate relative camera motion under limited overlap and pose ambiguity. Both threads remain
primarily observation-driven: they recover what the input views support but do not explicitly model
the distribution of complete object shapes, leaving outputs incomplete in unobserved regions when
input coverage is poor.
A complementary line of work introduces generative priors learned from large-scale 3D datasets [11]
into sparse reconstruction. View-based generative SfM [12, 13, 14, 15] casts structure and motion as
a generative problem in view space, while reconstruct-then-generate pipelines [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]
pair feed-forward reconstructors with native 3D generators [21, 22, 23]. The latter family in particular
has shown that 3D generative priors can complete hidden geometry that observation-driven methods
cannot. These systems remain cascaded: an external module predicts poses or image-aligned
representations and the 3D generator only completes the result, propagating errors across stages and
preventing joint optimization of a single 3D hypothesis that explains all input views.
In this paper, we invert this pipeline: instead of using a generative prior to complete the output of a
feed-forward reconstructor, we treat the 3D generative prior itself as the primary reasoning mechanism
for sparse-view reconstruction. We propose GENSFM, an end-to-end framework that formulates joint
recovery of complete object structure and per-view camera motion as a single conditional inference
problem in a native 3D diffusion model, as shown in Fig. 1. The diffusion prior operates directly
on a native 3D representation throughout inference, simultaneously denoising shape and per-view
pose under joint self-attention. This grounds the generated object in the observed views while using
object-level regularities to infer unobserved geometry.
Realizing this formulation requires a single diffusion transformer that jointly denoises a shared
3D shape with a per-view pose latent bound to its input image, while keeping the per-view pose-
token overhead small as views are added. We adopt CUPID’s [24] 3D-native UV-volume pose
tokenization, but compress each view’s pose latent to a compact token grid, since the dense volume
over-parameterizes a low-DoF perspective projection. A learned view embedding tags each view’s
pose latent and image tokens, binding pose to its source image. Since neither stream natively carries
cross-view information, we prepend a small set of register tokens to the shape stream as a shared
channel for all views to communicate through self-attention.
Instead of relying on back-projection to recover surface texture like feed-forward methods [4], we
add a second stage of native 3D refinement and texturing [22] so that hidden regions stay coherent
with the observed views. We design a pose-aware fine stage that, using the cameras predicted in the
coarse stage, projects every active voxel into the input views and conditions denoising on per-voxel
semantic and texture features sampled and averaged across views.

• We propose GENSFM, a joint native-3D architecture that recasts a diffusion model from a post-

and texture features at every active voxel and averages them across views, sharpening surface

Related Work
Sparse-view object reconstruction must jointly answer two coupled questions: what is the 3D object,
and where was each view taken from? What requires both visible and hidden object structure, while
where requires the camera motion relating the input views. Existing methods typically excel on one
side or address the two with separate models stacked in series (Tab. 1).
Feed-forward pose and 3D reconstruction.
Pose-centric approaches [7, 8, 9, 10] target the where
side, regressing relative camera motion under limited overlap and pose ambiguity, but leave what
largely untouched. More general feed-forward reconstructors [4, 5, 6] predict dense pointmaps,
correspondences, and camera parameters jointly, addressing camera motion and visible geometry;
hidden geometry, however, is recovered only indirectly through learned priors.
Native 3D generation.
Native 3D generators trained on large 3D corpora [11], such as TripoSR [21],
TRELLIS [22], and Hunyuan3D [23], are strong on what: complete object structure is modeled
explicitly and hidden geometry is hallucinated from the prior. They do not target where: camera
motion is typically assumed and only weakly encoded through cross-attention, so applied directly to
uncalibrated sparse views they can produce plausible but misaligned geometry.
View-based generative SfM.
View-based generative SfM [12, 13, 14, 15] casts what and where
as a joint generative problem in view space; DiffusionSfM, for instance, diffuses ray origins and
endpoints to recover per-view geometry and camera motion jointly. Because diffusion operates over
3


view-based quantities (rays, pointmaps, depths), hidden what is recovered only insofar as it can be
back-projected from individual views.
Reconstruction with 3D generative priors.
Reconstruct-then-generate pipelines [16, 17, 18, 19,
20, 27] couple feed-forward reconstruction with native 3D generators: e.g., ReconViaGen [18] injects
VGGT [6] geometry tokens into a 3D generator. These pipelines recover hidden geometry through
the generative prior while grounding camera motion and visible structure with feed-forward geometry,
but solve what and where in separate stages, so errors from the reconstruction backbone propagate
into a 3D hypothesis that need not remain coherent with all input views.

Method
We frame this joint recovery as a coarse-to-fine generative problem in the latent space of a native 3D
diffusion model (Fig. 2). Building on TRELLIS’s two-stage architecture and CUPID’s 3D-native
pose representation (Sec. 3), Sec. 4.1 jointly generates 3D structure and multi-view poses with a
multi-modal DiT over per-view pose tokens, and Sec. 4.2 refines geometry and appearance via
per-voxel pose-sampled image conditioning.
4.1
3D structure and pose recovery from multi-view images
The coarse stage casts joint recovery of 3D structure and per-view camera poses as a single denoising
problem: each camera is encoded as a per-view 3D-native pose token, and shape and pose are
denoised jointly by a multi-modal DiT with joint self-attention over both streams.

Experiments
5.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets.
We train our model on four public 3D-asset datasets: Objaverse-XL [11] (GitHub and
Sketchfab partitions), HSSD [34], 3D-FUTURE [35], and ABO [36]. After TRELLIS’s aesthetic
filter (≥4.5), about 430K instances remain. Each is rendered following TRELLIS’s protocol, with
24 condition views at 1024×1024 from cameras uniformly sampled on the upper hemisphere. We
evaluate on two held-out test sets, Toys4k [25] (synthetic) and GSO [26] (real-captured), neither
overlapping the training corpus.
Evaluation protocol.
We assess photometric fidelity, 3D geometry, and camera-pose accuracy.
Each test set uses its first 400 objects with two disjoint 4-view sets, {0, 6, 12, 18} and {3, 9, 15, 21},
yielding 800 object–viewset instances for aggregation. As baselines fall into three capability families,
we apply the most suitable evaluation protocol to each: (i) least squares on predicted vs ground-truth
input-view pointmaps for feed-forward reconstruction, tight only with accurate predicted geometry
and poses; (ii) similarity ICP on sampled point clouds for 3D generation; and (iii) gauge-invariant pair-
wise relative-pose comparison on unordered input-view pairs for pose-predicting methods. GENSFM
is reevaluated under each applicable protocol; comparisons are restricted to methods sharing a
table and protocol. We report PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS [37] on 512×512 novel views at the 8
held-out condition-view indices {2, 4, 8, 10, 14, 16, 20, 22}; bidirectional Chamfer Distance (CD;
reported as the median across objects, since a few severe failures dominate the mean) and F-scores
at τ ∈{0.01, 0.05}; and Relative Rotation Error (RRE) with Acc@{5◦, 15◦}, Relative Translation
Error (RTE), and mean absolute FOV error (RFOV; only for methods that predict intrinsics). See
App. A.1 for the full alignment protocol and App. A.2 for the pose metric definitions.

Conclusion
We presented GENSFM, which recasts a native 3D diffusion model as the primary inference mecha-
nism for sparse-view object reconstruction, jointly denoising shape and per-view camera poses in a
single multi-modal diffusion transformer. Three choices keep the formulation tractable: a UV-volume
pose latent compressed to a compact token grid, a unified multi-modal sequence that places shape
and per-view pose tokens in a single self-attention, and a pose-aware fine stage that conditions each
voxel on features sampled at its pose-aligned image projection. On Toys4k and GSO, GENSFM
outperforms feed-forward, 3D-generation, and pose-prediction baselines on photometric, geometric,
and pose metrics simultaneously, recovering both extrinsics and intrinsics to high accuracy without
any external pose estimator or post-hoc optimization.
Limitations and future work.
Our results are restricted to object-centric inputs at N=4; extending
to larger view counts, scene-level inputs, and in-the-wild captures with looser segmentation is a
natural next step. Under distribution shift, large pose/calibration errors may misregister fine-stage
image features, degrading local refinement while the coarse 3D prior preserves global completion.
The model also inherits the object distribution and resolution limits of its TRELLIS backbone, so
scaling the underlying generator should directly improve sparse-view recovery.

Technical appendices and supplementary material
A.1
Alignment protocol
Our baselines differ in what they natively output (input-view pointmaps, canonical pose-free 3D, or
input-camera poses), so a single shared alignment would unfairly discard the structure each method
already provides. We therefore align each prediction with the protocol best matched to its output
family before evaluating, and GENSFM is evaluated under each applicable family using that family’s
protocol so all comparisons within a table are like-for-like.
Input-view pointmap least squares (Table 2).
This protocol applies to feed-forward reconstruction
methods (LucidFusion, FreeSplatter, ReconViaGen), which already place geometry in a noisy estimate
of the input-camera frame. For each input view, we back-project the per-pixel predicted depth at the
predicted camera into 3D, alpha-mask the result, randomly subsample to at most 50K points per view,
and pair it with the GT pointmap obtained by back-projecting GT depth at the GT camera. Stacking
the per-view pairs with equal aggregate weight per view yields one set of 3D-to-3D correspondences
per object, which we fit with a closed-form Umeyama similarity [38]. The resulting transform is
applied once to the full predicted 3D before all photometric and geometric evaluation; we log the
per-object LSQ residual as an alignment-fidelity diagnostic. Note that this protocol exercises both
halves of feed-forward reconstruction: the matched correspondences require the predicted geometry
to align with the predicted cameras, so a method with weak geometry, weak pose recovery, or any
mismatch between the two will produce noisy correspondences and a loose Umeyama fit, inflating all
downstream metrics.
Similarity ICP (Table 3).
This protocol applies to 3D generation methods (TRELLIS-S, TRELLIS-
M, SAM 3D), which output a canonical pose-free 3D, and to GENSFM when evaluated under the
same family. To obtain the prediction-side point cloud uniformly across output formats (whether
the prediction is a mesh or a set of 3D Gaussians), we render depth from a set of orbiting random
views around the prediction and back-project the depth maps into 3D, which yields a well-covered
surface point cloud without surface-sampling biases specific to the underlying representation. The
GT-side cloud is sampled directly on the GT mesh surface. We initialize alignment by sweeping the
24 orientation-preserving signed axis permutations of the PCA-aligned bounding box and keep the
candidate with the lowest post-ICP residual; we then refine with similarity ICP and use the resulting
transform to map the prediction into the GT frame for evaluation.

A.3
Pose VAE
Architecture and training.
The pose VAE is a 3D-convolutional encoder–decoder over a 643
voxel grid with a deliberately aggressive bottleneck: four 2× spatial downsamples compress the
input into a 43 token grid with 8 channels per token. This compression is feasible because each
per-voxel target is a smooth function of voxel position and a low-DoF camera, so the latent does not
need to absorb high-frequency content. Per voxel the encoder receives three input channels (a binary
in-frustum indicator plus the two pixel coordinates obtained by projecting the voxel center under the
input camera), and the decoder emits the same three channels through a classification head (visibility
mask) and a regression head (pixel UV).
We use a dice loss on the visibility classifier and an L1 loss on the UV regression, with a small KL
weight (10−3) so the latent stays close to a standard Gaussian for the downstream diffusion stage.
The training distribution matches inference: each minibatch sample takes a real camera pose from the
multi-view renders of a sampled training object and projects it on the fly into the 643 voxel grid. We
optimize for 1M steps with AdamW (lr 3×10−4, weight decay 10−3) on a cosine schedule.
Out-of-frame voxels.
Voxel centers can project outside the input image. We handle this by
clamping target UVs to [0, 1]2 rather than dropping out-of-frame voxels, keeping the decoder output
well-defined everywhere. The visibility channel encodes the same in-/out-of-frame information as
a binary indicator with a small 1% inset margin (0.01 ≤u, v ≤0.99); the inference DLT solve
uses this channel to restrict to in-frustum voxels, so saturated boundary UVs never contaminate the
recovered pose. The UV reconstruction loss is applied uniformly to all voxels rather than only the
in-frustum subset, keeping saturated-boundary regions consistent between encoder and decoder.

A.4
Image conditioning across stages
Coarse stage: dual-stream encoder and token fusion.
Each view is encoded by two comple-
mentary streams that fuse into a single per-view token sequence for the coarse DiT to cross-attend
to. A frozen DINOv2-L/14 with registers at 518×518 produces 1374 tokens per view (CLS + 4
registers + a 37×37 patch grid) at 1024 channels, supplying object-level semantics. In parallel,
a lightweight 4-level CNN at 296×296 (5×5 convolutions, strides {1, 2, 2, 2}, output channels
[6, 6, 6, 8]) is patchified down to the same 37×37 grid, with per-token contributions [384, 96, 24, 8]
channel-concatenating into a 512-dim local-texture feature. We append the 512 local channels onto
the 1024 semantic channels at every patch token (non-patch tokens zero-pad on the local side), giving
a uniform per-token width of 1536 and a cross-attention key/value of length N × 1374. The two
streams are deliberately complementary: semantic anchors pose to image content, while local texture
restores the pixel-faithful appearance cues the semantic encoder has discarded, letting cross-attention
recover accurate cameras without polluting the joint self-attention with raw image tokens.

active voxels at 643 under the cameras already recovered by the coarse stage. Cross-attending

camera, and visual features are bilinearly sampled at that pixel. The visual encoder is a ConvNeXt-

depthwise kernel at internal width 32, with a 2× downsample between stages. Each stage exposes
a 4-channel tap (LayerNorm + 1×1 Conv); the four taps sampled at uj,i and channel-concatenated
yield a 16-channel multi-scale texture feature ctex

at the same per-voxel projection and re-encoded through the frozen SLat encoder, placing them on
the SLat target’s latent manifold and giving a compact 24-channel per-voxel conditioning vector. This
keeps fine-stage cost proportional to active voxels rather than view tokens, while the multi-scale taps
let each voxel combine coarse contour cues with fine local texture without inflating the per-voxel
width.
A.5
Implementation details
Pose VAE training.
The pose VAE of App. A.3 is trained for 1M steps on 8 H200 GPUs with batch
size 4 per GPU, using AdamW (lr 3×10−4, weight decay 10−3) under a cosine schedule with 1000
warmup steps decaying to 10−5, EMA 0.9999, and FP16 mixed precision. The same recipe trains the
43, 83, and 163 latent variants of Tab. 6.
Coarse-stage shape-pose DiT training.
The coarse stage is fine-tuned for 1M steps on 8 H200
GPUs at global batch size 64 (8 per GPU, split into two gradient-accumulation microbatches),
initialized from a prior single-view coarse-stage checkpoint of ours (itself from the pretrained
TRELLIS-image-large SS DiT). Each instance contributes N=4 random condition views uniformly
sampled from the 24 rendered views of the object; each input image is crop-resized at a 1.2×
tightness ratio around the alpha-mask bounding box and bilinearly resampled to 518×518 for the
frozen DINOv2 ViT-L/14-with-registers encoder. The DiT operates on 4096 shape, 256 pose, and
128 shared register tokens, with cross-attention over N × 1374 = 5496 image-condition tokens
augmented by the convolutional pyramid of Eq. 6. We use AdamW (lr 10−4, no weight decay), CFG
dropout 0.1, the same cosine schedule and EMA as above, and timesteps drawn from a logit-normal
distribution with mean 1.0 and std 1.0 (σmin=10−5). At each step we randomly choose between
two timestep-coupling modes: with probability 0.8, the shape stream and all pose streams share
a single timestep; with probability 0.2, each stream draws an independent timestep. The shared
mode dominates so the model stays well-calibrated to the synchronized inference schedule, while the
per-pose mode supplies enough timestep diversity that the per-modality AdaLN modulation actually
learns to handle independent noise levels.
Fine-stage SLat DiT training.
The fine stage is fine-tuned independently for 1M steps on 4
H200 GPUs at global batch size 32 (8 per GPU), initialized from TRELLIS’s pretrained SLat DiT
checkpoint. It denoises up to L≤32,768 active voxels at 643; ground-truth cameras and occupancy are
used during training so the fine stage learns from clean projections. Each instance again contributes
15


N=4 random condition views, with per-voxel features pooled across views by mean (16-channel
ConvNeXt-pyramid texture and 8-channel SLat-encoded semantic) and the cross-attention condition
the per-view mean of DINOv2 patches. We use AdamW (lr 10−4, weight decay 10−3), CFG dropout
0.1, and the same cosine schedule and logit-normal timestep distribution as the coarse stage.
Inference.
The coarse stage runs a 25-step flow-Euler sampler over the joint shape-pose sequence
at classifier-free guidance strength 7.5, active on the noise interval t ∈[0.5, 1.0] with a guidance
time-rescale factor of 3.0 [39]. We then decode each per-view pose latent through the pose VAE
and recover P∗
i by the DLT solve of Eq. 2. The fine stage runs a 25-step flow-Euler sampler with
sequential multi-view fusion at CFG strength 3.0 on the same [0.5, 1.0] interval and rescale factor,
conditioned on the recovered cameras and on the per-voxel pose-sampled features of Eq. 7.
A.6
Broader impacts
GENSFM produces a 3D representation of an object from a few uncalibrated images, jointly recov-
ering shape and camera. The most direct positive applications are in robotics (object-level scene
understanding from a small number of viewpoints), augmented and virtual reality (capturing personal
items as 3D assets for placement in virtual environments), and content creation (3D meshes from
reference photos as a starting point for downstream editing). Joint recovery of camera intrinsics and
extrinsics with shape removes the calibration friction that has limited deployment of feed-forward
reconstruction methods in casually-captured settings.
The negative considerations are those shared with image-conditioned 3D generation more broadly:
the resulting 3D models could be used to fabricate convincing 3D content of real-world objects,
including objects belonging to others, without their consent; coupled with downstream rendering
pipelines, this could enable image-only digital reproduction at a scale that traditional photogrammetry
has not previously allowed. The model is trained only on common, non-sensitive object categories
from public 3D-asset datasets, and recovery quality degrades on out-of-distribution categories, which
provides a partial natural mitigation. Released model checkpoints will carry an attribution requirement
and an acceptable-use note as the safeguard accompanying our open release.
