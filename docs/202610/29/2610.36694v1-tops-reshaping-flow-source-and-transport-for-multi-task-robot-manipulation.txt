# TOPS: Reshaping Flow Source and Transport for Multi-Task Robot Manipulation 

Tuo An 1, Chuhao Zhou 1, Jiahua Dong 1, Jingliang Li 1, Jiaqi Bai 1, Jindou Jia 1,†, Jianfei Yang 1,†

> 1

Flow-based policies for language-conditioned multi-task manipulation commonly share a single Gaus-sian source prior and a common linear transport path across tasks. However, reliably executing multiple tasks with distinct motion requirements remains challenging for a shared policy. We introduce 

TOPS (Task-Oriented Priors and Schedules), which adapts the flow source prior and interpolation schedule to each task without modifying the network architecture. TOPS first constructs multimodal task sources from reverse-mapped expert action sequences, allowing language instructions to select where generation begins. Guided by source-target covariance mismatch, it then slows the transition toward expert actions early in denoising and accelerates it later along directions requiring expansion. Under a Gaussian endpoint model, our analysis shows that this schedule adjustment can lower an upper bound on velocity-prediction risk. Experiments on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and CALVIN demonstrate improved performance across in-distribution, perturbation and cross-environment settings, alongside better data efficiency. Using one integration step, TOPS raises LIBERO-10 success from 53.44% to 69.67% compared with ten-step Standard Flow Matching (FM), while reducing inference time to approximately one-sixth of the baseline. TOPS also yields consistent gains when transferred to the large-scale flow-based π0.5 policy across all four evaluated settings. Real-world experiments further demonstrate improved task success, including an increase from 20% with standard FM to 80% with TOPS on the upper-drawer opening task.

# 1 Introduction 

Real-world manipulation exhibits substantial behavioral diversity. Different tasks require distinct motion patterns, and even a given goal can be achieved through different contact strategies, motion trajectories, and temporal execution patterns. Diffusion and flow-matching models (Funk et al., 2024; Chi et al., 2025; Braun et al., 2024) capture such multimodal action distributions directly and have become widely used in imitation learning. This expressive power has also carried the generative paradigm from task-specific visuomotor control to language-conditioned multi-task and generalist robot policies (Yan et al., 2025; Black et al., 2025b,a). In this setting, a shared policy must accommodate the motion requirements of different tasks and execute each task reliably. However, a shared policy can perform well on some tasks yet struggle to execute others reliably (Haldar et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023a; Ren et al., 2026). A shared policy must capture multiple action modes within a task and substantially different action distributions across tasks (Wu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026). Existing approaches introduce or redesign policy components to improve multi-task performance, using feature modulation (Haldar et al., 2024), visual selection (Huang et al., 2025), instruction-generated parameters (Ren et al., 2026), and expert routing (Deng et al., 2026). In flow policies (Lipman et al., 2022), the network must recover distinct task-specific action distributions from a shared Gaussian source under a common linear transport path. Before changing the policy architecture as in prior work, we step back to the generative process itself and ask: could adapting the source prior and transport to each task improve multi-task performance without changing the policy architecture? To investigate, we reverse a trained multi-task flow policy and trace expert actions back to their source samples (Tang et al., 2026). 1arXiv:2610.36694v1 [cs.RO] 27 Oct 2026 As shown in Figs. 1(a,b), reverse-mapped expert actions exhibit task-dependent organization under the warm-up policy, with further subclusters within individual tasks. These patterns motivate us to investigate whether fitting task-specific sources to the reverse images and directly denoising from the corresponding region can improve policy learning. To further understand what changes are needed to reach expert actions, we analyze the covariance mismatch between these sources and the corresponding expert-action distributions using a Gaussian optimal-transport map. Its eigenvectors define orthogonal axes, each representing a weighted combination of action components across timesteps. We find expert actions have substantially greater standard deviation than source samples only along a few axes. We refer to this source-to-target increase as expansion .Yet conventional flow matching (Lipman et al., 2022; Black et al., 2025a) starts every task from a single task-independent Gaussian and applies the same linear interpolation schedule along every axis. This leaves the network to recover task-specific action structure from shared noise, while interpolation timing remains unchanged despite the unequal source-to-target changes revealed by our analysis. Motivated by these findings, we introduce TOPS (Task-Oriented Priors and Schedules) for multi-task flow policies. It fits a Gaussian mixture with low-rank covariance structure (Tipping and Bishop, 1999) to each task’s reverse-mapped samples, allowing language instructions to select the source. Along expansion axes, TOPS slows interpolation toward expert actions early and accelerates it later, retaining linear interpolation elsewhere. Under a Gaussian endpoint model (Albergo et al., 2025), we show that adapting interpolation can lower an upper bound on velocity-prediction risk. This risk captures the irreducible error that arises when the same interpolated action sequence corresponds to different velocity targets. On LIBERO-10, our schedule reduces estimated time-integrated directional risk on expansion axes by 18 .5% relative to linear interpolation (Fig. 10). In controlled 2D experiments with few integration steps, it also places more samples in target regions than linear or fast-then-slow schedules (Fig. 7). Together, these choices support efficient few-step action generation. We evaluate TOPS on LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023a), LIBERO-Plus (Fei et al., 2025), CALVIN (Mees et al., 2022), and three real-world multi-task suites. With the same backbone as the baselines, TOPS achieves the highest mean performance across two in-distribution and two out-of-distribution settings, covering perturbations and unseen environments, using one schedule-aware step. Compared with ten-step Standard FM (Lipman et al., 2022), it raises LIBERO-10 success from 53.44% to 69.67% while reducing inference latency to approximately

Gains also extend to the large-scale π0.5 policy and real-world tasks.

Source distributions for generative policies. Flow reversal steering (Tang et al., 2026) uses inverted actions for direct steering or learning observation-conditioned noise policies while keeping the base flow frozen. TOPS fits low-rank Gaussian mixtures to each task’s reverse images pooled across observations and trains the velocity field under adapted sources and paths. 

Coupling and path design in flow matching. Prior work optimizes endpoint couplings (Tong et al., 2024; Sochopoulos et al., 2025) or straightens trajectories by retraining on flow-generated pairs (Liu et al., 2023b). MeanFlow (Geng et al., 2025) learns interval-averaged velocity for one-step generation. Other methods approximate geodesics under data-induced metrics (Kapuśniak et al., 2024) or adapt global schedules to velocity profiles or regression difficulty (Bondar, 2026; Tania and Khan, 2026). TOPS retains independent endpoint sampling and uses Gaussian OT to identify task-specific expansion directions for slow-then-fast scheduling.

# 4 Method 

4.1 Overview 

Building on the preceding analysis, TOPS adapts source distributions and interpolation schedules to each task (Fig. 2). We fit multimodal sources to reverse-mapped expert actions and use source–target covariance mismatch to guide slow-then-fast scheduling along expansion axes, retaining linear interpolation elsewhere. Both components are constructed offline and fixed during continuation training of the velocity field. At inference, a learned language selector chooses the source and associated schedule, and a schedule-aware solver generates the action chunk. The following subsections detail these components and their use in training and inference.

4.4 Training and Inference 

We continue training from θ0 with qτ and Kτ fixed. We use the FM objective (Eq. (1)), drawing x0 ∼ qτ and constructing the interpolated states and velocity targets according to Eq. (10). The lightweight language selector ψϕ is trained offline and remains fixed throughout policy training and evaluation. At inference, it maps instruction ℓ to ˆτ = ψϕ(ℓ), selecting the corresponding qˆτ and Kˆτ . We then sample x0 ∼ qˆτ and integrate vθ with the associated schedule-aware solver. By default, generation uses one two-stage step requiring two velocity-network evaluations. Appendices A.8 and A.10 detail source selection and the solver, respectively; Algorithm 1 summarizes the full pipeline.

5.1.2 TOPS learns more effectively from fewer demonstrations 

Since TOPS fits task-specific sources from reversed expert demonstrations, this raises a natural question: can this source construction retain benefits when demonstrations are limited? To investigate this question, we train each method on 5%, 10%, 20%, 30%, and 50% of the demonstrations and evaluate the four ID and OOD settings using the same protocol as above. Fig. 3 shows that TOPS outperforms Standard FM in all 20 configurations and matches or exceeds the strongest baseline in 14. With 5% demonstrations, TOPS achieves 

15.50% success on LIBERO-10 and 8.00% on LIBERO-Plus, compared with 8.50% and 4.10% for Standard FM, respectively. On CALVIN ABC →D, TOPS achieves average completed chain length of 1.009 using 30% of the demonstrations, exceeding all baselines trained on 50% (PriorGrad performs best at 0.925). 

5.1.3 TOPS Transfers to a Large-Scale Flow-Based VLA 

To test whether these gains extend to a larger flow-based policy, we apply TOPS to π0.5 (Black et al., 2025a), using the official pretrained model and the same LoRA configuration as the baseline. Both variants use the same total budget of 14K training steps; TOPS resumes from an intermediate baseline checkpoint, changing only the source distribution, transport target, and sampler. At inference, the baseline uses ten Euler steps, while TOPS uses one two-stage schedule-aware step (NFE = 2 ). As shown in Fig. 4, TOPS improves π0.5 in all four evaluated settings. 5.2 Real-World Experiments 

Experimental setup and protocol. We evaluate three multi-task suites on a Galaxea R1 Lite, actuating only its right arm and two-finger gripper. Policies receive RGB images from the head and wrist cameras, proprioception, and language instructions. Within each suite, all candidate targets are present in a shared scene, and the instruction specifies the task. Fig. 5 shows the task setups. Drawer Opening requires opening the instructed upper or lower drawer. Color Matching requires placing the instructed purple or black cube into its matching tray. Pick-Vegetable requires placing the instructed object (daikon, corn, mango, or peach) in a collection box. We collect 50 demonstrations per task for Drawer Opening and Color Matching, and 30 for Pick-Vegetable. We evaluate TOPS, Standard FM, and PriorGrad on eight tasks, with 20 rollouts per task (160 per method). We report per-task success rates and compare the pooled success rates of TOPS and Standard FM using a two-sided two-proportion z-test. Implementation and statistical details appear in App. A.5.2. 

Multi-task manipulation performance. Fig. 5 shows that TOPS matches both baselines on daikon and corn (100% success) and achieves higher success on the other six tasks. Compared with Standard FM, success on the upper and lower drawers rises from 20% each to 80% and 50% , respectively. On Color Matching, TOPS reaches 95% and 75% on the purple and black tasks, respectively, improving both by 20 percentage points over Standard FM. On mango and peach, success increases from Standard FM’s 0% and 40% to 50% and 

> 70%

, respectively. Pooled across the eight tasks, TOPS succeeds in 124/160 rollouts (77.50%), compared with 82/160 (51.25%) for Standard FM, a statistically significant improvement of 26.25 percentage points (z = 4 .90 , p < 0.001 ). 

5.3 Ablation Study 

Do both components contribute? As shown in Tab. 1, source-only variants with fixed Mτ = 2 or task-wise mixture counts outperform Standard FM in all four simulation settings under the same training budget and the reported inference budgets. With the Mτ = 2 source unchanged, nonlinear scheduling and its integrator jointly raise LIBERO-10 success from 59.17% to 69.67%. Robomimic (Mandlekar et al., 2021) provides a complementary comparison with a fixed Gaussian source (App. A.6), while App. A.4 compares solvers at a fixed checkpoint. 

How sensitive is TOPS to the number of mixture components? We vary Mτ ∈ { 1, 2, 3, 5, 8} in full TOPS on LIBERO-10, using the same count across tasks and fixing covariance rank, source scale, and training budget. All tested values outperform Standard FM (Fig. 6). Mτ ∈ { 2, 3} yields the strongest results, with modest degradation for the other tested counts. This preference for small mixtures is consistent with the within-task structure in Fig. 1 and supports our default Mτ = 2 on LIBERO-10. 

Do directions and scheduling matter? With the source fixed, we test random directions, shared trigonometric interpolation, and fast-then-slow timing along expansion directions. Random directions substantially reduce performance in both CALVIN settings (Fig. 6). In a controlled 2D experiment, slow-then-fast timing also 9improves few-step generation over linear and fast-then-slow schedules (Fig. 7). Matched LIBERO-10 MLPs without observation conditioning show 22 .1% lower time-integrated velocity-prediction error on expansion directions with slow-then-fast timing and 28 .0% higher error with fast-then-slow timing, both relative to linear interpolation (App. A.9.2). 

# 6 Conclusion 

TOPS incorporates task structure into source priors and interpolation schedules while preserving the policy backbone. Reverse-mapped expert actions guide multimodal source fitting, and an upper bound on velocity-prediction risk under independent Gaussian endpoints motivates slow-then-fast scheduling along expansion directions. Experiments demonstrate improved multi-task performance, robustness, and data efficiency with one inference step, together with higher real-world task success. 

Limitations. TOPS incurs an offline cost to construct task-specific sources and schedules, which remain fixed during subsequent training and deployment. It selects among predefined tasks and uses a fixed action-chunk length; generalization to unseen task identities is outside our evaluation. A.2 Language-based Source Selection 

TOPS separates source selection from the policy’s conditioning pathway. Task annotations organize the offline construction of the source distributions {qτ }τ ∈T and their associated scheduling matrices {Kτ }τ ∈T .At inference, a learned selector ψϕ maps the input instruction ℓ to a source index ˆτ ∈ T :

ˆτ = ψϕ(ℓ), x(0) ∼ qˆτ . (11) The selected source is used with its corresponding fixed scheduling matrix Kˆτ .In the main-text evaluations, the learned selector operates separately from the velocity network. It uses MiniLM (Wang et al., 2020) sentence features to predict the task index, while the policy condition c is constructed from observations and the provided instruction through the original observation encoders and frozen CLIP text encoder, independently of that prediction. Action generation then integrates vθ (x, t, c) from the selected source using its associated schedule. The selector implementation is provided in App. A.5.1. App. A.8 compares language-based task-identification methods on 358 instruction formulations covering 34 known CALVIN tasks.

A.5 Implementation details  

> A.5.1 Implementation Details for Simulation Experiments

Policy architecture. All methods share a diffusion Transformer (DiT) backbone with four blocks, a hidden dimension of 512, and eight attention heads. A ResNet-18 encoder extracts visual features, while a frozen CLIP ViT-B/32 encoder provides 512-dimensional language embeddings. These features are combined with proprioceptive observations to condition the DiT through adaptive layer normalization. The network predicts velocities for action chunks, and TOPS preserves this shared architecture. We infer the task index from the instruction using a frozen MiniLM sentence encoder (Wang et al., 2020; Reimers and Gurevych, 2019) followed by a five-model ensemble of lightweight MLP classifiers. The classifiers are trained offline on the benchmark-provided training instructions and their task labels using a standard cross-entropy objective and AdamW with a learning rate of 10 −3, independently of the policy. The sentence encoder remains frozen, and the trained selector is held fixed during policy training and evaluation. For TOPS, its predicted task index selects both qˆτ and Kˆτ ; the source-only variants use the same prediction to select qˆτ . The policy retains its original CLIP-based language conditioning. 

Training and hyperparameters. We first train the standard FM policy for 300 warm-up epochs. We then construct the task-specific sources qτ and scheduling matrices Kτ offline and continue training for another 300 epochs, keeping both fixed. All compared methods are trained for the same total of 600 epochs, including any warm-up stages, to ensure a fair comparison. Each epoch comprises 250 optimizer updates. We evaluate the final exponential moving average (EMA) policy. The main hyperparameters are summarized in Tab. 7. 

Random seeds and statistical reporting. We distinguish training seeds from evaluation seeds. An independent training run repeats the stochastic training pipeline; for TOPS, this includes warm-up, source and schedule construction, and continuation training. An evaluation seed controls rollout stochasticity for a fixed trained checkpoint. We first compute the benchmark metric separately for each evaluation seed. When multiple independently trained checkpoints are evaluated, we average evaluation repeats within each checkpoint before computing statistics across training runs. For fixed-checkpoint experiments, statistics are computed across evaluation seeds. Each experiment specifies the corresponding level of repetition. Unless otherwise specified, reported standard deviations are sample standard deviations, computed with denominator N − 1 for N repeated measurements. 

Method hyperparameters. For each task source, Mτ specifies the number of mixture components and r

Baseline implementation protocol. We compare baseline adaptations within a shared policy framework, using the same observation encoder, language conditioning, DiT backbone, action representation, and observation and execution horizons. Each normalized action chunk contains 16 ten-dimensional actions ( D = 160 ). All methods use the same optimizer settings and receive 600 epochs of policy training, including any warm-up stage. Continuation-based methods initialize from the EMA parameters of a shared 300-epoch Standard FM checkpoint and train for another 300 epochs. Offline prior fitting and teacher-pair generation are performed separately before continuation. Unless otherwise specified, inference uses ten explicit-midpoint steps, corresponding to 20 velocity-network evaluations (NFE = 20 ). 

Standard FM. We independently sample Gaussian sources x0 ∼ N (0 , 0.32ID) and expert action chunks x1.Training uses linear interpolation xt = (1 − t)x0 + tx 1, with t ∼ U [0 , 1] , and mean-squared regression of the displacement x1 − x0. The same source distribution and objective are used throughout training. 

OT-CFM. We replace independent endpoint pairing with minibatch optimal transport based on squared Euclidean distances in the flattened action-chunk space. The uniform-marginal transport problem is solved using exact earth mover’s distance, with Hungarian assignment as a fallback. Matching operates across the full minibatch using action-space costs. Only source samples are reassigned; expert chunks retain their associated observations and language instructions. The remaining training procedure follows Standard FM. 

2-ReFlow. We use the frozen warm-up EMA policy to generate four noise–endpoint pairs per demonstration window, conditioned on its observations and language instruction. Teacher generation uses 50 explicit-midpoint steps (NFE = 100 ). During continuation, one cached pair is randomly selected per window, and the student learns the linear FM objective between the paired endpoints. Inference uses one explicit-midpoint step (NFE 

= 2 ). 21 RF-distill. We use the same cached teacher pairs as 2-ReFlow and train the network to predict their complete displacement at t = 0 . A fresh Gaussian sample is mapped to an action chunk through ˆx1 = z + vθ(z, 0, c ),requiring one network evaluation. RF-distill directly distills the warm-up teacher and is trained as a separate continuation branch from 2-ReFlow, within the same 600-epoch policy-training budget. 

MeanFlow. We augment the shared DiT with an endpoint-time embedding branch to predict interval-average velocities. The additional branch is initialized to preserve the warm-up network’s predictions. Training uses the source-to-data form of the MeanFlow identity, with stop-gradient targets computed through Jacobian–vector products. We use time-reversed logit-normal sampling with parameters μ = −0.4 and σ = 1 .0, and set the two interval endpoints equal for 75% of examples. Detached inverse-error weights are normalized within each minibatch. Both the main and limited-data experiments use one full-interval update (NFE = 1 ), without classifier-free guidance. 

PriorGrad. We adapt the data-dependent prior to flow matching by fitting a diagonal Gaussian to each task’s normalized training actions. The ten-dimensional mean and standard deviations are shared across the action horizon, with independent samples at each position. Standard deviations are floored at 0.01 . Training uses linear interpolation and inverse-variance-weighted velocity regression. The weights are clipped at their global 99th percentile and normalized to mean one. The fitted prior remains fixed during policy training and inference. 

MM-FM source adaptation. For the MM-FM entry, we fit a task-specific diagonal Bayesian Gaussian mixture to normalized expert action chunks. We use up to eight components with a Dirichlet-process weight prior and at most 40,000 unpadded training windows per task. Components with weights below 0.01 are removed, the remaining weights are renormalized, and standard deviations are floored at 0.01 . The fitted source remains fixed during policy training. This adaptation evaluates the mixture-source component using independent source–target pairing within each task, in place of the original mode-dependent coupling. Training otherwise uses the standard linear FM objective. 

LeaP. We learn a proprioception-conditioned diagonal Gaussian source with a 16 → 512 → 20 MLP. Its input comprises two normalized proprioceptive observations, and its outputs specify ten-dimensional means and log variances shared across the action horizon. Visual and language features condition the velocity network. The source is initialized to the warm-up Gaussian and optimized jointly with the policy through reparameterized sampling. The training objective combines flow matching, Gaussian negative log-likelihood, and symmetric source–action contrastive alignment, with respective weights 1.0, 1.0, and 0.3. Alignment uses 128-dimensional projections and temperature 0.07 . Our adaptation uses the shared image-based backbone and normalized raw proprioception, with explicit-midpoint integration. The main experiments use ten steps (NFE = 20 ), and limited-data experiments use five (NFE = 10 ). NFE counts velocity-network evaluations and excludes the prior MLP forward pass. 

Deterministic action-chunk regression. We use the shared backbone with a zero action input and fixed time 

t = 0 to predict the normalized expert chunk directly with an L1 loss. The predictor uses neither a variational latent model nor temporal ensembling. Inference requires one network evaluation, followed by the same action unnormalization and eight-action execution used by the other policies. 

Comparison scope. The reported results compare these specified adaptations under a shared policy framework, a common policy-training epoch budget, and the stated inference budgets. For teacher-based methods, continuation fits cached teacher outputs, so performance depends jointly on the warm-up teacher and the student’s approximation at its inference budget.  

> A.5.2 Implementation Details for Real-World Experiments

Robot platform. All real-world experiments are conducted on a Galaxea R1 Lite, a wheeled dual-arm mobile manipulator whose upper body consists of two 6-DoF arms with parallel two-finger grippers, an articulated torso, and a head unit carrying RGB cameras. We restrict control to the right arm and its gripper; the left 22 arm, the torso, and the mobile base are held in a fixed configuration throughout both demonstration collection and evaluation, so that the head-camera viewpoint is identical in the two phases. 

Observation and action spaces. Policies receive two RGB streams, one from the head-mounted camera and one from the right wrist-mounted camera. Each frame is padded to a square aspect ratio and resized to 

256 × 256 . Proprioception is a 7-dimensional vector containing the six right-arm joint positions and the gripper opening. The language instruction of each task is encoded offline by the frozen CLIP ViT-B/32 text encoder into a 512-dimensional embedding, which is projected and concatenated with the visual and proprioceptive features to form the conditioning vector of the DiT backbone. Actions are 7-dimensional targets comprising absolute positions for the same six joints and a gripper command. We use the same prediction, observation, and execution horizons as in simulation ( 16 /2/8). 

Demonstration collection. Demonstrations are collected by human teleoperation and recorded at 15 Hz, with camera frames and proprioceptive states synchronized to a common clock. Within each suite, all candidate targets are present in a shared scene layout, and object placements are varied across demonstrations within a fixed workspace region; consequently the language instruction is the only signal that identifies the intended task at the start of an episode. Every demonstration is annotated with the instruction it executes. 

Training. Real-world policies follow the same two-stage recipe and the same optimization settings as in simulation (Tab. 7). We first train the standard FM policy for 500 warm-up epochs. We then construct the task-specific sources qτ and the scheduling matrices Kτ offline from this frozen warm-up policy, and continue training for another 500 epochs with both held fixed. All compared methods are trained for the same total of 1000 epochs, including any warm-up stage, and we deploy the final exponential moving average (EMA) policy. Source and schedule construction reuses the simulation procedure: expert action chunks are mapped backward through the warm-up flow with 10 explicit-midpoint steps to form the per-task reverse-image banks, each bank is fit with Mτ mixture components carrying a rank-r covariance term, the pooled source standard deviation is calibrated to σ0, and the scheduling strengths are bounded by κ with the sensitivity γ computed offline per suite according to Eq. 15. The resulting values are listed in Tab. 8. 

Evaluation protocol. Each task is evaluated over 20 rollouts. A rollout is scored as a binary success: for Drawer Opening, the instructed drawer is pulled open; for Color Matching, the instructed cube is released inside the tray of the matching color; for Pick-Vegetable, the instructed object is released inside the collection box. A rollout that does not satisfy its criterion within a fixed time budget is counted as a failure. All methods are evaluated under the same object layouts and the same reset procedure. 

Aggregate statistical analysis. We compare TOPS and Standard FM by pooling binary outcomes across the eight tasks, with 20 rollouts per task and method. Each task therefore receives equal weight in the pooled success rate. The success/failure counts are 124/36 for TOPS and 82/78 for Standard FM. Treating rollout outcomes as independent observations, we apply a two-sided two-proportion z-test with a pooled variance estimate and no continuity correction. Under the null hypothesis of equal aggregate success rates, the pooled proportion is ˆp = (124 + 82) /(160 + 160) = 0 .64375 . The test yields z = 4 .903 

and p = 9 .45 × 10 −7, supporting the aggregate improvement of TOPS over Standard FM. This analysis is conditional on the deployed policy checkpoints and the eight evaluated tasks.
