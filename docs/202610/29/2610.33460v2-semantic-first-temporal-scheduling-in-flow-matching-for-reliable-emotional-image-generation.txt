# Semantic-First: Temporal Scheduling in Flow Matching for Reliable Emotional Image Generation 

Zhenxuan Wang ∗

> Lanzhou University

Abstract 

Affect-controlled image generation should change how a scene feels while preserving what the prompt specifies. We study the temporal allocation of stochastic exploration and policy optimization in flow-matching models as a means of controlling this balance. During RL training, our semantic-first 

schedule uses deterministic ordinary differential equation (ODE) sampling during the high-noise phase and confines stochastic differential equation (SDE) exploration and group relative policy optimization (GRPO) to a late window. Controlled experiments separate exploration location, optimization location, and terminal-step sampling, showing distinct semantic benefits from late ex-ploration and late updates. On FLUX.1-dev, semantic-first improves prompt faithfulness, independently evaluated affect accuracy, and human-rated se-mantic preservation and overall preference. The expanded semantic audit records 2.7% subject drift, while GRPO training time decreases by 41.1% relative to original full-path Flow-GRPO. Window-width experiments charac-terize the affect–semantic and computational trade-offs, and replication on SD3.5-Medium reproduces the scheduling advantage. These results establish denoising time as a practical control dimension for reliable emotional image generation. 

Keywords: emotional image generation, flow matching, GRPO, temporal scheduling, semantic preservation

1. Introduction 

Emotion-aware image generation is useful in advertising, interface design, educational media, entertainment, and human–computer interaction, where the same semantic scene may need to convey different affective states. Apractical system should therefore answer two questions simultaneously: what 

should appear, and how should it feel. The continuous valence–arousal (V– A) model provides a compact control space for the second question [1, 2]. EmotiCrafter further demonstrates that an emotion injection transformer (EIT) can condition text-to-image generation on continuous V–A targets [3]. Nevertheless, stronger affective modulation may change a prompt’s main object, layout, or relations. In engineering deployment, a cheerful tree becoming fireworks is not a harmless stylistic variation; it is a specification failure. Online reinforcement learning (RL) offers a direct way to optimize non-differentiable affect and semantic rewards. Flow-GRPO formulates stochastic sampling in flow-matching models as a Markov decision process and optimizes the full trajectory [4]. Its full-path SDE rollout creates exploration but is computationally expensive. MixGRPO reduces this overhead by using SDE sampling and GRPO optimization only inside a short window while using deterministic ODE sampling elsewhere [5]. In the original formulation, the window is mainly an efficiency and curriculum mechanism. Yet denoising time is not attribute-neutral: high-noise steps establish global scene structure, whereas low-noise steps increasingly affect local appearance and detail [5, 6]. This observation motivates our central question: when should stochastic exploration be introduced if semantics and affect must share one probability-flow trajectory? We compare two temporal orders under the same backbone, rewards, number of sampling steps, and SDE-window size. Emotion-first 

exposes the high-noise phase to SDE exploration and later returns to an ODE trajectory. Semantic-first keeps the high-noise phase deterministic and introduces stochastic exploration only after the scene structure has formed. In our implementation, step 0 is the highest-noise step, so semantic-first corresponds to a late SDE window [20 , 24) , not an early one. On FLUX, full-path Flow-GRPO achieves the lowest V–A error under the training regressors, while semantic-first improves prompt alignment, cross-affect consistency, and independently evaluated affect accuracy. The proposed late-window schedule improves affect control over a non-RL baseline, improves the reported main metrics over progressive MixGRPO, and cuts end-to-end 2training cost. Controlled experiments separate where stochastic exploration occurs from where policy updates are applied, and a second flow-matching backbone tests the transfer of the scheduling principle. Our contribution is an empirical study of temporal allocation: where stochastic exploration and policy optimization act along denoising time to improve affect control while preserving prompt semantics. We reuse the GRPO objective and test the scheduling effects through separate sampling-window and optimization-window interventions. Our contributions are: • We study the temporal placement of stochastic exploration and policy optimization as control variables for the affect–semantic trade-off. • We instantiate a semantic-first schedule: ODE sampling at high noise followed by a fixed four-step SDE window at low noise, reusing the V–A EIT and MixGRPO objective. 3• We evaluate the scheduling principle with separate sampling and optimiza-tion controls, four window widths, two flow-matching backbones, indepen-dent affect evaluation, TIFA, semantic auditing, efficiency measurements, and a 30-participant user study. • We establish a practical operating point that combines strong affect control, prompt-content preservation, and lower training cost, supported by paired comparisons across automatic and human evaluations. 

2. Related work 

2.1. Continuous emotional image generation 

Visual affect has been modeled with discrete categories and continuous dimensions, with valence and arousal providing a widely used two-dimensional representation [1, 2]. Earlier generation and editing approaches used emo-tional color transfer, semantic emotion concepts, or diffusion-based image manipulation [7, 8, 9]. EmotiCrafter learns a continuous emotion embedding and injects it into prompt features, allowing one prompt to be rendered at different V–A coordinates [3]. Anchor-regularized GRPO combines contin-uous V–A conditioning with a neutral-image semantic reference to balance affective alignment and content preservation [10]. We study how the temporal allocation of exploration and policy updates controls the balance between affect alignment and prompt-content preservation. 

2.2. Flow matching and mixed ODE–SDE sampling 

Diffusion and flow models admit deterministic probability-flow ODEs and related stochastic sampling processes [11, 12, 13]. Rectified flow provides an efficient generative formulation [14], and transformer-based flow models such as SD3 [6] and FLUX [15] support high-resolution text-to-image synthesis. Deterministic solvers accelerate generation [16, 17], whereas stochastic transi-tions provide the action distribution needed by online policy optimization. MixGRPO combines these modes by placing SDE transitions inside a slid-ing window and ODE transitions outside it [5]. Its progressive schedule moves from low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) to high SNR. Recent work further shows that flow-model policy optimization is temporally non-uniform. TempFlow-GRPO concentrates branching at selected timesteps and applies noise-aware optimization weights [18]; Stepwise-Flow-GRPO assigns credit from esti-mated reward improvements at individual transitions [19]; and BranchGRPO 4reuses shared trajectory prefixes to improve rollout stability and efficiency [20]. Neighbor GRPO instead avoids SDE conversion by constructing a con-trastive policy objective around neighboring deterministic ODE trajectories [21]. These methods target temporal credit assignment, rollout reuse, or alternative policy construction. In contrast, we keep the reward, backbone, and four-transition budget fixed and treat the location of stochasticity as an attribute-control variable. We examine how the temporal placement of explo-ration and policy optimization changes affect accuracy and prompt-content preservation. The relation between noise level and structural preservation also moti-vates SDEdit, which adds noise to an image guide and denoises it to balance input faithfulness and realism [22]. In video generation, FreeNoise resched-ules initial noise across frames and uses windowed temporal attention to maintain long-range content consistency [23]. These studies provide context for time-dependent control: SDEdit selects an editing noise level, FreeNoise manages video-frame coherence, and our method allocates exploration and reward optimization across denoising transitions in affect-conditioned image generation. 

2.3. Reinforcement learning for image generation 

Reward-weighted fine-tuning and policy-gradient methods have aligned diffusion models with human preferences and task-specific objectives [24, 25, 26]. GRPO normalizes rewards within groups and avoids a learned value network [27]. Flow-GRPO and DanceGRPO adapt this idea to flow-based visual generation [4, 28]. Because reward optimization may exploit imperfections in evaluators, multi-objective evaluation and semantic auditing remain essential. Our experiments use separate affect and semantic rewards, report both outcome families, and include human evaluation rather than collapsing them into a single score. Application-oriented RL spans control, recommendation, and energy-management tasks, as reviewed by Sivamayil et al. [29]. Beyond image synthesis, PACE-MoE coordinates adaptive domain sampling and domain-specific LoRA for medical visual grounding, retaining the GRPO objective while changing the allocation of data and parameter updates [30]. Our choice of GRPO follows the structure of image generation: terminal image rewards rank a group of stochastic trajectories, and within-group normalization sup-plies a policy-gradient signal without a learned value network. Generation 5design also depends on the target output. SignCraft combines speech-to-sign-gloss processing with gesture synthesis for consumer devices [31]; our task instead preserves text-specified scene content while varying continuous image affect. These comparisons situate the policy choice and generation objective in their respective application settings.

4. Experimental protocol 

4.1. Research questions 

The experiments address five questions: (RQ1) how the positions of sampling and optimization windows change the affect–semantic trade-off; (RQ2) whether semantic-first improves upon progressive scheduling and a non-RL affect-conditioned base; (RQ3) how it compares with full-path training in quality and cost; (RQ4) whether the conclusion persists at extreme affect targets and under human judgment; and (RQ5) how it transfers across window widths and a second backbone. 

4.2. Models, data, and training 

The primary backbone is FLUX.1-dev [15]; SD3.5-Medium provides the second-backbone evaluation [34]. VA-EIT training uses the released EmotiCrafter prompt-pair corpus, which supplies neutral prompts, emotional rewrites, and their V–A coordinates [3]. We randomly split paired rows into approximately 98% training and 2% validation with seed 42 to train and select the adapter in Eq. (2). The same corpus supplies neutral prompts and V–A targets for GRPO. After adapter selection, we cache emotion-conditioned 9embeddings for all paired rows and use the complete cache across temporal schedules. GRPO uses 25 sampling steps, 720 ×720 output resolution, SDE noise scale 

η = 0 .7, timestep shift 3, 12 generations per prompt, and equal affect/semantic reward weights. The generation transformer is optimized with AdamW at learning rate 10 −5, while the text encoders, VAE, and EIT remain frozen. Runs use eight NVIDIA A100 GPUs and training seeds 42, 123, and 3407. Each formal run comprises 10 additional warm-up iterations followed by 300 formal outer iterations. The separate 200-update pilot uses the same three training seeds. The progressive schedule advances its four-step window by one index every 14 iterations. The 132 evaluation prompts do not overlap with the prompts used for VA-EIT or GRPO training. Evaluation crosses these neutral prompts with the 25-point grid V, A ∈ {− 3, −1.5, 0, 1.5, 3}, yielding 3,300 images per checkpoint. For each prompt, initial noise is shared across V–A targets and methods to control composition variation. The baselines are: (1) the reproduced original EmotiCrafter configuration; (2) FLUX+VA-EIT without RL, which isolates the stronger backbone and adapter; (3) full-path Flow-GRPO; and (4) progressive MixGRPO. The pro-posed method uses the fixed late window W (20) with the joint affect/semantic objective. All trained-checkpoint comparisons use full-trajectory ODE evalu-ation with trained weights at every step. 

4.3. Metrics 

V-Error and A-Error are mean absolute deviations between target values and predictions from the frozen CLIP-based affect regressors used in the reward pipeline. Independent affect evaluation uses frozen Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct [35], which receives the image and estimates V/A coordinates without the target, prompt, or method identity. Qwen V-MAE and A-MAE compare these image-only estimates with the target coordinates. CLIPScore is text– image cosine similarity reported after multiplication by 100 [33]. TIFA uses visual question answering to measure prompt faithfulness and is reported on a 0–100 scale [36]. For the formal comparison, a fixed question set is generated from each original prompt, filtered with UnifiedQA, and answered from each generated image by mPLUG-large; the same questions and answer keys are shared across methods, V–A targets, and repeats. Scores average question correctness within each image, then the 25 target variants and 132 prompts. CLIP-IQA measures no-reference perceptual quality on a 0–1 scale 10 [37]. DINO consistency is the mean pairwise cosine similarity, multiplied by 100, between DINOv2 embeddings of the 25 V–A variants generated for the same prompt [38]; it measures whether content remains stable as affect changes. For a target-specific score, we average the similarity of that target image to the other 24 variants of the same prompt, then average over prompts. Table D.2 reports these scores at five selected targets, with means and sample SD across seeds; the main comparison averages over all 25 targets. Semantic drift is the percentage of images with a replaced or absent principal subject. The expanded audit assesses prompt-specified identity, count, action, and spatial relations on shared applicable sets of 150, 130, 120, and 105 images per method, respectively. Both this audit and the five-target retention experiment in Section 5.6 use two independent annotators and a third adjudicator to determine final binary correctness labels. The audit uses the same seed-42 images as the user study in Section 4.4. For each requirement, Cohen’s κ is computed by pooling the two annotators’ independent, pre-adjudication labels across all six methods. 

4.4. Reporting protocol and user study 

Repeated RL results report means and sample SD over three training seeds; fixed-model sampling experiments use three sampling seeds. Automatic paired confidence intervals use 132 prompts as resampling units, retaining all 25 V–A variants and the fixed seed results within each prompt. We use 10,000 paired bootstrap resamples and percentile 95% intervals. These intervals describe prompt-sampling uncertainty conditional on the evaluated runs. For the single EmotiCrafter checkpoint, the comparator remains fixed while the proposed method is averaged over its repeated runs. The expanded user study includes 30 participants and a stratified random sample of 30 prompts from the 132-prompt test set, using four semantic strata: people, animals/natural scenes, man-made objects, and abstract/atmospheric content. Each prompt is evaluated at the four V–A corners and the neutral point, yielding 150 matched groups and 900 distinct seed-42 images across six methods. Each participant is randomly assigned 15 groups (90 images), and each group is evaluated in full by three different participants. The prompt and target are visible, method identities are hidden, and group order and within-group image order are randomized. The user study was exempt from ethics review, and all participants provided informed consent. Participants independently rate affect match, semantic preservation, and overall preference on a 1–5 scale. 11 For each participant, method, and rating dimension, we average scores over the 15 assigned groups. We report the mean and sample SD of these participant-level method means across 30 participants. For each baseline and rating dimension, we calculate 30 within-participant differences, defined as semantic-first minus the baseline, and construct two-sided 95% paired-t

confidence intervals using 29 degrees of freedom.

22 6. Engineering discussion 

The controls identify denoising time as an attribute-control resource under the shared joint reward in Eq. (7). With full-path exploration fixed, late opti-mization improves semantic consistency; restricting exploration to the same late window adds a further gain. Moving stochasticity out of the high-noise phase also preserves content before policy updates. Temporal allocation thus protects prompt faithfulness, subject retention, and cross-affect consistency through both exploration and learning. This agrees with MixGRPO’s time-dependent optimization signals [5] and extends the scheduling result from FLUX to SD3.5-Medium. Affect rankings depend on the evaluator. The reward regressors favor full-path training, whereas independent image-only Qwen evaluation favors semantic-first. A plausible explanation is the difference in optimization exposure: the regressors also supply the training reward, which full-path training optimizes across more transitions, while Qwen provides a separate affect estimate without participating in training. This can favor full-path performance under the optimized evaluator without producing the same ordering under Qwen. Human affect-match ratings are 4.22 for full-path Flow-GRPO and 4.13 for semantic-first; the paired difference has a 95% interval of [−0.31 , 0.13] , so this study does not establish a difference in affect match between them. Semantic-first improves semantic preservation and overall preference in the human study, with positive paired intervals against every comparator. Together with TIFA and the semantic audit, these results show a consistent content-preservation advantage and independently evaluated affect gains. The width study makes the operating choice explicit. A two-step late window prioritizes semantic consistency and low cost; a six-step window strengthens affect control with a larger semantic and computational cost. The four-step default balances these objectives. Equation (1) provides the schedule-selection criterion for applications with different content-preservation and computational requirements. 

7. Limitations 

The study evaluates FLUX.1-dev and SD3.5-Medium with continuous valence–arousal conditioning and 25-step sampling. The default window 20–23 is tied to this sampling budget and noise schedule; changing either requires 23 reassessing window placement. Window comparisons measure scheduling effects under the inherited time-dependent SDE noise schedule, including the noise magnitude associated with each window. Widths from two to eight transitions characterize the tested operating range. The experiments establish scheduling effects on the affect–semantic trade-off, but do not establish an independent factorization of semantic and affective variables along denoising time. Future work can calibrate both affect evaluators against human V–A annotations to test the source of their ranking differences. 

8. Conclusion 

We studied the temporal allocation of semantic formation and affective exploration in flow-matching image generation. During RL training, the semantic-first schedule uses ODE sampling at high noise followed by SDE exploration and GRPO optimization at low noise. Separate sampling and optimization controls establish the contribution of each temporal choice, and width tests characterize its affect–semantic and computational trade-offs. On FLUX, semantic-first achieves the highest main-comparison TIFA score, improves independent Qwen affect evaluation, and obtains the highest semantic-preservation and overall-preference ratings. Its expanded audit records 2.7% subject drift, while GRPO training time is reduced by 41.1% relative to original full-path Flow-GRPO. Replication on SD3.5-Medium confirms the scheduling advantage on a second backbone. Probability-flow time is therefore a practical control dimension for reliable emotional image generation.

Appendix C. Reproducibility details 

VA-EIT uses the architecture in Section 3 with a feed-forward expansion of four. Adapter training uses AdamW, learning rate 10 −4, weight decay 

10 −5, 10 epochs, batch size one, and gradient accumulation of eight; the lowest validation-loss checkpoint is selected. During GRPO, the EIT, text encoders, and VAE remain frozen. The generation transformer uses AdamW at learning rate 10 −5, weight decay 0.01, and gradient clipping norm 2. The GRPO clipping range is 10 −4, the KL coefficient is zero, and affect/semantic advantages have equal weights. The independent evaluator is Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, using its official processor, BF16 inference, deterministic decoding and at most 64 new tokens. It rates the visible image’s valence and arousal on [−3, 3] , with zero denoting neutral valence or moderate activation. TIFA question generation uses the released TIFA LLaMA-2 question generator; UnifiedQA-v2-T5-large filters the questions, and mPLUG-large answers them from each image. One frozen question set per prompt is shared across all methods, targets and repeats. For SD3.5-Medium, T5 features from the third text-encoder branch pass through a backbone-specific pretrained EIT; the native CLIP token and 28 pooled conditioning branches are retained. All configurations share this EIT and a sigma schedule with shift 3. M1–M3 use the same trainable transformer parameters and budget under the settings in Section 5.8.
