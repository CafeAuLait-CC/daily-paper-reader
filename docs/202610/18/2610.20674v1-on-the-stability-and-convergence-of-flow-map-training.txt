## ABSTRACT 

Flow maps are a recent method of probabilistic generation that amortize diffusion trajectories into one-step samplers, unlocking efficient generation. While progress has been made in improving their empirical performance, there is still a lack of understanding behind the mechanisms underlying their success. Specifically, many flow map learning methods employ stop gradient operators as a crucial part of their training target. Adaptive weighting also drastically improves the generation capabilities of flow maps. Although many interpretations have been proposed to support the inclusion of these heuristics, none have analyzed the impact of these assumptions on the training dynamics of flow maps themselves. In this work, we address this issue by providing grounded theory explaining why such tricks are required. Analyzing the functional gradient flow induced by the MeanFlow and Lagrangian losses, we show their convergence to the true flow map, settling the validity of these objectives. For finite updates, we highlight a severe roadblock to the usage of these losses in practice: the possible unboundedness of the update. Adaptive normalization not only provides bounded updates, but, combined with a specific weighting, additionally provides convergence guarantees towards a neighborhood of the solution. On synthetic data, we show that our functional gradient flow analysis exactly describes the true training dynamics for flow maps. Experiments on standard image datasets demonstrate that a proper reweighting scheme is essential for strong empirical performance, which is only achieved for a specific range of reweighting strengths. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Modern deep generative models, such as diffusion (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021) and flow matching models (Lipman et al., 2023; Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden, 2023; Liu et al., 2023), can now generate realistic data across a wide range of modalities. Diffusion and flow matching methods are able to generate samples that are indistinguishable from human-generated content, including images (Stability AI, 2023; Midjourney, 2023; Black Forest Labs et al., 2025), audio (Borsos et al., 2023), and video (Villegas et al., 2023; Brooks et al., 2024). Diffusion and flow matching have also recently shown great performance on text, either with discrete or continuous approaches (Savinov et al., 2022; Gong et al., 2023; Sahoo et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2026; DiffusionGemma Team et al., 2026). Once trained, sampling for flow matching models is typically done by numerically solving an ordinary differential equation, i.e. , integrating the learned velocity over hundreds of steps, hence requiring hundreds of neural network evaluations. Current promising one-step generative modeling approaches rely on distilling the inference trajectories and include techniques such as consistency models (Song et al., 2023), which learn to directly predict the trajectory endpoint of the flow matching ordinary differential equation from any point along the trajectory. In the same spirit, flow maps (Geng et al., 2025; 2026; Boffi et al., 2025a; Lu et al., 2026) learn the solution of the ordinary differential equation between any two times. A variety of approaches for training flow maps have been proposed (Geng et al., 2025; 2026; Boffi et al., 2025a; Zhou et al., 2026; Goldstein et al., 2026; Frans et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026), due to the abundance of possible training objectives. To stabilize training, several tricks are employed, including stop gradient operators, self-distillation from specialized velocity heads, loss reweighting, gradient normalization, and sampling time pairs from hand-picked distributions (Geng et al., 2025; 2026; Sabour et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2026). There is little understanding behind why certain losses work in practice , and whether the tricks used for training are theoretically sound. In particular, the inclusion of stop gradient operators has been found to substantially improve flow map training (Geng et al., 2025); however, it alters gradient directions and has yet to be shown to recover the true flow map. 

Contributions . In this paper, we aim to understand the impact of the stop gradient operator and of adaptive normalization on the learning dynamics of flow maps, and to show how they provide tangible benefits to the convergence and stability of flow map training. We show the following: 1. Functional convergence of flow maps . We show that, in a specific functional setting, the gradient flow of the MeanFlow and Lagrangian objectives converges to the desired flow map (Theorem 1). Importantly, the convergence results rely on the careful stop gradient placement in the MeanFlow and Lagrangian losses. 2. Adaptive normalization . We show that a fundamental limitation of gradient descent is the unboundedness of the update (Proposition 1), which may amplify arbitrarily small errors. Empirical validation . Comments on Equations (5) and (7) . Such stop gradient-based losses can be motivated in several ways. Computationally, since all partial derivatives of the network are contained within the stop gradient, this removes the need for double backpropagation. Theoretically, a minimizer of L

is necessarily a flow map, since L ≥ 0 with equality if and only if the Eulerian or Lagrangian identity holds. However, although a zero training loss implies being a flow map, the stop gradient operator might prevent optimization from necessarily decreasing the loss L: in practice, the MeanFlow loss has been shown not to converge to zero over iterations (Geng et al., 2026). Furthermore, Goldstein et al. (2026) theoretically showed that the model update induced by the MeanFlow loss cannot be the gradient of any scalar loss. In conjunction with the concurrent work of Shen et al. ## 3 THEORETICAL ANALYSIS OF FUNCTIONAL FLOW MAPS 

We now present our main theoretical results and rigorously analyze the training dynamics of the MeanFlow and the Lagrangian methods. The main tools required to perform functional gradient descent are introduced in Section 3.1. Section 3.2 shows our main convergence results: the MeanFlow and Lagrangian gradient flow training dynamics converge towards the true flow map. This uncovers the non-obvious result: regressing against the stop gradient target not only yields practical benefits, but provably recovers the true flow map . In Section 3.3, we analyze how, under gradient descent ,MeanFlow and Lagrangian training can fail to recover the true flow map due to the divergence of iterates, and how a specific range of adaptive normalization strengths prevents such a failure. 3.1 SETTING

The closed form in Theorem 1 also provides insights into how training under the MeanFlow loss structurally modifies the learned map: as training progresses, the MeanFlow map learns to transport samples using the true flow to increasingly later times tτ , and the initialization Φ0 estimates the remaining jump to the time t. ## 4 RELATED WORK 

Trajectory distillation . One of the most relevant related works is consistency models (Song et al., 2023; Song and Dhariwal, 2024), which build a function f (xs, s ) mapping directly to the trajectory endpoint x1. Continuous-time consistency models are trained with a similar loss to the MeanFlow loss (Lu and Song, 2025; Sabour et al., 2025), except with a fixed t = 1 , and hence are amenable to the same analysis as we have presented. In practice consistency models also require similar tricks such as tangent normalization for effective training (Lu and Song, 2025). 

Flow maps . Many works have proposed learning schemes for flow maps. Boffi et al. (2025b) derive fundamental equations for flow maps and introduce the LSD and ESD objectives. Geng et al. (2025) introduce the MeanFlow objective, which is further improved in Geng et al. (2026) by replacing a stochastic estimate for v with a self-distilled diagonal term. Lu et al. (2026) reparametrize the objective as u = (ˆ x(x, s, t ) − x)/(1 − s). ˆx can then be shown to lie in the convex hull of p1

(Lee et al., 2026). Align Your Flow (Sabour et al., 2025) introduces flow map distillation methods derived from consistency estimates. These are equivalent to the methods presented in this paper, up to reweighting. Terminal velocity matching (Zhou et al., 2026) uses a modified LSD loss, where the stop gradient teacher velocity u is replaced with an EMA version. In Goldstein et al. (2026), the authors argue for an alternative loss to MeanFlow, due to the presence of spurious minimizers. Shen et al. (2026) encompasses a larger class of problems such as reinforcement learning, but their convergence results are only valid for ρ = 1 . Here we assume the existence of a density ρ which alters the gradient dynamics, and show convergence in this broader setting. We also introduce novel L2 convergence rates, and our results on gradient descent are independent of prior work.

## 6 CONCLUSION 

In this paper, we have analyzed the impact of stop gradient operators and adaptive normalization on flow map training. We have shown how the placement of stop gradients in the MeanFlow and Lagrangian losses leads to a gradient update that, when optimized with gradient flow , recovers the true flow map pointwise, in L∞, and in L2 for MeanFlow. In addition to preventing double backpropagation, stop gradient operators provably move learned flow maps closer to the ground truth. 9Preprint. Work in progress. However, gradient descent iterations, when applied on the MeanFlow and Lagrangian losses, can yield divergence. We demonstrate how adaptive normalization gives us both a theoretical and practical solution, enabling higher fidelity flow map learning on CIFAR-10 and FFHQ 64 ×64 datasets. 

Limitations and future directions . The scope of the results derived in this paper is limited to the losses discussed in Equations (5) and (7). In particular, we do not show convergence for the original ESD and LSD objectives or the Terminal Velocity Matching objective (Boffi et al., 2025b; Zhou et al., 2026). Showing convergence for the gradient flow induced by any of these losses is future work. In addition, our convergence results are derived in a specific functional setting limiting the applicability of the derived results to neural networks, whose parametrization impacts the functional gradient. Extending these results to such parametrized functions would require analyzing the PDEs under preconditioned gradients. The main theory of this work is for the idealized gradient flow setting, where stochasticity-based challenges are ignored, as well as other tricks such as training with a fixed percentage of samples taken at the diagonal s = t. Quantifying the effects of these design choices is left for subsequent studies. ACKNOWLEDGMENTS

Our goal is to minimize the objective in Equation (8) over the infinite-dimensional function space

The gradient of L at u then points in the direction that increases L the fastest under an infinitesimal change to the input. Mathematically, this is written as DL(u)[ h] = ⟨∇L (u), h ⟩.Importantly, the gradient depends on the choice of inner product on the function space U. To formally define the gradient, we fix a choice of inner product, namely the L2(Rd × ∆) inner product. This choice of metric is natural and can be motivated by homogeneity. More precisely, a change in u

is felt equally irrespective of its position in space and time. Furthermore, it also does not result in spatial mixing, which is a central ingredient for the success of our analysis. It should be noted that neural network parametrizations do not naturally have this geometry. Under this inner product, we see that ∇L (u) = ρ(u − utgt ).
