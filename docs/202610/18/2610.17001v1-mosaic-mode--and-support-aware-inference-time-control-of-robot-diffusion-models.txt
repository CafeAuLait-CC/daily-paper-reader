# MOSAIC : Mode- and Support-Aware Inference-Time Control of Robot Diffusion Models 

Sabrina Bodmer 1, Rahel Rickenbach 1, Ren´ e Zurbr¨ ugg 1, Melanie N. Zeilinger †1, Colin Jones †2

Abstract — Generative policies such as diffusion models pro-vide expressive data-driven priors for robot behavior, yet the situations a robot faces at execution time rarely match its training data and impose constraints and objectives the policy never learned. Existing inference-time guidance methods either rely on gradients that can push samples away from the learned prior, or on sampling procedures that can be inefficient, collapse modes, or average them into out-of-distribution predictions. We introduce M OSAIC , a mode- and support-aware frame-work for gradient-free inference-time control of robotic diffusion models. M OSAIC treats the initial noise and all stochastic reverse-step perturbations as a joint input to the pretrained denoising process and adapts a distribution over this input using impor-tance weights computed from fully denoised outputs. Physical feasibility and consistency with the learned support are balanced using adaptive penalties, while mode-wise importance-weighted updates preserve modes and avoid destructive averaging. The same formulation can jointly steer multiple independently trained diffusion models through shared coupling costs, enabling coordinated behavior without requiring a joint model. We evaluate M OSAIC on navigation, dexterous grasping, motion planning with unseen geometry, and collaborative box manip-ulation with independently trained robot priors. Across these settings, M OSAIC adapts and composes frozen diffusion priors under new deployment constraints without retraining. Videos and additional results available at: mosaic-guide.github.io 

I. INTRODUCTION Diffusion models have emerged as powerful generative priors for robotic decision-making, capturing complex and multimodal distributions over grasps, trajectories, and action sequences from offline data. Their ability to represent multiple valid behaviors is particularly valuable in robotics, where a task may admit different configurations, homotopy classes, or coordinated behaviors. However, deployment inevitably introduces requirements that were absent during training, such as new obstacles, embodiment-specific constraints, or inter-actions with independently trained agents. Reusing a frozen diffusion prior therefore requires adapting its predictions to new deployment conditions without discarding the behavioral structure learned from data. The inference-time adaptation problem has several coupled difficulties. First, many relevant objectives, such as collision checking, inverse kinematics, and grasp stability, are non-differentiable, expensive to evaluate, or only meaningful

Second, an output may satisfy a physical objective while leaving the support of the learned prior, which can produce physically admissible yet implausible or semantically invalid behavior. Third, the learned prior itself may be multimodal, and global sampling updates can average incompatible solution families into low-density or infeasible predictions. For example, averaging paths that pass on opposite sides of an obstacle can produce a path through the obstacle. Addressing feasibility alone is therefore insufficient; the inference procedure must also preserve support and multimodality. Existing guidance methods address parts of this problem. However, gradient-based approaches require differentiable objectives and can push samples away from the support of the learned distribution. And while sampling-based methods avoid this requirement and admit arbitrary black-box costs, sequential particle methods can suffer from weight degeneracy and loss of particle diversity under repeated reweighting and resampling [1]. In diffusion steering, this difficulty is compounded when candidates are ranked on estimates of the eventual clean output at highly noisy intermediate states; recent work has observed that such value estimates can be inaccurate early in the reverse process [2]. Delaying guidance to later denoising steps makes terminal estimates more reliable, but leaves fewer reverse transitions through which earlier decisions can be revised. We introduce M OSAIC , a mode- and support-aware frame-work for gradient-free inference-time control of frozen robot diffusion models. M OSAIC treats the complete reverse process as a mapping from its stochastic inputs to a clean prediction. We expose the initial noise and all reverse-step perturbations as a joint control input and iteratively adapt a distribution over these inputs using model predictive path integral (MPPI)-style importance-weighted updates. The approach evaluates arXiv:2610.17001v1 [cs.RO] 12 Oct 2026 complete behaviors rather than intermediate denoising states, allowing deployment constraints to be assessed where they are most meaningful. To preserve the structure learned by the diffusion prior, M OSAIC balances physical feasibility with consistency to the learned support and maintains separate proposals for distinct solution modes, avoiding destructive averaging across incompatible behaviors. The same formu-lation also allows independently trained diffusion priors to be coordinated through shared inference-time costs, enabling multi-agent composition without training a joint model. In summary, our contributions are: 1) Complete reverse-process control. We expose the initial diffusion sample and all reverse-step perturbations as a joint control input, enabling arbitrary deployment objectives to be evaluated on fully denoised rollouts. 2) Mode- and support-aware proposal adaptation. We approximate the resulting constrained distribution using mode-specific importance-weighted proposals and adapt physical and support constraint weights online. 3) Inference-time composition of independent priors. We show that the same framework can coordinate inde-pendently trained diffusion models through shared coupling objectives, enabling multi-agent behavior without retraining a joint model. II. R ELATED WORK 

Gradient-based diffusion guidance. Gradient-based meth-ods steer the reverse process using derivatives of a reward, constraint, or auxiliary objective with respect to the diffusion state [3], [4], [5], [6]. For example, CoDiG incorporates barrier-function gradients for collision avoidance [4], while UMI-on-Air uses gradients of an embodiment-specific track-ing objective to bias manipulation trajectories toward dynam-ically executable solutions [5]. Related optimization-based approaches enforce deployment-time constraints by optimiz-ing reverse-process variables or solving constrained problems around denoised predictions [7], [8]. These approaches often require differentiable objectives, surrogates, or optimization during denoising, and may move samples away from regions supported by the learned diffusion prior. 

Sampling-based diffusion guidance. Sampling-based meth-ods avoid differentiating through the deployment objective by evaluating and comparing candidate predictions directly, making them applicable to non-differentiable constraints and objectives. One class maintains a population of candidates and progressively concentrates computation on promising samples through selection, mutation, reweighting, or resampling [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]. Feynman–Kac steering provides a sequential formulation of this idea, where particles are weighted and resampled throughout the reverse process using intermediate potentials, often constructed from denoised estimates of the eventual clean prediction [11], [12]. Such sequential updates can work well when intermediate estimates are reliable, but repeated reweighting and resampling can quickly concentrate the population on only a few candidates. If promising alternatives receive low weight early in denoising, they may disappear before their final behavior can be evaluated, leading to particle starvation and mode collapse. A second class uses evaluated samples to iteratively adapt the distribution from which subsequent candidates are drawn [14], [15], [16], [17]. Such proposal adaptation is closely related to MPPI, cross-entropy optimization, and adaptive importance sampling [18], [19], [20], which update parametric sampling distributions from weighted candidate populations. GRACE, for example, uses MPPI-style impor-tance weighting to update Gaussian proposals during the reverse process [16], while ZeNO applies a path-integral update to the source noise of a deterministic diffusion sampler [14]. By concentrating the proposal around promising regions, these methods can progressively improve candidate solutions without maintaining particle lineages throughout the reverse process. However, when a single proposal is updated from samples belonging to multiple distinct solution modes, its global statistics can combine incompatible behaviors [21]. Such averaging can move subsequent samples toward low-density or infeasible regions and lead to loss of multimodality. In contrast, M OSAIC treats the source noise and all stochastic reverse-step perturbations jointly as the proposal variable, evaluates them only after complete denoising, and maintains separate proposals for distinct output modes. 

Composition of diffusion models. Coordinating multiple diffusion models typically requires either learning their inter-action during training or combining independently generated predictions at inference time [22], [23]. DAGDiff learns a joint diffusion model over dual-arm grasp pairs and uses classifier guidance to steer the coupled prediction [22]. UniDiffGrasp instead generates candidates for the two arms independently and subsequently selects compatible grasp pairs [23]. Joint models require task-specific training, while post-hoc selection can require many samples when compatible pairs are rare. More generally, Feynman–Kac Correctors show how pretrained diffusion models can be combined through product-distribution constructions at inference time [12]. MOSAIC instead introduces coordination through an arbitrary terminal coupling cost, which need not correspond to a separately trained score or differentiable energy model. III. B ACKGROUND

MOSAIC approaches this problem by adapting the stochas-tic inputs of the complete reverse process. We first collect the initial noise and all reverse-step perturbations into a joint reverse input u, making the denoising process deterministic. We then formulate a desired deployment-time distribution over this input, balancing task performance, physical and support constraints, and deviation from the pretrained prior (Sec. V-B). Since this target cannot in general be sampled from directly, we approximate it using importance-weighted proposals. To avoid averaging incompatible solution families, the proposal is maintained separately for modes identified from the fully denoised outputs (Sec. V-C). Finally, the same construction extends to several independent diffusion priors by evaluating their complete rollouts under a shared coupling cost (Sec. V-D). VI. E XPERIMENTS 

We evaluate four capabilities of M OSAIC : preserving multimodal solutions, adapting to unseen geometry, generating executable grasps, and composing independent priors. 

Tasks. In 1) 2D navigation, inference-time obstacles constrain a multimodal trajectory prior with multiple homotopy classes using the model and code from [29]. 2) Dexterous grasping adapts a frozen Franka–XHand grasp prior from [30] using kinematic and collision constraints. 3) Trajectory planning and 4) collaborative box manipulation use the diffusion model from [31]; the former introduces unseen collision geometry and tool attachment, while the latter couples two independently trained arm priors on the real dual-arm system. All pretrained generative models remain frozen. 

Baselines and protocol. We compare against unguided DDIM [28], gradient guidance, Path-Space MPPI, FK steer-ing [11], and NDPP [17]. Path-Space MPPI uses a single Gaussian proposal over the complete reverse input; FK steer-ing sequentially reweights and resamples using intermediate clean-output estimates; and NDPP updates the current reverse correction from finite-horizon rollouts evaluated through Tweedie-predicted clean outputs. Within each task, all methods use the same frozen model, number of DDIM steps, and initial sample count. Since the methods expose different inference-time choices, we tune these separately and report the results of the best-performing configuration for each task, including the NDPP horizon, FK resampling schedule, mean versus best-sample selection for MPPI and M OSAIC , and optional online re-clustering for MOSAIC . Task-level DDIM horizons and initial populations are selected during preliminary tuning and then held fixed across methods. Success is task-specific: in 2D navigation, a trajectory is successful only if none of its 48 waypoints collides with an obstacle. Grasping success requires arm IK feasibility, no hand penetration of the table plane, and stability under simulated forces. Trajectory-planning considers solutions successful if the trajectory is collision-free and the final end-effector position error is below 3 cm. Box manipulation requires final box position and orientation errors below 2 cm and 

5◦, respectively, with the distance between the two hands varying by at most 3 cm throughout the motion. We also report success weighted by path length (SPL), using the straight-line start–goal distance as the reference length; failed trials contribute zero. Each method is evaluated on identical task instances. Navigation uses 8 mazes with 16 trajectories each over 3 seeds ( 384 trajectories total); trajectory planning and box manipulation use 50 trials per seed over 3 seeds ( 150 

trials per condition); and grasping uses a total of 280 grasps over 7 objects. We report standard deviation across seeds for navigation, trajectory planning, and box manipulation, and binomial standard error for grasping. 

Implementation details. For navigation, grasping, trajectory planning, and box manipulation, we use (8 , 4, 10 , 10) DDIM steps and (48 , 48 , 64 , 64) initial samples, respectively. The larger horizons and populations are used for the more involved planning and multi-arm tasks. Support models are fitted offline, with budgets calibrated from the unguided population at ℓ = 0 . Navigation modes use a path signature, while trajectory and box modes use subsampled end-effector path signatures; mode centers may remain fixed or be re-clustered online depending on the selected configuration.

Hardware deployment. We deploy the bimanual com-position task on a dual-arm Franka setup equipped with BrainCo hands (Fig. 1), using one independently trained single-arm diffusion model per arm. The hand configurations are fixed rather than generated: we use a pinch grasp for a small pan and flat-hand or regular grasps for a larger box. These setups induce different required wrist and arm configurations during transport. In each case, the object is lifted and moved to a new target while the two arms maintain feasible relative distances and orientations throughout the motion. Representative hardware rollouts are shown in Fig. 5. 

Effect of rollout-level evaluation. A clear trend emerges across the manipulation tasks: the advantage of M OSAIC 

is small for grasping, increases for trajectory planning, and is largest for collaborative manipulation. In the latter tasks, success depends increasingly on coupled properties of the complete behavior, such as swept-volume collisions, whole-trajectory feasibility, or coordination between two arms. Intermediate clean-output or Tweedie estimates used by sequential methods such as FK steering and NDPP may become less informative proxies for these rollout-level criteria, whereas M OSAIC evaluates them directly on complete rollouts. This may explain part of the observed trend; the remaining gap to Path-Space MPPI, which also uses complete rollouts, suggests benefit from mode-wise proposal adaptation, consistent with the mode-starvation observed in navigation. The two arms use independently trained frozen diffusion priors coupled only at inference time through Eq. (28). VII. L IMITATIONS 

While M OSAIC performs well across the evaluated tasks, some limitations remain. Evaluating complete rollouts in-troduces an inference-time overhead of approximately 2 ×

relative to comparable sampling-based baselines, which may become significant in high-rate closed-loop settings. Calibrating the support model requires prior training data, or alternatively unguided model samples at the cost of a less faithful density estimate. The task-specific mode embedding and fixed number of modes motivate learned embeddings and adaptive mode creation. The reverse-input perspective could also be extended beyond diffusion models, e.g., to flow matching, which we leave to future work. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

We presented M OSAIC , a gradient-free framework that adapts the complete reverse input of frozen robot diffusion models. Costs are evaluated on fully denoised outputs, physical and support constraints are balanced through adaptive multipliers, and mode-specific proposals preserve distinct so-lution families. The same formulation couples independently trained priors through a shared terminal objective without training a joint model. Across navigation, grasping, trajectory planning, and biman-ual manipulation, M OSAIC preserves multimodal solutions while adapting frozen priors to deployment-time constraints. It reaches 86 .7% trajectory-planning success, retains 79 .3% 

with unseen tool geometry, and achieves 78 .7% success in collaborative manipulation compared with 42 .7% for the strongest baseline. Reducing the cost of complete rollouts and learning more general mode representations are natural directions for future work.
