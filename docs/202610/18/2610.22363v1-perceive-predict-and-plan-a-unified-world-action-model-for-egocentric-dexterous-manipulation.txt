ABSTRACT
We introduce P3-WAM, a unified world action model for egocentric dexterous
manipulation that learns 3D perception, action-conditioned prediction, and goal-
directed planning through three complementary modes: PERCEIVE, which recon-
structs articulated hand motion and camera trajectories from video; PREDICT,
which generates visual futures under prescribed actions; and PLAN, which jointly
generates action trajectories and their anticipated visual consequences conditioned
on an initial image, a language instruction, and an optional visual goal. Our model
integrates a compact, structured 3D action representation into a pretrained video
diffusion transformer through a mixture-of-transformers architecture with shared
base weights, modality-specific adaptation, and bidirectional visual–action atten-
tion. Randomized modality visibility lets video and 3D actions serve as either
conditioning context or prediction targets across the three modes. Experiments
show that unified training improves all three capabilities over single-mode training,
including gains in planning quality under a vision–language model evaluation.
Through unified training, our model outperforms specialized baselines on 3D hand
reconstruction and action-conditioned video prediction across four datasets, while
producing goal-directed manipulation videos with stronger instruction adherence
and action progression than video-generation baselines.

INTRODUCTION
Dexterous manipulation requires more than mapping pixels directly to actions. An embodied model
must answer three complementary questions: What happened in an observed interaction? What
will happen under a specified action? What should be done to accomplish a desired goal? These
questions correspond to three fundamental capabilities: perceiving the 3D motion underlying observed
interactions, predicting action-conditioned world dynamics, and planning actions together with their
anticipated consequences. Infants can use what they learn from observing adults’ actions to guide
their own behavior (Hauf & Aschersleben, 2008). Through their own interactions, they learn to
predict action outcomes and use those expected outcomes to select actions (Verschoor et al., 2013)
and organize movements toward a goal (Claxton et al., 2003). Together, these findings suggest that
perception, prediction, and planning are linked by knowledge of actions and their consequences.
Despite these connections, prior work on egocentric manipulation has developed along three closely
related but largely separate lines. Egocentric reconstruction methods recover structured 3D interaction
information, such as hand motion and camera trajectories, from observed videos (Pavlakos et al.,
2024; Yu et al., 2025). Action-conditioned world models predict future observations under prescribed
motion trajectories (Hao et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b; Sun et al., 2026). World action models
jointly generate actions and future observations for goal-directed control (Ye et al., 2026). These
capabilities, however, are still largely learned in isolation or coupled only in subsets.
To close this loop, we introduce P3-WAM, a unified world action model for egocentric dexterous
manipulation that integrates three complementary modes: 3D interaction reconstruction (PERCEIVE),
action-conditioned world prediction (PREDICT), and joint action–future planning (PLAN). Recon-
struction develops the model’s capacity for 3D perception by recovering 3D motion from observed
interactions. Prediction teaches the model to anticipate the visual consequences of prescribed actions.
Together, these capabilities provide the foundation for world–action planning, in which the model
learns to jointly generate goal-directed hand action trajectories and their anticipated visual futures.
We learn all three modes through unified training. Figure 1 illustrates these three modes.
We build P3-WAM on a pretrained video diffusion transformer (Wan et al., 2025) to draw on its rich
visual and motion priors. We represent each action by the articulated poses of both hands and the
camera pose, and encode these states as compact tokens alongside the video tokens. We integrate this
representation into the video backbone through a mixture-of-transformers (MoT) architecture with
a dedicated action expert, which shares the backbone’s base weights while incorporating modality-
specific low-rank adaptation (LoRA) (Hu et al., 2022). To train this architecture across all three
modes, we randomly vary the visibility of visual observations and actions: providing video while
withholding actions supports perception, providing actions while withholding future video supports
prediction, and withholding actions and video supports joint action–future planning, exposing the
model to complete modes and partially observed interactions. Over long sequences, successive visual
subgoals specify the target arrangement for each planning window.
Unified training improves all three modes over single-mode training. Our model outperforms the
evaluated baselines in PERCEIVE and PREDICT. Its PLAN videos receive higher VLM scores for
action process and instruction adherence. Robot demonstrations illustrate how generated human-hand
motion can be transferred to a dexterous robot. Our main contributions are:
• A unified world action model, P3-WAM, for egocentric dexterous manipulation that integrates
3D interaction reconstruction, action-conditioned world prediction, and goal-guided world–action
planning. We will release our code and model checkpoints.
• A unified training framework that connects pretrained video priors with structured 3D actions
through a mixture-of-transformers architecture and randomized modality visibility, enabling
complementary learning across perception, prediction, and planning.
• Extensive experiments indicating that perception, prediction, and planning reinforce one another
through unified training, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines across all three tasks. Robot
demonstrations further illustrate the transfer of generated human-hand motion to a dexterous robot.

Monocular Hand Reconstruction.
Recovering 3D hand motion from video requires estimating
hand articulation while accounting for a moving camera. Single-image methods provide hand pose
and shape estimates: HaMeR (Pavlakos et al., 2024) scales transformer capacity and heterogeneous
training data, while WiLoR (Potamias et al., 2025) integrates localization, handedness estimation,
and image-aligned mesh refinement. For egocentric sequences, HaWoR (Zhang et al., 2025) and Dyn-
HaMR (Yu et al., 2025) combine hand-motion estimation with SLAM to recover global trajectories,
using temporal infilling or interacting-hand priors and optimization. Concurrent work MINT (Zhu
et al., 2026) jointly estimates camera and hand motion.
Video generative models offer another source of information about hand motion. Concurrent works
ViDiHand (Wang et al., 2026a) and ACE-Ego-Hand (Liu et al., 2026) use video diffusion features for
hand motion recovery. We train reconstruction jointly with future prediction and planning, allowing
the model to learn from how hand interactions unfold as well as from observed video. This joint
training improves 3D hand reconstruction in our single-mode comparison (Sec. 4.1).
Unified Multimodal Models.
A shared model can connect these tasks by changing which modalities
are observed and which are generated. Unified World Models (Zhu et al., 2025a) couples video
and action diffusion for forward, inverse, policy, and video inference. Language provides a further
interface for world modeling and control: Motus (Bi et al., 2026) combines language, video, and latent
actions, while MotuBrain (MotuBrain Team et al., 2026) jointly models text, video, and embodiment-
specific actions. Geometric modalities extend this shared formulation to spatial reasoning. Flex-
π (Yan et al., 2026a) includes pointmap and feature streams alongside RGB and actions, and
Aether (Zhu et al., 2025b) unifies 4D reconstruction, camera-conditioned prediction, and goal-image-
conditioned visual planning. P3-WAM applies joint reconstruction, prediction, and planning to
articulated hand interaction. It plans hand and camera trajectories together with video and supports
visual goals to preserve spatial targets across a task.

3.2
JOINT VIDEO–ACTION MODELING
We build the model on top of the pretrained Wan2.2-Fun-A14B-Control model (Alibaba PAI, 2025).
We retain its latent video representation, text and first-frame conditioning, skeleton-based control
pathway, and high- and low-noise diffusion experts as the RGB generation backbone.
Observed actions enter through two complementary paths: rendered hand skeletons and camera-ray
maps provide spatially aligned controls for hand placement and viewpoint, while compact action
tokens carry the corresponding states into joint attention.
Specifically, the RGB sequence is encoded into a spatiotemporal latent using the Wan2.1 VAE (Wan
et al., 2025). The temporally aligned hand trajectory is rendered as a 2D skeleton video and encoded
by the same VAE (Fig. 3). The resulting control latent is concatenated with the noisy RGB latent
before DiT patch embedding. For camera conditioning, we express all camera-to-world poses in the
first camera coordinate frame, making the first pose the identity, and construct per-pixel Plücker ray
maps (Plücker, 1865) from the relative poses and camera intrinsics. After temporal packing to the
video-latent frame rate, a lightweight convolutional adapter maps the Plücker representation into the
DiT patch-feature space. Its output is then added to the joint RGB–hand patch embeddings. The
same conditioning interfaces are used by both diffusion experts.
P3-WAM represents RGB and action as coupled latent streams (Fig. 3). A separately pretrained
action autoencoder, frozen during joint training, encodes hand and camera observations into compact
tokens that are appended to the video tokens. Within each transformer block, the RGB and action
experts share the base projection weights. The action expert adds action-specific low-rank updates
and normalization. Both streams attend to the combined RGB and action keys and values, allowing
bidirectional information exchange. We optimize both the shared base weights and action-specific
adaptations. This modality split is used within each of Wan’s high- and low-noise experts, which
operate at different diffusion noise levels. Appendix A specifies the shared layers and token encoding.
3.3
ACTION REPRESENTATION
We represent hands with MANO (Romero et al., 2017), a parametric model that maps hand pose and
shape to an articulated 3D mesh. For each hand, the model predicts global and local rotations ˆRt,
sequence-level shape ˆβ, camera-space root position ˆrt, and hand-presence probability ˆpt. The frozen
action decoder also outputs a visibility probability ˆvt,j for each joint. A fixed, differentiable MANO
layer reconstructs camera-space joints ˆJt from the rotations and shape, placing the wrist at ˆrt.
Observed hand states and camera states are encoded into compact tokens by a pretrained, frozen
action autoencoder. A shared hand encoder embeds each hand independently, while a separate
encoder embeds the camera state. Each frame is represented by three tokens: one for the camera and
one for each hand. Appendix B specifies the detailed architecture of the action encoder.

3.5
INFERENCE
PERCEIVE reads the observed video and predicts the full action sequence in one forward pass. In
PREDICT and PLAN, we iteratively denoise the unobserved RGB latents while keeping observed
images fixed. For PLAN, we keep the generated video and the action trajectory predicted at the final
denoising step. The VAE decoder reconstructs the video frames. The MANO decoder and fixed
MANO layer recover the hand motion, and the camera decoder recovers the camera motion.
Long tasks require several manipulation steps, each with its own target arrangement. We use an
image generator to create visual subgoals for these steps. Each planning window takes its starting
observation, instruction, and goal image as input and generates the video and action trajectory
between the observed images.
Figure 4(a) illustrates the spatial control provided by a visual target: the goal image specifies the
separation between the red blocks, which the text instruction leaves ambiguous. In the longer task in
Figure 4(b), successive visual subgoals specify the block arrangement at each stage. The generated
sequence follows these arrangements across planning windows, while the example without goal
images accumulates placement errors and departs from the intended structure.

4.1
DOES UNIFIED TRAINING BENEFIT EACH MODE?
We initialize the single-mode and unified models from Wan2.2-Fun A14B-Control and evaluate
them at 5,000 total training steps, with all other training variables held fixed. Evaluation covers
EgoDex (Hoque et al., 2026), ARCTIC (Fan et al., 2023), HOT3D (Banerjee et al., 2025), and
EgoLive (Li et al., 2026b). Tables 1 and 2 compare perception, prediction, and planning under
these matched training conditions. Unified training improves both global hand placement and local
articulation in PERCEIVE, reducing camera-space, root-relative, and PA errors by 23.46%, 14.23%,
and 5.69%, respectively. In PREDICT, PSNR increases from 17.356 to 18.138 dB and FVD decreases
from 1105.95 to 1009.84, indicating gains in both pixel accuracy and video feature similarity. The
PLAN-only baseline is trained with visual subgoals. At evaluation, both models receive the same
initial image, instruction, and goal image. We use matched generation settings and score the complete
video, including the goal-conditioned ending, for both models. Unified training improves PLAN’s
VLM scores across all five dimensions (Table 2). The largest gains are in temporal/identity consistency
(2.15 to 3.57) and goal transition (1.98 to 3.29), while higher action-process and physical-interaction
scores reflect more coherent manipulation sequences. Appendices C, E, and G.1 give mode exposure,
evaluation details, supplementary human ratings, and qualitative comparisons.
4.2
PERCEIVE EVALUATION
We compare video-based 3D hand reconstruction with HaMeR (Pavlakos et al., 2024) and Dyn-
HaMR (Yu et al., 2025) on the same four datasets. Following ViDiHand (Wang et al., 2026a), we
report presence-aware joint errors in camera space, after wrist alignment (RR), and after Procrustes
alignment (PA) to assess global placement and local pose, using the protocol in Appendix D.3.
Our model achieves the lowest presence-aware reconstruction error on all four datasets (Table 3).
Average camera-space MPJPE-p is 63.05 mm, compared with 145.34 mm for HaMeR and 231.54
mm for Dyn-HaMR. Root-relative error is 22.54 mm, compared with 46.61 mm for HaMeR and
45.25 mm for Dyn-HaMR, while PA error is 11.79 mm, compared with 16.75 and 32.94 mm,
respectively. Our model also achieves the highest hand-visibility F1 on all four datasets. Appendix F.1
separates joint accuracy from missing-hand penalties, and Appendix F.2 evaluates reconstruction
against the original EgoDex joints.
Qualitative results.
As can be seen in Figure 5, our predictions show more consistent hand
placement and articulation, while HaMeR and Dyn-HaMR miss or misplace hands, and left/right-
hand confusion. We interpret our model’s consistency as a benefit of combining a generative prior
over plausible motions with full-video context, since surrounding frames help resolve ambiguity
when a single frame admits several possible hand poses.

CONCLUSION
We introduced P3-WAM, a unified world action model for egocentric dexterous manipulation that
integrates perception, prediction, and planning. A coupled video–action transformer connects visual
generation with explicit hand and camera trajectories and supports all three modes within the same
model. Through extensive experiments, we show that unified training benefits hand reconstruction,
action-conditioned video prediction, and planning.
Limitations. Estimating the global 3D hand position from monocular video is inherently ambiguous.
Incorporating tactile or depth sensing could provide stronger geometric constraints. Our robot
experiments currently rely on open-loop execution. Introducing closed-loop feedback would allow the
robot to correct its motion online, potentially improving robustness over longer-horizon interactions.

ADDITIONAL METHOD DETAILS
A.1
SHARED WEIGHTS AND MODALITY-SPECIFIC ADAPTATION
Within each diffusion expert, RGB and action use the same base self-attention Q/K/V and output
projections and the same two FFN projections. The action branch adds low-rank updates to these six
projections and to the text cross-attention Q and output projections. Text keys and values are shared.
Each update has the form ∆Wa = (α/r)BaAa, with rank r = 128 and α = 128. Aa uses Kaiming
initialization and Ba starts at zero. The pretrained base weights remain trainable.
The action branch has separate affine Q/K normalization in self-attention, Q normalization and
pre-normalization in text cross-attention, and adaptive normalization parameters, initialized from the
RGB branch. The parameter-free layer normalizations retain the same operation in both branches.
The high- and low-noise diffusion experts each have their own base weights and action adaptations.
The video VAE, text encoder, and action codec are frozen.

ACTION CODEC DETAILS
Architecture and training.
The deterministic action autoencoder represents each frame with three
64-dimensional tokens: camera, left hand, and right hand. A shared hand encoder takes the 21-joint
observation for each hand, and a separate camera encoder takes rotation, translation, and field of
view. Encoders and decoders use residual MLPs of width 512 with three residual blocks. Training
minimizes masked reconstruction losses for joints, image coordinates, depth, visibility, and camera
states. We use AdamW with learning rate 10−3 and a global batch of 128. Codec training takes
approximately 110 H100 GPU-hours. The codec is frozen during video–action training.
Table 6 reports held-out reconstruction on EgoDex, ARCTIC, HOT3D, and EgoLive, matching the
main reporting datasets. The multi-domain codec achieves camera-space joint errors of 0.868–2.600
mm and image-space errors of 0.323–0.882 pixels. These measurements characterize compression
of annotated actions before video-model prediction.

IMPLEMENTATION AND TRAINING DETAILS
Optimization.
We initialize the video backbone from Wan2.2-Fun-A14B-Control and train the
base transformer weights, camera adapter, action adaptations, and MANO head. The video VAE,
text encoder, and pretrained action codec remain frozen. Training uses bfloat16, fully sharded data
parallelism, and gradient checkpointing. The final unified training recipe uses 16 H100 GPUs, one
clip per GPU, and a global batch of 16. Clips contain 81 frames at 16 fps and 848 × 480 resolution.
We use AdamW with (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.999), weight decay 0.01, and a backbone learning rate
of 10−5, held constant after 200 warmup updates. We clip video gradients at norm 1 and use an
exponential moving average with decay 0.999 for evaluation. We train the model on 16 H100 GPUs
for approximately 450 hours in total.
Training-mode exposure.
The single-mode and unified ablations use the same architecture, ini-
tialization, data mixture, and total number of optimizer updates. Single-mode training assigns every
clip to its designated mode. Unified training uses the mixture in Appendix A.3: 20%, 50%, and 30%
of latent positions are assigned to PERCEIVE, PREDICT, and PLAN in expectation. The whole-clip
branch accounts for 10%, 25%, and 15% of clips, respectively; the other 50% use independently
sampled modes across time. These are sampling probabilities, so matching total updates does not
match the exposure to any one complete mode. Action supervision uses available annotations in
every mode, while video flow matching supervises unobserved RGB positions. The shared trans-
former weights receive gradients from both objectives, connecting action reconstruction and video
generation across the sampled tasks.
Sampling.
PERCEIVE uses one action prediction from the clean video at zero noise. PREDICT and
PLAN use 50 denoising steps, with the high-/low-noise expert boundary at τ = 0.9. Text guidance is
6 for the main PREDICT comparison and 2 for PLAN evaluation. Observed initial and goal latents
stay fixed throughout sampling; Appendix D.4 specifies their encoding. Paired comparisons use
matched inputs and seeds.

D.2
PREDICT RGB AND HAND-REGION METRICS
For full-frame and hand-region CLIP-I (Radford et al., 2021), we compute paired image-feature
cosine similarity using OpenAI ViT-B/32 with its standard bicubic short-edge resize to 224, center
crop, and normalization. FVD (Unterthiner et al., 2018) uses the StyleGAN-V (Skorokhodov et al.,
2022) Kinetics-400 I3D TorchScript model’s 400-dimensional pre-softmax features, bilinear resizing
to 224 × 224, pixel scaling to [−1, 1], and one feature vector per video. We use unbiased empirical
covariances and an exact low-rank Fréchet-distance calculation.
Hand-region metrics use WiLoR detections with confidence threshold 0.3 and NMS IoU threshold
0.7. We expand ground-truth boxes by 1.5×, clip them to the image boundary, and apply the same
crop to the reference and prediction. Crop PSNR, SSIM (Wang et al., 2004), and VGG-LPIPS (Zhang
et al., 2018) are measured at the native crop resolution, with small boundary crops padded for LPIPS.
For IoU, we match unexpanded boxes by maximum total IoU within handedness classes. We divide
the matched IoU sum by the larger detection count, so unmatched detections contribute zero. Frames
with no detections on either side are omitted; one-sided detections receive zero. Crop metrics average
hands within a frame, valid frames within a window, and then windows within a dataset/method.
Crop metrics use frames with a valid GT hand detection. FVD is computed separately for each
dataset/method. The Macro block reports the arithmetic mean of the four dataset-level scores for
each method and metric, using unrounded values and equal dataset weights.
D.3
PERCEIVE TARGETS AND METRICS
Following ViDiHand (Wang et al., 2026a), we report presence-aware MPJPE-p in camera-space, root-
relative, and Procrustes-aligned coordinates. A reference hand is eligible when at least one annotated
joint lies inside the image at camera depth greater than 0.01 m. This is also the positive GT definition
for hand visibility. Joint errors use the valid annotated joints of each eligible hand; RR requires a
valid wrist, and PA additionally requires at least three joints and a nondegenerate prediction.
Association and missing-hand penalties.
Our model emits a hand prediction when its maximum
joint-visibility probability is at least 0.5; HaMeR and Dyn-HaMR use their released hand detections.
All methods are matched to reference hands of the same side by descending mesh-box IoU, using
a 1.1× enlarged reference box and an IoU threshold of 0.1. An eligible hand without a match
receives a canonical MANO hand in the penalized metrics. Its camera-space and RR penalties
are the corresponding canonical-hand errors; its PA penalty uses the canonical RR error without
Procrustes fitting. The same matching and missing-hand penalties apply to all three methods.
Ordinary errors for each method use its own successfully matched eligible hands, rather than a
common intersection across methods.
For EgoDex-MANO, we fit MANO to the annotated 3D joints of each hand, optimizing global
orientation, finger articulation, and shape with pose and shape regularization. The annotated wrist
anchors the hand position. We reconstruct the fitted MANO21 joints in camera coordinates and
use them as the common evaluation targets for all methods. ARCTIC, HOT3D, and EgoLive use
their provided 3D hand annotations. We average windows within each dataset and then average
the four datasets equally.

For HaMeR, we recover camera-space translation using known dataset

focal length 5000 max(W, H)/256 (16562.5 pixels for our 848 × 480 inputs). For each detected
hand in each frame, let qj be its predicted wrist-relative 3D joints and uj its native full-image 2D
projections. We fit t by minimizing P

D.4
GOAL-IMAGE CONDITIONING
Both PLAN settings receive the task instruction and initial image. The default additionally conditions
on cgoal = IT ; the w/o goal image setting uses cgoal = ∅. The goal image fixes the terminal
video latent during sampling, while hand and camera trajectories are generated jointly. Figure 9
shows the conditioning masks.
Encoding the reference images.
At inference, we repeat the goal image into an 81-frame constant
clip and encode it with the video VAE, starting with a fresh temporal cache. Its final latent supplies
the terminal observation at ℓ= 21. The initial image is encoded separately in the same way, using its
first latent at ℓ= 1. These encodes use the two reference images alone. Both observed latents stay
fixed during denoising, and the VAE decodes the entire output video. The terminal latent corresponds
to frames t = 78–81; the intervening frames t = 2–77 are freely generated. The final column of
Figure 7 shows the supplied goal image as a reference.
During training, observation masks select clean latents from VAE-encoded training clips. The same
mask construction keeps observed positions clean and excludes them from the video loss. Action
supervision uses the annotations aligned to all video frames, including the terminal interval. At
inference, hand and camera trajectories are predicted from queries for all frames.
Evaluation interval.
The VLM evaluates the complete video, including its transition into the goal
segment, with the goal image supplied separately as a reference. Our qualitative analysis compares
object placement before this segment begins. Appendix F.3 also reports video–action consistency
over the freely generated interval.

E.5
REAL-WORLD EXECUTION EVALUATION
In addition to evaluating the generated videos perceptually, we test whether the predicted manipulation
behaviors can be transferred to and executed by a physical robot. We conduct two real-world
experiments in which the model-generated action sequences are converted into executable robot
trajectories and replayed in the corresponding physical environments. This evaluation tests whether
20


Preprint
the generated behavior captures sufficiently accurate object interactions and action ordering to
support downstream robot execution.
Starting from a generated manipulation video, we reconstruct the relevant objects and scene and
recover their motion over time. The reconstructed hand motion is then retargeted to the robot hand
to obtain an initial robot trajectory. The resulting action sequence is then deployed on the physical
robot without further modification.
Thus, the complete evaluation pipeline is generated video →scene and object reconstruction →
robot retargeting →real-world execution. This follows do-as-I-do Paliwal et al. (2026) evaluation
paradigm, in which a visual demonstration is converted into an executable robot behavior.
For both experiments, we report whether execution reproduces the intended manipulation process
and reaches the specified task outcome. These real-world trials complement the human and VLM
evaluations above: whereas the perceptual metrics measure whether an action appears correct and
physically plausible in the generated video, real-world execution provides a direct test of whether the
predicted interaction can be grounded into a physically executable manipulation trajectory.

G.2
GOAL-CONDITIONING ABLATION AND BASELINE COMPARISONS
The default PLAN task is conditioned on a visual goal. The w/o goal image configuration removes
this image while retaining the initial image and instruction; hand and camera trajectories remain
outputs. For multiple windows, visual subgoals are the goal images specifying intermediate stage
targets. Removing the goal image from every window is distinct from retaining a final visual goal
while removing only intermediate subgoals.
Matched condition-ablation protocol.
The with/without-goal corpus contains 188 paired genera-
tions over 94 windows, with two matched seeds per window. Both settings use the same checkpoint,
initial images, instructions, and sampling settings, including 50 denoising steps and CFG 2. Each
window starts from its reference initial image. Only the goal-image condition is removed; the no-goal
model does not receive future hand or camera states.
Comparisons without a goal image.
Figure 13 compares our model with Wan2.2 and Cosmos
when all three methods receive only the initial image and instruction. Our model maintains the shape
of the wooden support as the hands place it into the corner mount. In the puzzle example, it keeps
individual pieces identifiable as the hands separate them. Wan2.2 stretches the puzzle pieces, while
Cosmos deforms the support and merges puzzle pieces into elongated strips. These examples show
hand–object consistency during manipulation without a supplied terminal image.
A visual goal adds control over the desired arrangement beyond the instruction. Figure 4, for example,
uses the goal image to specify the separation between the red blocks.
Spatial targets and multiwindow behavior.
Figure 4 illustrates the task information supplied
by visual goals. The no-goal example places the blocks differently and the autoregressive rollout
accumulates placement errors. The goal-conditioned windows use prescribed reference starts and
guidance 2; the no-goal rollout uses generated starts and text guidance 6. This execution-strategy
comparison is separate from the matched condition ablation above. The subgoals provide stage
targets; these comparisons do not evaluate closed-loop error detection or correction.

RESPONSIBLE USE AND ASSET DOCUMENTATION
H.1
SOURCE ASSETS AND INTENDED USE
The reporting datasets are EgoDex (Hoque et al., 2026), ARCTIC (Fan et al., 2023), HOT3D (Baner-
jee et al., 2025), and EgoLive (Li et al., 2026b). Their video, action annotations, and calibration
remain subject to the respective providers’ access and redistribution terms. Wan model weights and
the MANO assets retain their upstream terms. Derived annotations and visualizations should pre-
serve source attribution; access-controlled raw data and model assets should be obtained from
their providers.
Intended use.
P3-WAM supports research on visual prediction, 3D perception, and robot manipula-
tion. Its benchmark coverage reflects the activities, cameras, hands, and environments in the source
datasets. Performance across broader populations and embodiments requires additional evaluation.
H.2
ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS
Egocentric video can reveal bystanders, personal spaces, and daily activities. Use and sharing of
these data should follow the source datasets’ access and consent terms, with identifying information
minimized in released examples. Generated interaction videos should be labeled as synthetic. Motion
generated from visual goals requires embodiment-specific validation and supervised execution before
use on physical robots.
