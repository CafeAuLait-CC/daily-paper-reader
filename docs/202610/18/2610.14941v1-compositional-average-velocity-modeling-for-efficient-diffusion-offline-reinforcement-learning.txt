# Compositional Average Velocity Modeling for Efficient Diffusion Offline Reinforcement Learning 

Thanh Nguyen 1 and Abu Hanif Muhammad Syarubany 1 and Hwanhee Kim 1 and Chang D. Yoo 1

Abstract — Diffusion-based policies have recently emerged as a powerful paradigm for offline reinforcement learning, but their reliance on multi-step denoising introduces substantial computational overhead and requires backpropagation through time during training, limiting scalability and practical deploy-ment. In this work, we propose Instant Flow Q-Learning (InsQL), a single-step generative policy learning framework that retains the expressive capacity of diffusion models while eliminating iterative sampling and BPTT. InsQL builds on the flow-matching perspective of diffusion policies, but replaces marginal velocity parameterization with average-velocity mod-eling and introduces a principled compositional mechanism, yielding a theoretically grounded objective that learns average velocity consistently across scales. This formulation provides a direct noise-to-action mapping and enables single-step action generation. InsQL further unifies compositional average-velocity modeling with behavior-regularized actor–critic optimization in a single framework, requiring only a single policy network and avoiding distillation, multi-stage procedures, and Jacobian computations during training. At inference time, actions are generated in a single forward pass and can be refined via value-guided candidate selection to improve performance. Extensive experiments on standard offline RL benchmarks and real-world robotic manipulation tasks show that InsQL matches or surpasses state-of-the-art diffusion-based methods while substantially reducing both training and inference time. Overall, these results establish compositional average-velocity modeling as an efficient and scalable alternative to multi-step generative policies for offline RL. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Offline reinforcement learning (RL) aims to learn decision-making policies from fixed datasets, enabling deployment in domains where online exploration is unsafe, costly, or infea-sible, including robotics, autonomous driving, and healthcare [1]. A growing focus in offline RL is the development of policies that are both expressive and efficient , especially in complex control settings where behavior data can induce multimodal action distributions [2], [3]. Generative policy classes—most notably diffusion-based models—have recently emerged as a compelling alternative to Gaussian actors by transforming a simple base distribution into a flexible action distribution through learnable transformations [4]–[8]. This increased representational capacity has translated into strong empirical improvements over Gaussian policies on widely used offline RL benchmarks [2], [4].

Despite these gains, most diffusion-style policies remain tightly coupled to iterative generation , producing actions through multi-step refinement from noise [2]. This dependency introduces substantial computational overhead during training, where action sampling is repeatedly required throughout optimization [2], [9]. Moreover, when combined with value-based objectives, multi-step generation can impose addi-tional optimization burdens—often involving backpropagation through time (BPTT) across solver steps—which may increase sensitivity to hyperparameters and affect final performance [3]. At deployment, the same multi-step procedure translates into non-trivial inference latency, posing a challenge for real-time closed-loop control. Existing work has attempted to mitigate these issues through faster samplers [9], alternative learning paradigms (e.g., IQL-style updates) [10], and acceleration mechanisms such as auxiliary policies or distillation-based training [3], [11]–[13], sometimes involving Jacobian-related computations [14]. While effective in certain regimes, these approaches often introduce extra design choices, training dependencies, or multi-stage pipelines that complicate scaling and impose trade-offs among simplicity, efficiency, and policy quality. We propose Instant Flow Q-Learning (InsQL) , an end-to-end offline RL framework that enables single-step action generation during both training and inference. InsQL builds on the flow-matching interpretation of diffusion policies, but is explicitly designed to avoid iterative denoising and solver un-rolling. Instead of learning an instantaneous marginal velocity field at intermediate timesteps, InsQL parameterizes the policy using the average velocity over finite intervals , allowing the full noise-to-action transformation to be queried directly. To make this representation consistent and learnable across inter-val lengths, we introduce a velocity composition mechanism that recursively composes short-interval average velocities into a coherent long-range update; in the infinitesimal limit, this structure recovers the marginal velocity field learned by standard flow matching. As a result, when integrated into a behavior-regularized actor–critic objective, InsQL eliminates solver unrolling and backpropagation through time (BPTT) over denoising trajectories, yielding a simpler and substantially more compute-efficient learning procedure while preserving expressive capacity. Finally, to better exploit value information at test time, we incorporate a lightweight Q-guided sampling strategy that improves action quality at a modest additional cost. Empirically, InsQL achieves state-of-the-art or competitive performance on standard D4RL benchmarks while significantly reducing training time and increasing decision frequency. We further demonstrate its arXiv:2610.14941v1 [cs.LG] 10 Oct 2026 practicality in real-world robotic manipulation, where InsQL attains higher success rates than prior methods. II. R ELATED WORK 

Offline reinforcement learning (Offline RL) aims to learn decision-making policies from static datasets without addi-tional environment interaction [1], [15]–[18]. The paradigm has recently been explored in conjunction with online rein-forcement learning [19]–[21] to offer a more sample-efficient learning paradigm. To mitigate distributional shift and over-estimation, early value-based offline RL methods introduced conservative objectives, including CQL [6], TD3+BC [5], and IQL [7]. While often effective, these approaches typically rely on unimodal Gaussian actors, which can struggle to represent the multimodal action distributions induced by heterogeneous behavior data. Motivated by their ability to model complex, high-dimensional distributions, diffusion models [22], [23] have recently been incorporated into offline RL in several ways. Prior work has used diffusion models for trajectory synthesis and planning [24], [25], as expressive policy parameterizations [2], [10], and as generative augmentation tools [26]. Among policy-based methods, Diffusion Q-Learning (DQL) [2] is a prominent baseline that replaces the Gaussian actor in TD3+BC with a diffusion policy to better capture multimodal action spaces. Follow-up studies such as Clean Diffuser [4] and recent large-scale evaluations [13], [27] report that DQL remains consistently competitive relative to both planner-style diffusion approaches and conventional policy-learning baselines. However, DQL’s reliance on iterative denoising introduces non-trivial computational overhead during both training and inference [9], and its interaction with value-based optimization can lead to sensitivity and occasionally suboptimal convergence [3]. Several lines of work attempt to address these limitations. One direction accelerates sampling by designing improved solvers that reduce the number of denoising steps required by diffusion policies [9]. Another avoids backpropagation through time by training a diffusion policy primarily via behavior cloning and using a separately trained IQL-style value function to reweight or select actions [10]. A comple-mentary strategy distills a multi-step diffusion policy into a single-step actor [11], [12]. However, IQL-style learning can underperform actor–critic alternatives in some regimes [28]. To obtain single-step inference while retaining actor–critic updates, [3] replaces diffusion with a flow-based policy trained to imitate behavior and then distills it into a one-step actor; however, the distillation stage still requires repeated queries to the underlying iterative generator. In addition, recent one-step formulations based on MeanFlow [14], [29], [30] typically involve explicit Jacobian-related computations, which have been argued to suffer from explosion or poor conditioning in high dimensions [31]. In contrast, our goal is to remove the iterative bottleneck directly by training an end-to-end single-step generative policy that preserves expressiveness while avoiding multi-stage training procedures, explicit distillation, and Jacobian computation. M ETHODOLOGY 

We now introduce Instant Flow Q-Learning (InsQL) ,designed to satisfy three requirements: high expressive capacity for multimodal action distributions, single-step action generation for efficient training and inference without BPTT, and a simple framework without distillation, multi-stage training, or Jacobian computation. Unlike conventional flow-matching approaches that learn marginal velocity fields at infinitesimal time scales, InsQL parameterizes the policy in terms of the average velocity over finite time intervals. As illustrated in Fig. 1(a,b), the marginal velocity represents the instantaneous tangent direction of a trajectory, whereas our formulation captures the finite-interval average velocity between two time points. A key structural property of average velocity is compo-sitionality: the average velocity over a long interval can be constructed by composing average velocities over shorter sub-intervals. As shown in Fig. 1(c), a long-range average velocity (blue) can be decomposed into intermediate average velocities (green), each of which can in turn be decomposed into shorter ones (red). This hierarchical decomposition yields a well-defined limiting behavior: as the interval length approaches zero, the average velocity converges to the marginal velocity field. Standard flow matching therefore emerges naturally as the infinitesimal base case, providing both a principled boundary condition and a natural termination criterion for the recursive construction. Leveraging this structure, InsQL uses a single shared network to model average velocity across all temporal scales. Training is anchored at the marginal-velocity level to learn short-interval average velocities, which are then recursively composed to represent longer intervals. Through this aggregation, the model ultimately learns the full noise-to-action average velocity, enabling direct single-step action generation while preserving the expressive power of flow-based generative policies.

A. Offline RL Benchmark. 

We evaluate InsQL on a diverse set of tasks from the D4RL benchmark suite [32], a standard testbed for offline RL. Our experiments cover multiple domains, including locomotion and navigation, to assess generalization across different control regimes. MuJoCo locomotion tasks evaluate fine-grained continuous control under complex physical dynamics. Agents must maintain stability and efficiency while performing short-horizon decision-making in high-dimensional action spaces. AntMaze combines continuous control with long-horizon planning in maze-like environments. Agents must navigate to goal locations under sparse rewards, requiring coordination between low-level locomotion and high-level planning. Fig. 3 (a-d) illustrates representative tasks used in our experiments. 

a) Baselines: We evaluate InsQL against a diverse set of competitive baselines spanning multiple policy classes. These include: (1) Non-diffusion methods , namely Behavior Cloning (BC), TD3-BC [5], and IQL [7]; (2) Multi-step diffusion-based approaches , including IDQL [10], DQL [2], and EDP [9]; and (3) Single-step flow-based policies , represented by FQL [3]. 

B. Real-World Robotic Manipulation. 

We further evaluate InsQL on real-robot manipulation using a 7-DOF Sawyer arm with a parallel-jaw gripper in a structured tabletop workspace (Fig. 2). The scene contains both task-relevant and distractor entities (e.g., a banana, a cube, a plate/tray, and a drawer), each instrumented with AprilTags [33]. A static RGB camera tracks tag poses, and all positions are expressed in the coordinate frame of a fixed table tag, yielding a low-dimensional, fully observed Markov state without loss of generality. Specifically, the state is a 10-D vector s = [ pa, p o, p d, g s] ∈ R10 comprising the end-effector tag position pa ∈ R3, the target object position po ∈ R3, a distractor pose pd ∈ R3, and the gripper status gs ∈ { 0, 1}. Actions are 5-D relative commands 

a = [ vx, v y , v z , ω ψ , g cmd ] ∈ R5 (Cartesian velocity, yaw rate, and gripper command), where gcmd is thresholded for binary actuation. We consider two manipulation tasks requiring coordinated reaching and grasping as illustrated in Fig. 3 (e-f): Pickup Banana (reach, grasp, and lift a toy banana to a target height) and Sweep Cube (push a cube to a goal region on the tray). Success is defined by task-specific geometric thresholds on the tracked target pose (e.g., height for pickup and planar displacement for sweeping). 

a) Dataset collection.: For each task, we collect an offline dataset of 100 trajectories by combining 50 successful human teleoperated demonstrations with 50 autonomously generated suboptimal rollouts. Suboptimal data are mined either from a noisy BC policy trained on the human demon-strations or from a noisy random reaching PD controller, producing a multimodal mixture of near-success and failure behaviors. This combination provides both achievable solu-tions and informative failure modes, yielding a feasible and multimodal dataset. 

b) Baselines.: We compare InsQL against Behavior Cloning (BC) and DQL, reporting real-robot success rates over randomized initializations.

In this paper, we introduced Instant Flow Q-Learning (InsQL) , an end-to-end offline RL framework that enables 

single-step action generation by modeling compositional 

average velocity rather than marginal velocity fields. This design eliminates solver unrolling and BPTT while retaining high expressiveness, and can be further improved at test time via lightweight Q-guided candidate selection. InsQL achieves state-of-the-art or competitive results on D4RL MuJoCo and AntMaze with substantially lower training cost and higher decision frequency, and also performs strongly on real-robot tabletop manipulation. Overall, InsQL demonstrates that single-step flow-based policies offer a practical and scalable alternative to multi-step diffusion policies for offline and resource-constrained settings. 

Limitation. This work focuses on state-based Offline RL tasks, which avoid confounding factors such as visual fea-ture extraction, multimodal fusion, and architecture-specific biases, allowing a cleaner evaluation of InsQL’s algorithmic contributions. Extending InsQL to more challenging settings, including offline-to-online reinforcement learning, visual observation-based control, and broader applications [34]–[36], remains an important direction for future work.
