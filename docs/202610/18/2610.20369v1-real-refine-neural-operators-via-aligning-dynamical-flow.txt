Abstract
Neural partial differential equation (PDE) surrogates trained with mean squared
error (MSE) can capture dominant dynamics but often lose high-frequency in-
formation and fine-scale structures.
Generative models (e.g., Flow Matching)
can capture the distribution of fine-scale details, yet stochastic errors may accu-
mulate during autoregressive rollouts and compromise long-term stability.
To
address these limitations, we introduce Refine and Align (ReAl), a model-agnostic
plug-in that augments pretrained PDE surrogates to improve the reconstruction of
high-frequency structures associated with complex physical phenomena. In the re-
finement stage, a conditional flow-matching model learns the residual distribution
around the deterministic base prediction, recovering missing fine-scale structures
while retaining the dominant dynamics. In the alignment stage, to mitigate the gen-
erative discrepancies amplified in the autoregressive rollout, particularly in chaotic
systems, we generate multiple trajectories from the same input and reinforce the
refiner with trajectory-level rewards, thereby achieving long-horizon dynamical
alignment.
Experiments across diverse surrogate architectures and PDE tasks
demonstrate that ReAl consistently improves high-frequency fidelity, forecasting
accuracy, and long-horizon stability.

Introduction
Partial differential equations (PDEs) are fundamental to modeling physical systems across fluid
mechanics (Davidson, 2004), heat and mass transfer (Bergman, 2011), electromagnetics (Schwinger
et al., 1998), and chemical diffusion (Crank, 1979). Classical finite-difference (Morton & Mayers,
1994) and finite-element methods (Hughes, 2003) provide reliable solutions, but their computational
costs grow rapidly with spatial resolution, dimensionality, and parameter-space size. This limitation
has driven the development of deep-learning-based PDE surrogates and neural operators, including
DeepONet (Lu et al., 2021), the Fourier Neural Operator (Li et al., 2020), and the Physics-Informed
Neural Operator (Goswami et al., 2023). By approximating PDE solution operators, these methods
enable substantially faster inference for large-scale simulation and real-time scientific prediction.
Most deep-learning-based PDE surrogates are trained with mean squared error (MSE) objective,
which provides stable optimization and effectively captures large-scale, low-frequency dynamics.
However, as a mean-regression objective, MSE tends to suppress high-frequency information, local
vortices, and sharp interfaces
(Lippe et al., 2023; Fotiadis et al., 2025), particularly on real-
world datasets such as RealPDEBench (Hu et al., 2026), where measurement noise and unresolved
physical processes further complicate the underlying dynamics. Generative models (Ho et al., 2020;
Rombach et al., 2022; Lipman et al., 2023) can alleviate this limitation by modeling the conditional
distribution of PDE solutions instead of producing only one deterministic prediction, enabling richer
high-frequency details (Yao et al., 2026; Utkarsh et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2025). However, stochastic
sampling may introduce predictive variance and compromise the dynamical consistency and long-
term stability required by deterministic PDE systems.
Motivated by these observations, we adopt a complementary modeling strategy: retain the MSE-
trained surrogate as a stable predictor of dominant dynamics and apply a conditional flow-based
refiner to its output. Rather than regenerating the entire PDE state, the refiner is conditioned on

As shown in Fig.1 (a), this strategy recovers high-frequency information lost by MSE training
while preserving low-frequency dynamics consistent in PDE predictions. Despite these benefits,
a generative refiner can only partially mitigate the rollout shift rather than completely eliminate
it. Stochastic perturbations can accumulate during autoregressive rollout, creating a gap between
recovering fine-scale structures at individual steps and maintaining forecasting accuracy over the
resulting trajectory (Luo, 2025; Yoo et al., 2026). As shown in Fig.1 (b), the base model preserves
low-frequency dynamics over longer horizons, while high-frequency errors progressively accumulate
and amplify, degrading long-term stability and physical consistency.
These observations motivate optimizing the refiner beyond individual prediction quality by ac-
counting for its cumulative effect on the entire autoregressive trajectory. To this end, inspired by
reinforcement learning for diffusion models (Liu et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2026), we introduce
trajectory-alignment post-training for the flow-based refiner. Starting from the same input state, we
sample multiple autoregressive trajectories using independent random seeds and evaluate their long-
term quality with trajectory-level rewards. These rewards guide flow-matching updates to the same
refiner, favoring refinements that yield accurate long-horizon forecasts. This extends the optimization
target from one-step accuracy to long-term trajectory quality without sacrificing the refiner’s ability
to recover fine-scale structures. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose Refine and Align (ReAl), a lightweight, model-agnostic PDE forecasting plug-
in based on Flow Matching. ReAl addresses the loss of high-frequency information and
fine-scale structures commonly observed in MSE-pretrained surrogates on real-world data,
refining their predictions without modifying the underlying architectures.
• To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to post-train long-term autoregressive PDE
trajectories from a reinforcement learning perspective. ReAl samples multiple stochastic
rollouts from the same initial state and optimizes the Flow refiner with trajectory-level
rewards, aligning local residual corrections with long-term dynamical evolution.
• Extensive experiments demonstrate that ReAl improves both prediction accuracy and long-
term stability. Compared with the base models, it reduces the average RMSE by 34.3% at
the 1-st rollout step and by 45.4% at the 20-th step, with larger gains over longer horizons
on RealPDEBench.
ReAl also recovers high-frequency physical structures, suppresses
long-term error accumulation, and mitigates numerical dissipation.

Related Works
Neural PDE Surrogates.
Neural PDE surrogates approximate expensive numerical solvers by
learning mappings between physical fields. Representative approaches include DeepONet (Lu et al.,
2021), the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) (Li et al., 2021), and physics-informed neural opera-
tors (Goswami et al., 2023). Subsequent architectures have explored latent spectral representations,
geometry-aware attention, hierarchical modeling, and large-scale pretraining, including LSM (Wu
2


et al., 2023), DPOT (Hao et al., 2024), Transolver (Wu et al., 2024), Poseidon (Herde et al., 2024),
and HINE (Jiang et al., 2026). Although these models are effective for autoregressive forecasting,
prediction errors and insufficiently modeled frequency components can accumulate over long roll-
outs (Lippe et al., 2023; Yoo et al., 2026). We retain such models as deterministic backbones and
improve their predictions through a model-agnostic refinement plug-in.
Generative Models and Refinements for PDEs. Diffusion and Flow Matching models (Ho et al.,
2020; Rombach et al., 2022; Lipman et al., 2023) have been widely applied to PDE simulation, data
assimilation, and physics-constrained generation (Shysheya et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2026; Utkarsh
et al., 2026). DiffusionPDE addresses PDE solving under partial observations (Huang et al., 2024),
DYffusion targets probabilistic multi-step forecasting (R¨uhling Cachay et al., 2023), and autoregres-
sive conditional diffusion models have been studied for turbulent-flow prediction (Kohl et al., 2026).
Other methods improve multiscale reconstruction through wavelet or latent representations (Hu
et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). PDE-Refiner (Lippe et al., 2023), Adaptive Flow Matching (Fotiadis
et al., 2025), PDE-SpectralRefiner (Luo, 2025), and FlowRefiner (Dai et al., 2026) further recover
non-dominant frequency components through generative refinement. Unlike those methods based
primarily on short-horizon objectives, ReAl directly optimizes long-term autoregressive rollouts.
Post-Training of Diffusion Models. Reward-based post-training aligns diffusion and Flow Matching
models with task-specific objectives. DDPO (Black et al., 2024) and DPOK (Fan et al., 2023) optimize
diffusion sampling through reinforcement learning, Flow-GRPO (Liu et al., 2025) compares groups
of samples, and DiffusionNFT (Zheng et al., 2026) employs negative-aware online reinforcement
learning. We extend these ideas to PDE forecasting by post-training the refiner with trajectory-level
rewards for long-term accuracy, stability, and physical consistency.
3
Method
In this section, we introduce Refine and Align (ReAl), a model-agnostic plug-in for stable and accurate
long-term partial differential equation forecasting, as illustrated in Fig. 2. During refinement, we
pretrain a flow matching refiner to correct the residual distribution of the base predictions. During
alignment, we employ trajectory-level post-training to improve long-term forecasting stability.

Experiment
Benchmarks. We evaluate on RealPDEBench (Hu et al., 2026), comprising four physical sub-
datasets: Cylinder, Controlled Cylinder, Foil, and FSI, which provides paired numerical simulations
and real-world measurements. Real observations contain noise, unresolved effects, and finer-scale
dynamics absent from simulations. We consider two settings: Sim2Real, training on simulations and
testing on real data; and Real2Real, training and testing on real data2.
Evaluation metircs. To evaluate quality of prediction , we report the root mean squared error
(RMSE) and relative (Rel. 𝐿2) error. To further assess physical fidelity, we use the Fourier-space
RMSE (fRMSE), frequency error (FE), and kinetic energy error (KE). Detailed definitions of these
metrics are provided in the Appendix A.
Experiment setup. In our experiments, following the task setting of RealPDEBench, we use 10
consecutive input frames to directly predict the subsequent 10 frames. We adopt U-Net (Ronneberger
et al., 2015), FNO (Li et al., 2021), DPOT-S (Hao et al., 2024), and Transolver (Wu et al., 2024)
as the base architectures. We then freeze the base model and train a lightweight time-conditioned
U-Net flow matching Refiner (approximately 5.04M parameters, typically under one-tenth of the
base model size) for 50,000 steps with a batch size of 16 and a learning rate of 1 × 10−4. Details are
provided in Appendix B. Subsequently, we initialize ReAl from the best Refiner checkpoint while
keeping the base model frozen, and optimize ReAl for 10,000 steps using the trajectory-alignment
objective. We use AdamW with a batch size of 4 and a learning rate of 2 × 10−5. For each condition,
we sample 𝐾= 4 complete trajectories. Each trajectory consists of five autoregressive rollout blocks,
with each block generated by integrating the flow velocity field using four Euler flow steps. We use
the negative MSE of the final, fifth prediction block as the trajectory reward. We set 𝛽= 0.1 in
Eq. 14, and set 𝜆ref = 0.01 and 𝜆sup = 0.1 in Eq. 15.

4.2
Ablation Study
Ablation of Reward Horizon and Metric. We investigate how the rollout block used to compute the
training reward, ℎ𝑟∈{1, 5, 10}, and the choice of reward metric affect rollout performance. A clear
horizon-matching effect emerges: ℎ𝑟= 1, 5, and 10 achieve the best RMSE at R1, R5, and R10,
respectively, while remaining competitive elsewhere. Longer reward horizons improve recursive
stability. RMSE provides the most consistent gains in RMSE, Rel. 𝐿2, and fRMSE, while KE yields
the lowest kinetic-energy error. FE optimization does not consistently minimize FE, indicating that
physics-specific rewards need not improve their corresponding metrics. We therefore use RMSE
with ℎ𝑟= 5 by default, which best balances short- and long-horizon performance.
5
Conclusion
In this paper, we propose Refine and Align (ReAl), a model-agnostic post-training framework
for long-horizon PDE forecasting.
ReAl retains an MSE-trained base model to capture stable
dominant dynamics and uses a lightweight conditional Flow Refiner to generatively correct prediction
residuals, recovering fine-scale high-frequency structures that are often lost under MSE training. We
further introduce trajectory-alignment post-training, which optimizes the Refiner using trajectory-
level rewards from multiple stochastic autoregressive rollouts to align local corrections with long-
term dynamics and suppress accumulated sampling perturbations. We systematically evaluate ReAl
on RealPDEBench using UNet, FNO, DPOT-S, and Transolver. ReAl reduces RMSE by 33.9%
on average for 1-st step predictions and 45.4% by 20-th step, which effectively mitigates error
accumulation, trajectory divergence, and numerical dissipation over long autoregressive rollouts.
Frequency analysis shows that the Refiner gradually extends to multiscale correction, while ReAl
mainly provides additional improvements in high-frequency errors. These results demonstrate that
ReAl serves as a general, lightweight, and plug-and-play post-training module that improves fine-
scale detail recovery and long-horizon prediction stability for diverse PDE surrogates under real-world
data distributions and simulation-to-reality shifts.

B
Details of architecture
We evaluate four representative neural operators under a unified direct multi-step forecasting protocol.
U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015). We use a spatiotemporal U-Net with an encoder–decoder archi-
tecture and skip connections. The network contains four resolution scales with channel multipliers
[1, 2, 4, 8], corresponding to feature widths 64, 128, 256, and 512 for the 64 × 64 fields used in our
experiments. Downsampling captures large-scale flow structures, while skip connections preserve
local details and sharp spatial variations during decoding. The 10-frame input and 10-frame output
are processed jointly, enabling the model to learn temporal correlations within a prediction block.
The model contains 85.98M parameters for three-field datasets; for Controlled Cylinder, where two
additional control variables are included in the input, the model has 86.02M parameters.
FNO (Li et al., 2021). We employ a 3D Fourier Neural Operator that alternates spectral convolution
layers with pointwise channel mixing. Spectral convolutions operate jointly over the two spatial
dimensions and time, allowing the model to capture long-range interactions efficiently in Fourier
space. All FNO models use four Fourier layers. For Controlled Cylinder and Foil, we use width 64
and retained Fourier modes (4, 12, 16), resulting in 50.36M parameters. For Combustion, we retain
(4, 16, 16) modes with width 64, resulting in 67.14M parameters. For FSI, we use width 128 and
(4, 16, 16) modes, resulting in 268.52M parameters.
DPOT-S (Hao et al., 2024). DPOT-S is a discriminative pretrained operator transformer. It tokenizes
spatiotemporal fields into 8×8 patches, maps them into a 1024-dimensional latent space, and applies
six operator-Transformer layers with eight internal mixing blocks. Global token interactions and
spectral/operator mixing enable the model to capture non-local physical dependencies. DPOT-S
contains 30.80M parameters.
Transolver (Wu et al., 2024). We use Transolver as a coordinate-aware Transformer baseline. The
model represents each point in the spatiotemporal mesh using its (𝑥, 𝑦, 𝑡) coordinate and associated
15


physical variables, and models their interactions through slice-based physics attention. Our config-
uration contains six Transformer layers, hidden dimension 256, eight attention heads, 32 slices, and
reference resolution parameter 4. The model directly maps the complete 10-frame input block to the
complete 10-frame output block. It contains approximately 42.59M parameters.
Refiner architecture. The refiner adopts a time-conditioned 2D U-Net that jointly predicts residuals
for all ten future frames. For datasets with three physical variables, the residual trajectory is repre-
sented by 30 channels, corresponding to ten frames and three variables. The refiner is conditioned
on the concatenation of the deterministic prediction of the ten-frames and the observation history of
the ten-frames, resulting in 60 conditioning channels. The U-Net has a base width of 64 and consists
of a 256-dimensional sinusoidal time embedding, two downsampling stages, two bottleneck residual
blocks, two upsampling stages, and encoder–decoder skip connections. Its six time-conditioned
residual blocks each employ Group Normalization, SiLU activations, two 3 × 3 convolutions, and
a time-dependent affine projection. The resulting refiner contains 5,03M trainable parameters on
standard three-variable datasets and 5,05M parameters on Controlled Cylinder, whose observation
history includes two additional control channels.
