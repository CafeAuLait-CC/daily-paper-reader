Abstract
Language-conditioned humanoid motion generation enables users to create and
refine robot movements through natural language. The central challenge is to con-
nect an underspecified request to concrete motion structure that can subsequently
be revised relative to an accepted reference. We present TRACE, a generation and
editing framework for the Unitree G1 humanoid robot built around typed, mea-
surable kinematic quantities. Our insight is to ground generation conditions in
paired training motions and editing goals in the current motion. A language model
learns to predict support, root, and hand events from motion-derived supervision;
their time-aligned encodings complement the original text in a diffusion generator.
Gravity-referenced states and trajectory–contact objectives improve kinematic con-
sistency. For editing, typed requests define body–time scopes and source-relative
goals. A separate reference-conditioned diffusion editor uses these goals and re-
vised events to realize changes, while sampling-time projection preserves protected
state coordinates. Quantitative evaluation and human assessment show improved
semantic alignment and motion quality over the compared methods. Controlled ab-
lations support the value of motion-grounded conditioning and kinematic consis-
tency, while multi-turn editing evaluations demonstrate more reliable execution of
successive relative requests under shared preservation constraints. Together, these
results support typed kinematic structure as a common basis for motion generation
and reference-aware revision.

Introduction
Language-conditioned humanoid motion generation maps a natural-language instruction to a se-
quence of root poses and joint configurations. It lets users specify behavior such as walking, dancing,
or jumping without authoring each pose. The resulting trajectory can serve as an animation or a ref-
erence for a downstream robot controller. In interactive use, generation also provides a starting point
for refinement: after seeing a motion, a user may ask for a higher jump or a different hand position
while expecting the accepted parts of the sequence to remain recognizable.
Existing text-to-motion models learn mappings from captions to continuous trajectories or discrete
motion codes (Tevet et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023a; Jiang et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2024). Structured
approaches expose body-part or temporal conditions (Zhang et al., 2023b; Yazdian et al., 2025), and
language-model planning decomposes instructions into detailed descriptions (Ouyang et al., 2026).

These intermediate conditions provide a way to specify motion structure, but a caption alone rarely
determines exactly when a foot supports the body or how high a hand rises. A plan inferred solely
from that caption can describe a valid realization different from its paired training motion. Grounding
the plan in measurable properties of the demonstration gives supervision a direct connection to the
trajectory the generator learns to reconstruct.
Editing makes this connection to a particular motion equally important. Source-conditioned methods
learn revisions from language and an existing sequence (Athanasiou et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Lin
et al., 2026). A relative request such as “raise the hand” must be interpreted against the current pose
and trajectory, while the rest of the motion supplies a reference to preserve. An interactive system
therefore needs both an explicit description of the requested change and a mechanism that restricts
its effect to the selected body region and time interval.
The key challenge is to turn underspecified language into a coherent motion reference whose intended
properties can be revised selectively. Figure 1 illustrates this requirement. An initial instruction pro-
duces a jumping jack; the follow-up “Jump higher, rest unchanged” refers to that particular motion.
The edited example crouches deeper and jumps higher, requiring coordinated changes in preparation
and take-off while retaining the overall action. A useful intermediate description should therefore
expose quantities such as support and height over time. Our insight is that these measurable quan-
tities can connect both stages: the paired demonstration determines the event targets used to train
generation, and the current reference determines the baseline and protected coordinates for editing.
We introduce Typed Reference-Aware Conversational Editing (TRACE) for the Unitree G1 humanoid
robot. Typed refers to explicit categories for support, root, and hand events and for requested changes.
Reference-aware means that the current motion defines both the baseline for a relative change and
the coordinates to preserve. For generation, we extract event targets from training motions through
forward kinematics and supervise an LLM to predict duration and event intervals from text. The pre-
dicted Plan is converted into numerical conditions aligned with motion patches. A diffusion Trans-
former combines these conditions with the original caption, retaining language information beyond
the compact event vocabulary. A gravity-referenced state directly represents height and tilt, while
integrated-trajectory and support-selective contact objectives constrain motion consistency.
For editing, a language request is parsed into a typed change and compiled into a body–time scope
and a source-relative goal. A separately trained diffusion editor receives the current motion, its re-
vised events, and the compiled goal. At every sampling step, reference projection restores protected
coordinates, allowing the selected region to change while retaining the rest of the encoded motion.
Each output supplies the reference for the next request. Generation and editing thus share a motion
representation and a kinematic vocabulary, with separate models for their respective conditioning
tasks.
Experiments on BONES-SEED (Bones Studio, n.d.) evaluate generation, kinematic consistency, and
successive request execution. TRACE leads the compared methods in retrieval, MM-Dist, FID, and
human ratings (Section 4.2). Matched controls establish the value of event conditioning and motion-
derived auxiliary supervision, while representation and objective ablations measure their effects on
trajectory and contact errors (Section 4.3). For editing, hand-height requests provide measurable
source-relative goals; TRACE achieves higher all-turn success than the adapted learned editors under
shared scopes and reference projection (Section 5.3). Our contributions are:
1. Motion-grounded typed-event conditioning. We derive confidence-weighted support,
root, and hand event targets from training trajectories and learn a text-to-Plan predictor.
Patch-aligned numerical events complement raw-caption features in continuous G1 mo-
tion generation. Matched controls demonstrate gains from event conditioning and motion-
derived auxiliary targets.
2. Gravity-referenced motion diffusion. We directly represent absolute height and gravity-
relative tilt, and combine integrated-trajectory objectives with support-selective forward-
kinematics penalties to reduce accumulated trajectory errors and improve foot–ground con-
sistency. Representation and objective ablations quantify these effects through kinematic
errors.
3. Typed, source-relative motion editing. We compile supported editing requests into body–
time scopes and source-relative goals, and combine them with source-motion features and
revised events in a separately trained editor. Reference projection preserves protected state

Related Work
Motion-grounded planning and conditioning.
Text-to-motion models generate discrete codes
(Zhang et al., 2023a; Jiang et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2024) or continuous trajectories (Tevet et al., 2023).
Structured representations add finer control: MotionScript converts motion into detailed language
(Yazdian et al., 2025), and FineMoGen models body-part and temporal composition (Zhang et al.,
2023b). Motion-R1 synthesizes decomposed reasoning plans from descriptions and refines genera-
tion with RL (Ouyang et al., 2026). For supervised motion generation, the intermediate conditions
also need to agree with the specific demonstration paired with the caption. TRACE addresses this
alignment through confidence-weighted support, root, and hand events extracted from that training
motion. A text-conditioned planner predicts these events at inference, and their numerical sequence
supplements raw-caption features with event content and temporal alignment.
Kinematic consistency and humanoid generation.
MDM incorporates geometric and foot-
contact objectives (Tevet et al., 2023). PhysDiff adds simulator-based motion projection during de-
noising (Yuan et al., 2023), while CLoSD closes the loop between a diffusion planner and a tracking
controller (Tevet et al., 2025). Language-conditioned humanoid systems connect generation to robot
motion or control (Jiang et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Jian et al., 2026); imitation
and transfer methods address downstream tracking (Fu et al., 2024; He et al., 2024; 2025). These
methods address motion plausibility and downstream execution at different stages. TRACE targets
upstream reference quality through gravity-referenced height and tilt, integrated trajectory objectives,
and support-selective FK contact losses.
Typed source-relative and reference-preserving editing.
MDM demonstrates temporal and
body-part inpainting (Tevet et al., 2023), and PriorMDM composes diffusion priors for joint and tra-
jectory control (Shafir et al., 2024). FineMoGen also uses LLMs for fine-grained editing (Zhang et al.,
2023b). MotionFix introduces source–instruction–target pairs and the source-conditioned TMED
editor (Athanasiou et al., 2024); SimMotionEdit adds motion-similarity supervision (Li et al., 2025),
and CIME studies spatial preservation and non-uniform temporal mapping (Lin et al., 2026). TRACE
connects source conditioning and preservation to explicit relative requests: a typed relative change
is compiled into a kinematic body–time scope and coupled to source-relative goals, revised source
events, and reference projection.

Method
3.1
Overview
Typed event plans structure generation; typed edit requests specify how an accepted motion should
change. Reference-conditioned denoising realizes the change within its compiled scope, while pro-
jection preserves protected coordinates. TRACE uses separate generator and editor weights; Figure 2
depicts the generation pathway.
For an instruction u, a frozen text encoder gives e = f(u), while a supervised LLM jointly predicts
duration and typed event intervals. Deterministic rasterization converts its structured output into the
plan:
P = (N, C) = Rasterize(LLMϕ(u)),
C ∈[0, 1]L×16,
L = ⌈N/4⌉.
(1)
The LLM predicts support, root, and hand events and is frozen during diffusion training and infer-
ence. We denote its rasterized events at length N by h(u, N). A conditional diffusion Transformer
generates continuous G1 states from (e, P).
3.2
Motion-Grounded Sparse Planning and Conditioning
We construct a compact event vocabulary from measurable properties of the paired training motion.
These motion-derived targets provide realization-aligned supervision for the language-to-plan pre-
dictor. At inference, predicted events supply explicit support, root, and hand structure, while the
original caption retains semantics outside this vocabulary.
Motion-derived supervision.
For each clean training motion, deterministic FK and root recon-
struction produce sole heights/speeds, root vertical motion and height, and palm heights relative to
the pelvis. Fixed thresholds convert these signals into typed events. Each four-frame patch carries
two independent support probabilities and four categorical distributions:

Time-aligned conditioning.
The raw caption is encoded once. A learned numerical event pro-
jection and a separate raw-text residual branch use patch progress features to align conditions to the
denoiser. Both supplement the raw-text backbone. At diffusion timestep k, the residual condition
is scaled by α(k) = min(1, k/[0.25(K −1)]), attenuating it near clean motion. Matched controls
retain the same branch capacity, so removing plan means zeroing its event inputs while preserving
all trainable parameters.
3.3
Gravity-Gauged and Contact-Consistent Diffusion
The plan specifies motion structure, but accurate local poses alone do not ensure a coherent trajectory.
Small errors in integrated root motion can accumulate, and plausible poses can still move a supporting
foot. We therefore represent height and tilt relative to gravity and supervise integrated geometry and
foot contact explicitly. This separates quantities that require an absolute reference from those that
describe displacement.

Grouped training objective.

Motion reconstruction supervises states, velocities, and accelerations. Geometry consistency con-

consistency uses differentiable FK to penalize support-foot sliding, drift, and height error, plus pene-
tration on all valid frames. Clean training motion selects support masks; predicted plan probabilities
are conditions, not substitutes for these masks. Each group includes its component weights, detailed
in Appendix C.2.
3.4
Automatic Masking and Reference-Aware Motion Editing
Editing requires both locating the requested change and preserving the remaining motion. We com-
bine automatic body–time masking, source-relative goals, and reference projection to address these
complementary requirements.

Automatic scope localization.
At turn t, Xt = (Mt, ut) contains the source and request; Nt is the
source length. A supervised LLM, frozen at inference, predicts At = δt = πϕ(ut, Nt, s(Mt)): an
attribute, effector, signed change, and interval. The source-only summary s(Mt) contains FK hand
heights relative to the pelvis. A deterministic compiler constructs (St, Dt) = g(At, ut, Nt). For
the body–time mask St, it expands the selected effector et to its kinematic chain K(et) and resolves
interval [at, bt) against Nt:
St(f, j) = 1[j ∈K(et)] 1[at ≤f < bt].
(6)
For hand-height edits, K contains seven arm-joint coordinates; its complement remains protected,
including that arm outside the interval. Explicit time expressions are compiled from language; invalid
requests leave the mask closed. Localization uses only the source and request. The grammar is
detailed in Appendix D.2.
Source-relative motion revision.
For controlled hand-height evaluation, Dt ∈RNt×2 encodes
signed wrist-height offsets with a sine-squared temporal envelope. The editor conditions on source-
motion features, the scope, these offsets, and revised source events. The event sequence is extracted
deterministically from the current source motion, with the requested hand-height groups updated
according to Dt. Original and synthetic edit pairs supervise local motion fidelity, relative goals,
and event consistency. The wrist-offset protocol evaluates hand edits; Figure 1 provides a separate
qualitative jump refinement. Appendix D.2 defines request compilation, and Appendix D.3 details
the editor and its training.

Experimental Setup
Data.
We use the processed BONES-SEED test split of 13,302 text–motion pairs. Each UID con-

Processing, split integrity, and reassembly are detailed in Appendix E.1.
Baselines.
We distinguish two groups.
Human-motion methods adapted to G1 comprise
MDM (Tevet et al., 2023), MoMask (Guo et al., 2024), and MotionGPT3 (Zhu et al., 2026), retrained
on the processed BONES-SEED/G1 split with heading-local 38D outputs. Native G1 methods com-

All rows use the same frozen G1 text–motion evaluator for 32-candidate R-Precision,
MM-Dist, FID, and Diversity. Diversity is a distributional diagnostic, not a ranked objective. Physical
errors are assessed only in matched component controls. Table captions identify the uncertainty

Implementation.
TRACE generates 40D G1 states at 20 FPS with four-frame patches. The diffu-
sion Transformer has eight layers, width 448, and eight heads. Frozen Qwen3-Embedding-4B sup-
plies caption features; a Qwen3-4B-Instruct adapter jointly predicts duration and all event groups.
The main-table generator continues a 32k-step raw-text model for 8k steps with the 16-event branch
and frozen LLM predictions. Test inference uses predicted conditions, EMA, DDIM-50, and CFG
2.5. Appendix E.2 records the full training and sampling configuration and batch sizes.

4.3
Ablation Study
Motion-Grounded Plan Ablation.
Matched controls support typed, time-aligned Plan condition-
ing and supervision grounded in the paired motion (Figure 4). Aligned Plan uses the main-table
model; Text Only retains the same raw-caption features, branch capacity, duration predictor, initial-
ization, objectives, and continuation budget while zeroing the event inputs. A second comparison
replaces motion-derived auxiliary targets with caption-derived events during training and retains the
same predicted Plan at test time.
The retrieval and distributional improvements are consistent with two roles of the Plan: temporal
conditioning exposes when support and height events occur, and motion-derived supervision aligns
auxiliary targets with the trajectory being reconstructed. A caption-derived Plan can describe another
valid realization. Test conditions are predicted from text throughout. Appendix B.2 provides the
Global Events control, absolute values, and full protocols.
Kinematic Design Ablation.
The kinematic controls show complementary effects of integrated-
trajectory supervision and foot–ground constraints (Figure 5). Heading-local, Gravity-Gauged, Ge-
ometry, and Contact models share the training budget, data, and event conditions. Gravity-Gauged
reduces contact-height error, consistent with predicting absolute height and tilt directly. Geome-
try further improves trajectory-related diagnostics but increases penetration; adding Contact reduces
penetration and yields lower errors than Heading-local across all reported measures.

5.1
Experimental Setup
Data and tasks.
We use hand-height editing as a controlled test of source-relative request execu-
tion. A requested height change defines a measurable FK goal while allowing multiple valid joint
trajectories, so evaluation can assess goal achievement and preservation without requiring a unique
8


target motion. Within this protocol, the signed offset follows a fixed sine-squared profile over the
requested interval, using the benchmark’s temporal convention.
We use 199 held-out BONES-SEED/G1 source motions with human-reviewed synthetic English edit-
ing requests. Each initial request specifies a hand, a signed 5 cm height change, and an explicit time
interval. We first evaluate the predicted body–time mask without generating motion. For sequential
editing, each source yields three scripted four-turn patterns: same-arm edits, alternating arms, and
reversals, giving 597 chains.
Models and controls.
For motion editing, we compare the complete TRACE editor with local G1
adaptations of TMED (Athanasiou et al., 2024) and SimMotionEdit (Li et al., 2025) under a shared
scope-compilation and reference-projection protocol.
Metrics.
Mask localization uses body–time IoU against private reference masks. All-turn success
requires every request through a depth to meet change and preservation criteria relative to its actual
preceding output (Appendix E.4).

The full pipeline matches the prescribed masks on all

Multi-turn editing under shared preservation constraints.
TRACE achieves the highest all-turn

Conclusion and Limitations
TRACE combines motion-grounded typed-event conditioning, gravity-referenced diffusion, and
source-relative editing to generate and revise Unitree G1 motion. Generation comparisons show
9


strong semantic alignment and perceptual quality; matched controls support temporal event condi-
tioning, motion-grounded supervision, and kinematic consistency. Under shared scopes and refer-
ence projection, TRACE sustains successive hand-height changes more reliably than the adapted
learned editors. Typed motion quantities thus connect language-conditioned generation with mea-
surable revisions of an evolving reference.
Scope and limitations.
The system addresses text instructions and flat-ground kinematics without
scene perception. Retrieval and distributional metrics assess generated motions, not exact adherence
to each predicted event. Contact objectives encourage foot–ground consistency without guaranteeing
zero slip.

D.1
Language Interfaces and Qualitative Whole-Body Example
The two language adapters share the instruction-tuned backbone reported in Section 4.1, but are
trained and serialized independently because generation and editing expose different output schemas.
Both adapters are frozen when their respective diffusion models are trained or sampled.
Adapter interfaces.
The generation adapter receives a standalone instruction and emits a duration
plus a sequence of typed event intervals. Each interval records its start and end, left/right support,
root vertical state, root-height state, and left/right hand-height states. Deterministic validation clips
interval endpoints to the predicted duration, resolves overlaps by output order, fills uncovered regions
with unknown states, and rasterizes the result onto the four-frame Plan grid. The original instruction
is encoded separately alongside the serialized Plan.
The editing adapter receives the follow-up instruction together with the duration and a source-only
kinematic summary. It emits an attribute, effector, signed change, and temporal interval. A determin-
istic compiler maps this record to a body–time scope, a source-relative target when the attribute has
a metric interpretation, and revised source-event channels when the request changes an event-level
property. Invalid or incomplete records produce a closed scope and leave the source unchanged.

E.1
BONES-SEED Data Processing
Source and scope.
We use BONES-SEED’s supplied G1 retargeting and captions (Bones Studio,
n.d.). Its 142,220 metadata entries include original and mirrored motions. We select a predominantly
ground-supported subset and construct study-specific grouped splits.
Selection and filtering.
Requiring body-position standing and temporarily excluding mirrors
gives 61,292 originals. We reject malformed paths, Stunts, and metadata indicating non-standing
or externally supported motion. At an intermediate 50 FPS, a kinematic screen rejects clips whose
two lowest foot-collision points stay above world ground by 5 cm for at least one second; sampled
rejection candidates are checked frame by frame. This leaves 58,574 originals.
A semantic filter removes 3,115 originals (1,969 keyword rules; 1,146 Qwen-Max classifications),
leaving 55,459. It excludes difficult acrobatics and externally supported actions, retaining walking,
running, turns, gestures, squats, daily dance, and small jumps. Persisted filtering decisions are reused;
semantic filtering is separate from deterministic Plan annotation.
Mirrors and captions.
Restoring 55,423 supplied mirrors gives 110,882 clips; 36 originals re-
main unpaired. No mirrors or captions are synthesized. We retain four natural, one technical, and
two short-description fields, plus filename-matched temporal annotations. Mirror annotations come
from their own records without left/right substitution; clip-level captions may still be shared. Af-
ter whitespace/case-insensitive deduplication, the generator takes the first nonempty caption in this
order; semantic evaluation uses the corresponding first natural description.
Grouped split and leakage checks.
We connect records sharing a motion family, its mirror-
canonical family, an original/mirror pair, or a normalized exact caption. Caption matching uses all
available English alternatives and an existing locally translated Chinese copy of the same metadata.
Text normalization uses Unicode normalization, case folding, and punctuation/whitespace cleanup.
Connected components are kept intact when assigning train/validation/test with seed 20260822 and
target proportions 76/12/12, balancing clips and frames. Adding mirrors rebuilds the assignment and
produces 2,557 final components. The recorded audit finds zero cross-split overlap for paths, exact
and mirror-canonical families, caption keys, components, and mirror pairs. This is not an actor-
disjoint split or a guarantee that every semantic paraphrase is disjoint; generic temporal annotation
phrases are not grouping keys.

to letters are randomly permuted once with a fixed seed; the resulting order is shared across par-
ticipants. Videos appear together with the prompt in a grid, using a common G1 rendering setup,
background, and camera configuration, without method-name overlays. Participants can pause and
replay the clips and rate every video on two separate criteria: motion Quality and Text match.
The displayed three-point anchors are: 1 (poor), clearly unnatural or discontinuous motion with
severe visible artifacts, or a mismatch with the main action or intent; 2 (fair), broadly plausible
motion with local coordination defects or mild artifacts, or a broadly correct action with inaccurate
details, direction, amplitude, or timing; and 3 (good), natural, continuous, coordinated motion with
few visible artifacts, or accurate expression of the principal action, direction, and intent. The two
criteria are scored independently. Score definitions remain visible during rating, and all required
ratings must be supplied to complete an item. Overall is the equal-weight mean of the two criteria.
We first average each participant’s ratings for a method, then report the mean and sample SD across
participants.
