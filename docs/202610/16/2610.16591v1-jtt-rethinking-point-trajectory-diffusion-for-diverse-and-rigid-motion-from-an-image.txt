ABSTRACT
We study forecasting rigid-body motion from a single image: generating diverse
and physically plausible future trajectories for arbitrary points in a scene, without
building rigidity into the architecture or relying on geometric inputs such as depth.
Like video generators, recent trajectory generators fail at this task, even in simple
synthetic Kubric scenes, despite scaling up model size and training on real data.
We show how to solve this with the right architecture and parameterization. Our
model, JTT, is a simple diffusion transformer that operates directly in coordinate
space and decodes arbitrary and dense query patterns, without the trajectory VAEs
or fixed grids assumed in prior work. Through a systematic study of the design
space of point trajectory diffusion, we find that diffusing per-frame velocities is
the only parameterization that captures rigidity, that it must be paired with the
right timestep schedule, and that adding full-resolution RGB at the query points
resolves the boundary failures of prior methods. However, evaluating such 2D
models rigorously is itself non-trivial. To our knowledge, we are the first to observe
that physical plausibility and diversity do not improve together during training:
larger models, as used in prior work, memorize the single ground-truth future per
training image, becoming more rigid while covering fewer possible futures. Yet
prior work has paid little attention to this, so we show how model rankings depend
on model size, metric, and stopping time, and adopt a stricter, checkpoint-averaged
evaluation protocol. Under this protocol, JTT improves over prior methods on both
distributional and rigidity metrics and approaches ground-truth rigidity, with only
about 50M parameters.

INTRODUCTION
We study the problem of forecasting the motion of rigid objects from a single image. These predictors
are increasingly used in robot manipulation to generate target motions for policies to execute,
represented either as videos (Ko et al., 2023; Bharadhwaj et al., 2024a; Patel et al., 2025) or as
point trajectories (Wen et al., 2024; Bharadhwaj et al., 2024b; Xu et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2024).
Motion forecasting is also used in model-based control (Yang et al., 2025; Mazzaglia et al., 2024)
and controllable video generation (Polyak et al., 2024; Wan et al., 2025; Brooks et al., 2024).
Since an image does not fully determine the state of the scene, many futures are possible, and a useful
predictor must capture their distribution rather than a single realization. The goal is to make such
realizations physically plausible. Unfortunately, even the best generative forecasting models struggle
with physical plausibility. Video generators often violate object permanence and basic physical
laws (Kang et al., 2025; Motamed et al., 2025; Bansal et al., 2025; 2026); in particular, they may fail
to predict rigid motion even when fine-tuned for forecasting in simple synthetic scenes (Boduljak
et al., 2026). Some authors simply assume that objects are rigid and only predict rigid motion (Ko
et al., 2023; Bharadhwaj et al., 2024b; Patel et al., 2025), but this is not general.
Using 2D point trajectories as the representation for motion forecasting has several advantages: it is
general, it guarantees object permanence and temporal coherence by construction, it avoids the need
to recover 2D motion from generated pixels (Ko et al., 2023; Patel et al., 2025), and it yields more
rigid motion than videos under an identical architecture (Boduljak et al., 2026). Furthermore, as for
video models, large quantities of real-world training data can be obtained by using reliable 2D point
trackers (Doersch et al., 2023; 2024; Karaev et al., 2025) for pseudo-labeling videos (Bharadhwaj
et al., 2024b; Zhang et al., 2026b; Thakkar et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026).
However, point trajectories alone do not guarantee plausible motion: even on the synthetic Kubric-A
scenes, which contain only a few rigid objects, no prior trajectory generator achieves exact rigidity
(Table 2). Instead, they trade off plausibility and diversity: MotionForecasting (Thakkar et al., 2026)
is the most rigid but has the worst distributional fidelity, whereas WHN (Boduljak et al., 2026) is
the opposite. They also often miss small objects and object parts and generate jittery backgrounds
(Fig. 4).
In this paper, we study in depth how to learn to predict diverse and plausible 2D point trajectories from
single images. We deliberately avoid injecting hard inductive biases. We also avoid using additional
observations of the scene, such as depth. We consider this to be a particularly interesting case for two
reasons. First, it is a hard setting for the model: without additional observations, the distribution of
possible futures is highly multimodal and difficult to capture even using diffusion (Lavoie et al., 2025).
Second, 2D pseudo-labels can be obtained with reasonable accuracy by modern trackers (Doersch
et al., 2023; 2024; Karaev et al., 2025), but 3D pseudo-labels remain much less accurate (Koppula
et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2024; 2025) and harder to get, which makes our case of practical interest.
We are not the first to consider this an interesting setting. However, we have identified two gaps in
current best practices that we believe are limiting progress in this area. The first is a modeling gap
and the second is an evaluation gap. Our contribution is to characterize and address them.
On the modeling gap, existing trajectory generators (Table 1) compromise on at least one of three
quality axes: (i) physical plausibility, respecting physical invariants such as the rigidity of solid
objects; (ii) query flexibility, querying any number of points at arbitrary 2D locations at inference; and
(iii) query density, predicting motion everywhere in the image rather than at a sparse, fixed set of points.
The default approach to improving quality is to scale the model and training data, or to introduce
latent RGB and video representations, or custom VAE losses and rollout fine-tuning (Boduljak et al.,
2026; Zhang et al., 2026b). Here we take a step back and ask whether a simple but well-designed
diffusion model that operates directly in trajectory (coordinate) space can do better. We show it
can, given four design choices that prior works treat inconsistently: (1) what to diffuse: absolute
coordinates, offsets relative to the query, or velocities; (2) how to inject visual information: sampled
image features or full-resolution RGB; (3) which timestep schedule to use for flow matching; and (4)
which inductive biases help, in particular implicit (e.g., reconstruction model features) or explicit
(e.g., world coordinates) 3D geometry. Most importantly, diffusing per-frame velocities is the only
parameterization that captures rigidity, and adding full-resolution RGB at the query points resolves
the boundary failures of prior methods, which use low-resolution image features (Fig. 4).

Building on these findings, we introduce the Just trajectory Transformer (JTT; Fig. 2), which predicts
the per-frame velocities of arbitrary query points in an input frame. A small support set of query
tokens does the heavy lifting, attending to each other and to multi-layer image features, while all
other non-support queries decode their trajectories cheaply by cross-attending the support set. Unlike
Perceiver IO (Jaegle et al., 2021; 2022), D4RT (Zhang et al., 2026a), and CoTracker3 (Karaev et al.,
2025), which use latent or support tokens for tracking, we use them in a generative model and
randomly re-partition the queries at every training step, so that a single model, trained with 1 to 1024
query points, decodes arbitrary and dense query patterns without retraining (Fig. 1) and lets users
trade compute for accuracy. JTT requires no VAE, auxiliary losses, rollout fine-tuning, or pre-trained
video latents; with only about 50M parameters, it outperforms prior methods on FVMD and rigidity.
On the evaluation gap, we note that assessment in prior works is often unreliable, partly due to
reporting a single checkpoint when model ranking can change substantially from one to the next,
and partly due to only reporting results on real videos, which, each providing a single realization,
make it difficult to measure distributional coverage. In general, rigorous evaluation is non-trivial. For
instance, rigidity cannot be measured directly from 2D point trajectories and must be assessed via
proxy metrics. Furthermore, there is a clear trade-off between physical plausibility and distributional
coverage.
By studying the training dynamics of trajectory generators in a controlled setting, to our knowledge
for the first time, we find that larger models converge faster but then memorize the single ground-truth
future per training image, becoming more rigid while covering fewer possible futures (Fig. 3), so that
which model appears best depends on model size, metric, and stopping time. We therefore evaluate
with a checkpoint-averaged protocol on the Kubric-A benchmark of Boduljak et al. (2026), whose
multiple ground-truth futures per image enable rigorous evaluation of stochastic predictors; on real
videos, a model that memorizes the single observed future (or predicts the implausible average) would
even appear best.
We summarize our contributions as follows: (1) We introduce JTT, a transformer architecture and
diffusion parameterization that learns the 2D motion of rigid bodies with neither rigidity built into
the architecture nor extra geometric inputs, while decoding arbitrary and dense query patterns. It
improves over prior methods on distributional and rigidity metrics, approaching ground-truth rigidity,
while using a smaller model and simpler design than many prior works. (2) We systematically
study the design space of point trajectory diffusion and show that diffusing per-frame velocities is
the only parameterization that captures rigidity, that it depends on the right timestep schedule, and
that full-resolution RGB at the query points resolves boundary failures; these choices should take
precedence over more complex model architectures, such as introducing trajectory VAEs. (3) To our
knowledge, we are the first to show that physical plausibility and diversity do not improve together
during the training of point trajectory generators: larger models converge faster but then memorize
the single ground-truth future per training image, improving rigidity while degrading distributional
fidelity, so that the ranking of models depends on model size and on the evaluated checkpoint. We
therefore adopt a checkpoint-averaged protocol for rigorous evaluation. (4) We show that explicit
oracle geometry, such as inverse depth at query points or generation in world coordinates, improves
performance, but, in terms of rigidity, our 2D-only model closes most of the gap between prior 2D
methods and the 3D oracle, verifying our design; integrating 3D reconstruction models is promising
future work.

RELATED WORK
Visual trajectory forecasting. Visual trajectory forecasting has progressed quickly, but existing
methods have limitations that our work addresses. Track2Act (Bharadhwaj et al., 2024b) is a
600M-parameter diffusion model that predicts absolute coordinates for arbitrary query points, but
it conditions on a single ResNet feature vector, which limits spatial resolution and causes it to
hallucinate objects and miss small ones. WHN (Boduljak et al., 2026) runs latent rectified flow over
a fixed 32×32 grid via a VAE with patch-level DINO conditioning, improving diversity and showing
the importance of global reasoning over large point sets. However, the fixed grid prevents arbitrary
query patterns, the VAE increases training complexity, and jitter persists in generated samples, even
on Kubric-A, with degraded accuracy near boundaries. TrajLoom (Zhang et al., 2026b) extends
WHN’s latent flow matching to video-conditioned generation at a larger scale. It diffuses VAE

any VAE. Second, we condition on full-resolution RGB and multi-layer DINO features rather than
low-resolution global features or RGB-video VAE latents. Third, we decouple the query distribution
from the architecture via the support/non-support partition, so the same model can predict with
arbitrary configurations of query points, including dense query patterns (Fig. 1).
Architectures supporting arbitrary configurations of query points. An important property of our
design is support for arbitrary query configurations. Our design is inspired by Perceiver (Jaegle et al.,
2021) and Perceiver IO (Jaegle et al., 2022), which introduce the idea of using a latent representation
of fixed size paired with an arbitrary collection of output queries to read it out. This decouples the
size of model inputs, internal state, and outputs; D4RT (Zhang et al., 2026a) applies this idea to
dynamic 4D reconstruction with randomly resampled read-write tokens. Closer to our domain, in the
context of 2D tracking, CoTracker3 (Karaev et al., 2025) (and its predecessor (Karaev et al., 2024a))
use a small set of support tracks that interact through self-attention and a large set of non-support
tracks that read from them. Our support/non-support decomposition (Section 3.3) uses a similar idea
for generating instead of estimating tracks. Unlike in prior work, the partition is randomized at every
training step to keep the model general. Thus, a single model trained with 1 to 1,024 points per scene
generalizes to dense query patterns at inference time.

METHOD
We study the problem of visual trajectory forecasting. Formally, a trajectory is a sequence x =
(x0, . . . , xT ) ∈R(T +1)×2 of 2D points xt = (ut, vt) ∈R2 that starts at a query location q ≡(u0, v0)
(i.e., x0 = q). The model is given as input an image I ∈RH×W ×3 as well as a collection of N
query points Q = {qn}N
n=1. The goal of the model is to sample corresponding trajectories from the
conditional distribution p({xn}N
n=1 | I, Q). Our problem formulation is more flexible than some
prior works; in particular, compared to Boduljak et al. (2026), the set of query points Q is supplied at
inference rather than being fixed. On the other hand, unlike Bharadhwaj et al. (2024b), the model is
only given the image I as evidence; it is not given a goal frame, segmentation, or correspondences.
We introduce a new model, JTT, to solve this problem. Our design, illustrated in Fig. 2, is (i)
generative, sampling multiple distinct futures per scene; (ii) flexible, accepting an arbitrary number
of query points at arbitrary 2D locations; and (iii) dense, scaling to thousands of points per scene on a
single GPU. We take a deliberately minimal approach and train a single diffusion transformer that
operates directly in coordinate space. In particular, we use no VAEs, auxiliary losses, or pre-trained
video latents. While the design is simple, we find numerous choices critical to achieving good
performance. We discuss these next.

3.1
TRAJECTORY PARAMETERIZATION
We parameterize each trajectory as the sequence of instantaneous velocities ∆t ≡xt −xt−1 ∈R2,
t = 1, . . . , T and target those for generation using denoising diffusion. We isotropically normalize
the velocities across the dataset so the diffusion target has roughly unit variance. The full trajectory
for query q is recovered at inference as xt = q + Pt
i=1 ∆i. Compared with absolute coordinates
xt (Track2Act, WHN), which couple the diffusion target to the query location, and grid-cell offsets
xt −xc (TrajLoom), which assume a fixed inference grid, per-frame velocities are translation-
equivariant in the query, place the target on a near-zero, dataset-normalized scale, and decouple the
trajectory from the absolute pixel grid. Empirically, we found that this helps the model learn the
motion of rigid objects under arbitrary query patterns.
3.2
IMAGE CONDITIONING
We patchify the image I into a regular P × P grid and form one token per patch by concatenating
(i) the 2D pixel center (u, v) ∈R2, (ii) the RGB patch itself (stacked to a 3P 2 vector), and (iii)
DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2023) features extracted from multiple transformer layers l ∈{3, 6, 9, 12},
each contributing a d-dimensional descriptor, analogous to the multi-layer aggregation in DPT (Ranftl
et al., 2021). A single linear layer maps these vectors to a fixed hidden dimension, yielding P 2
image tokens that the trajectory tokens cross-attend to. We found that including full-resolution
RGB patches instead of a single global vector (Track2Act) or only a low-resolution feature grid
(MotionForecasting) works better, particularly at image boundaries, and reduces hallucinated and
missed objects that sometimes affect prior work. We also observed that multi-layer DINO features
perform better than relying solely on the final layer.
3.3
ARCHITECTURE
The JTT model (Fig. 2) is a diffusion transformer that takes as input the trajectory velocity ∆τ at a
noise level τ, the image I, and the query set Q, and outputs a denoising signal ˆvθ(∆τ, I, Q, τ) (see

Section 3.4). Hence, the transformer must process the velocities ∆encoded by the query tokens as
well as the input image I and noise level τ.
The bulk of the computation is taken up by the query tokens, for which we use a Perceiver-IO-
style (Jaegle et al., 2021; 2022) encoder-decoder. The idea is to split the N query points Q into a
support set of S tokens (cyan in the figure) and a non-support set of M = N −S tokens (pink).
Support tokens attend to the image features and reason globally; non-support tokens are decoded
cheaply by reading information off the support set in parallel. By comparison, self-attention across
all query tokens would scale quadratically in their number N, which would make dense prediction
(e.g., using a 256 × 256 grid of queries) all but intractable. CoTracker3 (Karaev et al., 2025)
and D4RT (Zhang et al., 2026a) sidestep this problem by using a latent representation of fixed
dimensionality, which acts as a bottleneck. We retain that efficiency, but make the support set fully
flexible. Users can specify any set of queries Q and still control the compute budget by choosing
the size S ≪N of the support set, without having to retrain the model. The diffusion timestep τ
modulates layer norms via AdaLN (Peebles & Xie, 2023) in every block.

constant diffusion velocity v = ∆1 −∆0 (not to be confused with the trajectory velocity). The model

objectives. At inference, we draw ∆0 ∼N(0, I) and solve the SDE with the learned drift via 1000
Euler–Maruyama steps, then accumulate the predicted velocities. We use this sampler because it
gives the best rigidity (LRTL); a 10-step Euler ODE sampler achieves similar rigidity and even better
scores on the other metrics (Table 8). Details are in Section C.

EXPERIMENTS
We begin by studying the interplay between model size, sample diversity, and physical plausibility
during training, which determines how models should be selected and compared. We then show that
JTT outperforms prior work, explain step by step which design choices lead to this performance, and
finally compare it with oracles given privileged 3D geometry.
Data. We run most experiments on Kubric-A, strictly following the protocol of WHN (Boduljak
et al., 2026): sequences of T = 24 frames rendered with the Kubric simulator (Greff et al., 2022),
with multiple ground-truth futures per initial condition obtained by re-simulating the physics under
different initial velocities. As established in WHN, this setup is necessary for rigorous evaluation
of a stochastic motion predictor: real videos supply only one future per scene and no instance-level
rigidity ground truth, ruling out both distributional metrics and physically grounded ones such as
LRTL. We also use Kubric-D, with richer 3D scene geometry, to assess explicit geometric inputs.
We verify our findings on diversity and rigidity qualitatively on real videos from Cityscapes (Cordts
et al., 2016) and Physics 101 (Wu et al., 2016) (Sections E and F; videos in the supplement).
Metrics. We follow the evaluation protocol of Boduljak et al. (2026); lower is better for all metrics.

Model size, flow-matching loss, and downstream metrics. Prior work on trajectory forecasting
typically reports a single checkpoint, sometimes just for one model size (Bharadhwaj et al., 2024b;
Thakkar et al., 2026), without analyzing how metrics evolve during training. In Fig. 3, we show
how model size affects the flow-matching loss and downstream metrics over training for JTT-XS, -S,
and -B (exact values in Table 9). Larger models converge faster but show clear signs of overfitting:
their validation flow-matching loss (on the official Kubric-A validation split, disjoint from the test
set; averaged over timesteps 0.1–0.9) rises while the training loss keeps falling; for B, it is lowest
at 60K steps and nearly doubles by 130K, and S follows later and more mildly. The first effect of
overfitting is that the model collapses to predicting a single mode for each input image, evidenced
by an increase in FVMD, while LRTL (and Best-of-K, Fig. 6) keeps improving, with the LRTL of
B eventually falling below that of the ground truth (5.31 vs. 6.68 at 130K). XS, in contrast, does
not overfit: its training and validation losses stay close, and its FVMD decreases steadily. Overall,
plausibility (LRTL) and diversity (FVMD) do not improve together, and rankings depend on when
training stops: at 50K steps, B has the lowest FVMD (D) of the three models; at 130K, the highest.
Validation loss and model selection. The validation flow-matching loss is indicative of distributional
fidelity: its minimum coincides with the best FVMD (D) and (S) for XS at 130K (and, for FVMD (S),
also at 200K when trained twice as long; Table 10); it is one checkpoint later for B (60K vs. 50K), a
1.2–1.4% FVMD difference within the checkpoint-to-checkpoint noise of FVMD, whose moving
average is indeed lowest at 60K; and it is later for S (100K vs. 70K), whose validation loss is nearly
flat from 90K to 120K. This suggests that the validation loss, which needs one forward pass per
sample, could serve as a cheap proxy for FVMD, which requires sampling many futures per image.
To avoid single-checkpoint results, we evaluate an EMA checkpoint every 10K steps and report the
average over the last five (60K–100K), together with the best among them, as Average (Best).

5
CONCLUSION
We showed that diverse and plausible rigid-body motion can be forecast from a single image with
JTT, a simple diffusion transformer operating directly in trajectory space, without trajectory VAEs,
fixed grids, or rigidity priors, on synthetic multi-future data. Its key ingredients are diffusing per-
frame velocities with logit-normal timestep sampling, full-resolution RGB at the query points, and a
randomized support set for arbitrary and dense queries. As plausibility and diversity do not improve
together during training, fair comparison requires checkpoint-averaged evaluation on such data, under
which JTT outperforms prior methods on distributional and rigidity metrics. We hope our findings
promote better evaluation practices in the field; we leave rigorous real-data evaluation to future work.

A
EXTENDED RELATED WORK
This section expands the discussion of Section 2 with details omitted for space.
A.1
IMAGE-CONDITIONED TRAJECTORY FORECASTING
We forecast object motions as 2D point trajectories from a single image. Existing methods differ in
what they predict (absolute vs. relative coordinates) and how they model uncertainty (deterministic
vs. generative).
Deterministic regression of absolute coordinates. ATM (Wen et al., 2024) pseudo-labels
LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023a) with CoTracker (Karaev et al., 2024b) and trains a deterministic trans-
former to regress trajectories of 32 active points; Tra-MoE (Yang et al., 2025) adds a mixture-of-
experts head. Regressing a single output per query, these methods cannot represent the multimodal
future implied by a single image, and are biased toward active points by training-time supervision.
Generative diffusion of absolute coordinates. Track2Act (Bharadhwaj et al., 2024b) samples 400
trajectories with a denoising diffusion model (Ho et al., 2020) conditioned on an image and a goal
image, pseudo-labeled from Goyal et al. (2017); Damen et al. (2022); Brohan et al. (2023); Walke
et al. (2023). While stochastic, it diffuses absolute coordinates and ingests the image as a single
global vector with a 600M-parameter network, which in practice hallucinates objects and misses
small ones. A separate, training-free method (Pandey et al., 2024) guides a pre-trained image-to-flow
model (Shi et al., 2024) with hand-crafted energies, but only handles one pre-segmented object.
Latent trajectory diffusion. These methods generate trajectories in a learned latent space, typically
via a VAE (Walker et al., 2016; Li et al., 2018); see Section A.2 for details. Closest to our setting
is WHN (Boduljak et al., 2026), which trains a β-VAE over a fixed 32×32 trajectory grid and
runs rectified flow (Lipman et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023b) in the resulting latent space, conditioned
on patch-level DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2023) features. WHN is generative and uses patch-level
conditioning, but the VAE introduces jitter and boundary errors, and the fixed 32×32 grid couples
training and inference patterns, limiting the flexibility required for downstream applications. JTT
builds on WHN’s generative, image-grounded formulation and rigorous evaluation, but removes
the VAE entirely, generates per-frame velocities directly in coordinate space, and supports arbitrary
inference patterns at any density. Following WHN, we evaluate motion plausibility with FVMD (Liu
et al., 2024) and the rigidity-based LRTL score (Karazija et al., 2024), in the spirit of Salimans et al.
(2016); Unterthiner et al. (2018); Huang et al. (2024); Bansal et al. (2025), since pixel-level metrics
are poor proxies for motion generation (Boduljak et al., 2026).
A.2
EARLIER LATENT-VAE TRAJECTORY METHODS
Walker et al. (2016) use a DCT-based linear compression of trajectory offsets together with a VAE to
sample futures from static images. The linear compression has limited expressivity, and the evaluation
does not test physical plausibility or distributional coverage. Li et al. (2018) use a VAE to generate
optical flow maps from a static image and translate them to RGB; this inherits the failure modes
of frame-pair flow (drift under occlusion, no long-range consistency) and uses pixel-level metrics
rather than motion-aware ones. Both serve as early demonstrations that latent generative modeling of
motion is viable, but neither addresses the multi-object, rigidity-aware setting we target.
A.3
RECENT WORK IN DETAIL
TrajLoom. TrajLoom (Zhang et al., 2026b) extends the latent-VAE formulation of WHN to video
conditioning at larger scale: it diffuses VAE latents on a fixed grid using Wan2.1 (Wan et al., 2025)
RGB video latents and trains with auxiliary VAE losses and rollout fine-tuning. The contribution
is essentially an offset parameterization relative to the grid, plus additional video signal at larger
scale. The underlying recipe, latent diffusion over a fixed grid, and its limitations (no arbitrary query
patterns, no density beyond the training grid) carry over, while the offset-based parameterization is
suboptimal, as we show in this work.
MotionForecasting. MotionForecasting (Thakkar et al., 2026) (“Forecasting Motion in the Wild”)
extends Track2Act to in-the-wild forecasting on wildlife footage and is, architecturally, Track2Act

with the goal image dropped and ResNet-50 swapped for DINOv3 (Siméoni et al., 2025b). The model
is a joint-attention transformer over query points, which scales quadratically in the number of queries
and cannot run densely. The relative-coordinate parameterization is adopted but never ablated, so its
importance is not established. The evaluation runs WHN zero-shot on animal videos, even though
WHN is trained only on Kubric, so it remains unclear whether the proposed method outperforms WHN
under matched supervision. Even in this zero-shot setting, WHN still outperforms MotionForecasting
on the acceleration-based distributional metrics. The model also inherits the low-resolution image
conditioning of Track2Act, and consequently the same failure mode at object boundaries. The setting
also differs from ours: a single animal moving with simple, mostly translational motion, rather than
scenes with multiple interacting objects and rigid-body collisions. In addition, MotionForecasting
requires occlusion labels as input, whereas we deliberately minimize the number of inputs. Finally,
Table 5 shows that, within our architecture and on the benchmark of Boduljak et al. (2026), bilinearly
sampling DINO features at the query points, as in MotionForecasting, is worse than sampling
full-resolution RGB on every metric except foreground rigidity.
Reimplementation of MotionForecasting. Neither the implementation nor the dataset of Thakkar
et al. (2026) is publicly available, so we reimplement the method ourselves. We start from the
Track2Act codebase and strictly follow the architecture and training recipe described in the paper.
We set the trajectory horizon to T = 24 Kubric timesteps, matching prior work and our method, and
use ground-truth occlusion labels and the same isotropic delta normalization as for our method. We
train for 140 epochs, as in the paper, which amounts to about 180K steps. In contrast, the checkpoints
we report for our models are trained for at most 100K steps, since larger models overfit beyond that
point (Fig. 3).
Parameter count of GPTF. No public implementation of Lu et al. (2026) is available, so we estimate
its size from the architecture described in the paper. On Kubric, the model is a DiT with 6 blocks
of width d = 384; each block has temporal, spatial, and point–image attention (3 × 4d2), an MLP,
which we assume has ratio 4 (8d2), and AdaLN modulation for its four sublayers (12d2), giving about
32d2 ≈4.7M parameters per block and about 28M for all six. The feature projection, upsampling
layer, embeddings, and output layer add about 2M, for about 30M in total. The same estimate for the
Kinetics model (12 blocks of width 768) gives about 230M. As for JTT, we exclude the pre-trained
image encoder (DINOv3-S).

QUALITATIVE RESULTS ON PHYSICS 101
Following WHN (Boduljak et al., 2026), we also train JTT on real videos from Physics 101 (Wu et al.,
2016), which records simple physical experiments, and show samples on held-out sequences. As for
Cityscapes (Section F), real videos provide a single observed future per image and no object-level
rigidity ground truth, so a rigorous evaluation is not possible and we report qualitative results only
(Figs. 13 to 15). JTT generates rigid motion for rigid bodies, such as objects sliding down a ramp or
falling onto a table. It also captures the interaction between rigid and non-rigid bodies, such as a hand
releasing an object or a block entering water, and propagates forces through contact, such as a sliding
object pushing another one on collision. Finally, it reflects different kinds of forces acting on rigid
bodies, such as gravity and buoyancy on a wooden block dropped into water. JTT also retains its
ability to generate diverse and plausible futures on real data: for the same input frame, samples differ
in how objects slide, fall, and bounce, while remaining consistent with the ground truth (Fig. 16).

QUALITATIVE RESULTS ON CITYSCAPES
To test whether JTT transfers to real data, we train it on Cityscapes (Cordts et al., 2016), using the
training and evaluation data of WHN (Boduljak et al., 2026). This analysis is qualitative: real videos
provide a single observed future per image and no object-level rigidity ground truth, so the rigorous
evaluation of our synthetic experiments (Section 4) is not possible here. The training set has 2,975
sequences, the same order of magnitude as Kubric-A. We train for the same number of steps as on
Kubric-A, with identical hyperparameters (Table 14), and stop training once we observe overfitting
on the held-out set. Figures 17 and 18 show samples. Qualitatively, JTT preserves the rigidity of
objects that should move rigidly, such as cars, and handles egocentric camera motion well. It also
generalizes to some extent to deformable objects such as pedestrians: their overall motion is plausible,
but less precise than for rigid objects. We also verify that JTT samples diverse futures consistent with
the ground truth (Fig. 19). It does, and the samples are reasonable, although less diverse than on
Kubric-A, likely because it is trained on about 5× less data.
