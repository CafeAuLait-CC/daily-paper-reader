ABSTRACT
Pixel-space diffusion models provide an appealing alternative to latent-space dif-
fusion models by directly modeling raw images without relying on a learned to-
kenizer. However, scaling pixel-space models has predominantly followed dense
strategies, tightly coupling model capacity with active computation. In this work,
we show that standard LLM-style Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures can be
effectively transferred to pixel-space diffusion. Building on this finding, we intro-
duce PixelMoE, a pixel-space generative framework that sparsely scales model
capacity with standard LLM-style MoE, without diffusion-specific routing mech-
anisms or auxiliary routing objectives. Specifically, PixelMoE combines standard
Token-Choice (TC) routing with fine-grained routed experts for input-adaptive
sparse computation, a shared expert for capturing common representations, and a
bias-based load-balancing strategy. To complement sparse capacity scaling, Pix-
elMoE further introduces Pixel-Space Hierarchical Flow Matching, which varies
spatial resolution along the denoising trajectory from global structure formation
to fine-grained detail refinement. At comparable active parameter counts, Pixel-
MoE consistently outperforms its dense counterparts across model scales, demon-
strating effective sparse capacity scaling in pixel space.

ImageNet-1K, PixelMoE achieves FID scores of 1.47 at 256 × 256 and 1.53 at

INTRODUCTION
Recent advances in diffusion models, particularly Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) (Peebles & Xie,
2023; Ma et al., 2024) trained with flow matching objectives (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023),
have substantially improved the quality and scalability of image generation. Most modern diffu-
sion models (Rombach et al., 2022) denoise in latent spaces extracted by variational autoencoders
(VAEs) (Kingma & Welling, 2013), whose lossy compression may discard high-frequency details
and introduce structural artifacts, leading to a reconstruction bottleneck that limits achievable image
fidelity. Recently, pixel-space diffusion models (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Li & He, 2026) have
regained attention by directly modeling raw images rather than learned latent representations. Ad-
vances in clean-image prediction (Li & He, 2026), architectural design (He et al., 2026; Yu et al.,
2026), and training objectives (Ma et al., 2026b) have made raw-pixel modeling easier, enabling
pixel-space models to achieve performance competitive with leading latent-space approaches.
Despite this progress, sparsely scaling pixel-space diffusion remains largely unexplored. Existing
pixel-space models primarily scale their generators densely by increasing model width and depth,
tightly coupling capacity growth with active computation. In contrast, Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)
architectures (Shazeer et al., 2017) expand total capacity through sparse expert activation while
controlling active computation, and have become a standard scaling paradigm in Large Language
Models (LLMs) (Liu et al., 2024). However, directly transferring standard LLM-style MoE to dif-
fusion has not consistently yielded clear gains over dense counterparts (Fei et al., 2024; Liu et al.,
2025). Subsequent approaches therefore adopt alternative routing paradigms such as Expert-Choice
(EC) (Sun et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025), or introduce diffusion-specific routing guidance and ob-
jectives (Wei et al., 2026). This naturally raises the question: Can standard LLM-style MoE be
directly transferred to pixel-space diffusion?
We answer this question affirmatively. We propose PixelMoE, a Pixel-space Mixture-of-Experts
generative framework that sparsely scales pixel-space diffusion transformers without diffusion-
specific routing mechanisms or auxiliary routing objectives. Specifically, PixelMoE adopts standard
Token-Choice (TC) routing, where each image token dynamically selects its top-A experts based on
the routing scores. Each MoE layer combines fine-grained routed experts for input-adaptive sparse
computation with a shared expert that captures common representations across image tokens. To
maintain balanced expert utilization without auxiliary balancing objectives, we further adopt a bias-
based load-balancing strategy. Beyond sparse capacity scaling, pixel-space representations can be
directly downsampled and upsampled across resolutions while preserving meaningful visual struc-
ture. We exploit this property with Pixel-Space Hierarchical Flow Matching, organizing generation
into a low-resolution coarse stage followed by full-resolution refinement.
Extensive experiments demonstrate strong sparse scaling behavior of PixelMoE. Under comparable
active parameter counts, PixelMoE outperforms its dense counterparts across model scales. Fur-

diffusion.

RELATED WORK
Pixel-Space Generative Models.
Early generative models, including GANs (Brock et al., 2019;
Casanova et al., 2021) and diffusion models (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Ho et al., 2022), synthe-
size images directly in pixel space. Latent-space models (Rombach et al., 2022; Esser et al., 2021)
later became dominant by modeling compact representations extracted by variational autoencoders
(VAEs) (Kingma & Welling, 2013), whose lossy compression introduces a reconstruction bottle-
neck that limits the achievable generation fidelity. This limitation has renewed interest in pixel-
space generative models, which directly model raw images without lossy compression. Simple Dif-
fusion (Hoogeboom et al., 2023) demonstrated competitive high-resolution pixel-space generation
through architectural scaling, noise schedules, and spatial downsampling, while VDM++ (Kingma
& Gao, 2023) studied diffusion-objective weighting and noise augmentation. JiT (Li & He, 2026)
further showed that a large-patch Transformer can effectively model raw pixels through clean-image
prediction. Subsequent approaches improve pixel-space generation through multi-resolution gener-
ation (Chen et al., 2025), neural-field decoding (Wang et al., 2026a), hierarchical architectures (Yu
et al., 2026; He et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2026), frequency-decoupled modeling (Ma et al., 2026a),
representation alignment (Shin et al., 2026), perceptual supervision (Ma et al., 2026b), and alterna-
tive flow parameterizations (Chen et al., 2026a). Despite these advances, most existing pixel-space
models remain dense, coupling increased model capacity with increased active computation, while
sparse MoE scaling in pixel-space diffusion remains underexplored.
Mixture-of-Experts in Visual Generation.
The Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) paradigm was intro-
duced to enable specialization among multiple expert networks (Jacobs et al., 1991) and later ex-
tended with sparse gating (Shazeer et al., 2017). Modern MoE architectures (Fedus et al., 2022;
Dai et al., 2024) have become a key scaling mechanism for Large Language Models (LLMs) (Yang
et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024), motivating their adoption in visual generation. Early expert-based
diffusion models, largely built on U-Net backbones, partition the denoising trajectory by noise level,
denoising task, or generation path and assign specialized parameters to different regimes (Balaji
et al., 2022; Feng et al., 2023; Xue et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2024; Ganjdanesh et al., 2024). As dif-
fusion backbones shift toward Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) (Peebles & Xie, 2023), recent work
instead explores sparse expert routing within Transformer blocks. However, directly applying con-
ventional Token-Choice routing has not consistently yielded clear gains over dense models (Fei
et al., 2024). Subsequent approaches have therefore introduced Expert-Choice routing (Sun et al.,
2025), diffusion-aware routing or dynamic capacity allocation (Cheng et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025),
and specialized token-expert assignment with auxiliary routing objectives (Yuan et al., 2025; Wei
et al., 2026). However, most existing sparse DiT approaches focus on latent-space diffusion. JiT-
MoE (Liu et al., 2025) has recently extended MoE to pixel-space diffusion but reports only modest
gains over its dense JiT counterparts. We therefore investigate whether standard LLM-style sparse
MoE can provide systematic scaling gains in pixel-space generation.

3.2
PIXEL-SPACE HIERARCHICAL FLOW MATCHING
Pixel space naturally supports coarse-to-fine generation, as spatial resampling directly produces
meaningful multi-resolution images. We therefore vary the spatial resolution along the denoising
trajectory, using coarse representations during early denoising and full-resolution representations
for later refinement. This hierarchical trajectory also requires the model to process heterogeneous
pixel-token representations across resolutions and noise levels, which are naturally accommodated
by the input-adaptive routing of PixelMoE. Specifically, Pixel-Space Hierarchical Flow Matching
organizes generation into two stages: Stage 0 performs partial denoising at low resolution for coarse
structure modeling, while Stage 1 continues at full resolution for detail refinement. As illustrated
in Figure 4, both stages share the same PixelMoE model while denoising at different spatial resolu-
tions and noise levels.

EXPERIMENTS
We conduct all experiments on class-conditional ImageNet-1K generation (Deng et al., 2009).
Training Details.
During training, we apply random horizontal flipping with probability 0.5.
Models for comparison with previous methods are trained for 320 epochs, while other experiments
use 100 epochs unless otherwise specified. For PixelMoE-H-E32A4, we apply additional regu-
larization after epoch 200. We use a learning rate of 2 × 10−4 with a 5-epoch linear warm-up,
followed by a constant schedule with cosine decay over the final 40 epochs. For optimization, we
apply Gram Newton-Schulz Muon (gMuon) (Jordan et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026) with momen-
tum 0.95 to eligible matrix-valued parameters, while the remaining parameters are optimized with
AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) using (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.95). We employ stage-aware Logit-
Normal timestep sampling with (µg, δg) = (0.8, 0.8) for stage allocation and (µl, δl) = (0.6, 0.8)
for stage-relative timestep sampling. Two exponential moving average (EMA) copies with decay
rates 0.9996 and 0.9999 are maintained during training. Detailed training configurations are pro-
vided in Section E.

4.1
SPARSE SCALING ANALYSIS
We first investigate whether sparse MoE scaling can effectively improve pixel-space generation. We
study the sparse scaling behavior of PixelMoE from three perspectives: active parameter scaling,
expert scaling, and computation scaling via token granularity.
Active Parameter Scaling.
We first compare PixelMoE with its dense counterparts across model
scales. For controlled comparisons, each dense baseline uses the same hierarchical flow setup and
training recipe as its PixelMoE counterpart, with sparse MoE layers replaced by dense FFNs at com-
parable active parameter counts. As shown in Table 1, PixelMoE consistently achieves lower FID
than dense models across Base, Large, and Huge scales. Beyond generation quality, we compare
the convergence of Dense-L and PixelMoE-L-E32A4, extending the training to 200 epochs for this
analysis. As shown in Figure 5, PixelMoE-L-E32A4 reaches the FID achieved by Dense-L at 200
epochs after only about 71 epochs, reducing the required training epochs by a factor of approx-
imately 2.8. These results show that sparse MoE scaling improves generation quality while also
accelerating convergence at comparable active parameter counts.
Expert Scaling.
We next isolate the effect of sparse model capacity by varying the number of
routed experts E while fixing the number of activated experts to A = 4. As shown in Table 2a,
increasing E from 8 to 16 and then 32 improves FID from 2.28 to 2.03 and 1.78, respectively, while
keeping the active parameter count fixed at 632M. This shows that PixelMoE effectively benefits
from increased total model capacity without increasing the number of active parameters.

Computation Scaling via Token Granularity.
We further study how PixelMoE benefits from in-
creased computation by reducing the patch size, which increases the token sequence length while
keeping the backbone scale fixed. As shown in Table 2b, PixelMoE consistently outperforms the
dense models at both patch sizes, with a larger improvement at finer token granularity. A possible ex-
planation is that smaller patches produce more spatially localized token representations and provide
more token-level routing decisions, enabling the router to exploit finer-grained spatial variation.
4.2
EXPERT SPECIALIZATION
We analyze the routing behavior of PixelMoE-L-E32A4 on the ImageNet-1K validation set. For
selected MoE layers, we collect token-level routing assignments and examine how expert prefer-
ences vary across semantic classes and along the denoising trajectory. We report the log2 ratio of the
observed assignment frequency to that under uniform routing, where positive and negative values
indicate preferred and suppressed experts, respectively.
As shown in Figure 6, PixelMoE exhibits distinct routing patterns across semantic classes and
along the denoising trajectory. Class-dependent routing is relatively weak in shallow layers, be-
comes clearer in intermediate layers, and is most pronounced around Layers 12–20, where different
ImageNet-1K classes favor different subsets of experts. In contrast, routing along the denoising tra-
jectory is already prominent in shallow layers, weakens in intermediate layers, and emerges strongly
again in the final layer. The routing pattern also changes noticeably across the transition between
the two hierarchical stages. Overall, class-dependent routing specialization is more pronounced in
intermediate layers, whereas specialization along the denoising trajectory is stronger in shallow and
final layers.

4.3
BENCHMARKING AGAINST PREVIOUS SYSTEMS
Table 3 compares PixelMoE with representative latent-space and pixel-space generative systems on
class-conditional ImageNet-1K at 256 × 256 resolution. PixelMoE-B-E32A4 achieves an FID of
1.88 with only 225M active parameters. As total model capacity increases, PixelMoE-L-E32A4
further improves the FID to 1.54, while PixelMoE-H-E32A4 reaches 1.47. Notably, these results
are achieved without representation alignment (Yu et al., 2025; Shin et al., 2026) or perceptual
losses (Ma et al., 2026b). Compared with leading dense pixel-space models, PixelMoE achieves
better generation quality. PixelMoE also remains competitive with leading latent-space approaches
without relying on a learned VAE. Together with the controlled scaling results in Tables 1 and 2a,
these comparisons support LLM-style sparse MoE as an effective scaling paradigm for pixel-space
generative modeling.

Fine-Grained Routed Experts.
We next study the effect of expert granularity under matched
total and active parameter counts. As shown in Table 5b, changing the expert configuration from
E8A1 to E32A4 expands the number of possible expert combinations from 8 to 35,960 without
increasing either total or active parameter count. The corresponding FID improvement from 1.95 to
1.78 indicates that PixelMoE benefits from finer-grained expert composition.
Bias-Based Load-Balancing Strategy.
The bias-based load-balancing strategy offers a simple
but effective way to regulate expert utilization without introducing an auxiliary loss. As shown
in Table 5c, the bias-based strategy improves FID from 2.05 to 1.78 compared with an auxiliary load-
balancing loss, with identical total and active parameter counts. Together with the expert-utilization
analysis in Figure 7, this result indicates that PixelMoE can preserve flexible input-adaptive routing
while maintaining balanced global expert utilization.
Token-Choice Routing Strategy.
We compare Token-Choice and Expert-Choice routing to exam-
ine which routing paradigm is better suited to pixel-space diffusion. We implement DiffMoE (Shi
et al., 2025), which adopts Expert-Choice routing, using the same pixel-space backbone and train-
ing recipe as PixelMoE while keeping the total and active parameter counts comparable. As shown
in Table 5d, Token-Choice routing achieves a substantially lower FID. This suggests that token-
centric expert selection better accommodates the heterogeneous pixel-token representations across
semantic classes and along the denoising trajectory.
Explicit Routing Guidance.
A natural question is whether expert routing requires explicit
timestep or semantic information. For timestep-aware routing, we provide the timestep embedding
directly to the router together with the token hidden states; for semantics-aware routing, we follow
the semantic routing design of ProMoE (Wei et al., 2026). As shown in Table 6a, neither variant
improves upon the default PixelMoE router, which makes routing decisions solely from token hid-
den states. Together with the expert specialization observed across semantic classes and along the
12


Preprint
denoising trajectory in Figure 6, these results suggest that token hidden states in pixel-space diffu-
sion already contain rich timestep and semantic cues that support effective routing without explicit
guidance.
Hierarchical Flow Schedule.
The pixel-space hierarchical flow schedule progressively changes
the spatial granularity along the denoising trajectory. We compare it with a single-stage counterpart
that performs the entire denoising process at full resolution. For a fair comparison, we adjust the
number of training iterations such that both variants process the same total number of image tokens.
As shown in Table 6b, hierarchical flow improves FID from 2.54 to 2.46. This result shows the
benefit of adapting spatial granularity along the denoising trajectory by using coarser representations
for early denoising and finer representations for later refinement.
Stage-Aware Timestep Sampling.
The two hierarchical stages denoise at different spatial reso-
lutions, motivating stage-aware timestep sampling. The baseline uses a single global Logit-Normal
distribution (µg, δg) = (0.8, 0.8) to sample timesteps over the complete denoising trajectory. In
contrast, our default strategy uses the same global distribution for stage allocation and indepen-
dently samples the stage-relative timestep σ within each stage from a Logit-Normal distribution with
(µl, δl) = (0.6, 0.8). As shown in Table 6c, this stage-aware sampling strategy improves FID from
2.65 to 2.46, allowing the timestep distribution within each stage to be controlled independently of
how frequently that stage is sampled.
DDT Prediction Head.
We further ablate the Decoupled Diffusion Transformer (DDT) predic-
tion head (Wang et al., 2026b) on PixelMoE-B-E32A4. Without DDT, the backbone features are
mapped directly to the high-dimensional pixel-space prediction, whereas the default model intro-
duces a wider prediction pathway after the backbone. As shown in Table 6d, the DDT head im-
proves FID from 3.76 to 2.46. This result supports the use of the DDT prediction head in our final
configuration.

CONCLUSION
This paper presents PixelMoE, a pixel-space image generation framework that sparsely scales model
capacity by directly adopting an LLM-style Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture. We further in-
corporate Pixel-Space Hierarchical Flow Matching to progressively increase spatial resolution along
the denoising trajectory. Experiments on ImageNet-1K show that PixelMoE consistently outper-
forms its dense counterparts across model scales and achieves competitive performance relative to
leading latent- and pixel-space generative models. Our routing analyses further reveal clear expert
specialization across semantic classes and along the denoising trajectory under standard Token-
Choice routing, without explicit semantic or timestep routing guidance. These results support LLM-
style sparse MoE as an effective scaling paradigm for pixel-space generative modeling.

class PixelMoELayer(nn.Module):
def __init__(
self, dim, routed_dim, shared_dim,
E=32, A=4, gamma=1e-3
):
self.router = nn.Linear(dim, E, bias=False)
self.experts = nn.ModuleList(
[Expert(routed_dim) for _ in range(E)])
self.shared_expert = Expert(shared_dim)
self.router_bias = torch.zeros(E)
self.A = A
self.router_scale = math.sqrt(A)
self.gamma = gamma
def forward(self, x):
scores = torch.sigmoid(self.router(x))
# Balancing bias is used only for expert selection
expert_ids = torch.topk(
scores + self.router_bias,
self.A, dim=-1
).indices
# Normalize the original routing scores
weights = gather(scores, expert_ids)
weights = weights / weights.sum(
dim=-1, keepdim=True)
weights = self.router_scale * weights
routed = sparse_expert_forward(
x, self.experts, expert_ids, weights)
shared = self.shared_expert(x)
return routed + shared

Algorithm 2 Pixel-Space Hierarchical Flow Matching: PyTorch-like Pseudo-code
class HierarchicalFlow(nn.Module):
def __init__(self, model):
self.model = model
# shared PixelMoE
def training_loss(self, x, c):
stage = sample_stage()
if stage == 0:
x_low = downsample(x)
noise = delta_n * torch.randn_like(x_low)
x_start = noise
x_end = (1 - t_e0) * x_low + t_e0 * noise
t_start, t_end = 1.0, t_e0
else:
x_coarse = upsample(downsample(x))
noise = delta_n * torch.randn_like(x)
x_start = (1 - t_s1) * x_coarse + t_s1 * noise
x_end = x
t_start, t_end = t_s1, 0.0
# Stage-relative timestep
sigma = sample_timestep(stage)
x_sigma = sigma * x_start + (1 - sigma) * x_end
# Corresponding global timestep
t = t_end + sigma * (t_start - t_end)
# Stage-wise endpoint prediction
x_end_pred = self.model(x_sigma, t, c)
sigma_safe = sigma.clamp_min(sigma_min)
loss = ((x_end_pred - x_end) / sigma_safe) ** 2
return loss.mean()
def velocity(self, x, t, c, stage):
if stage == 0:
t_start, t_end = 1.0, t_e0
else:
t_start, t_end = t_s1, 0.0
# Convert global timestep to stage-relative timestep
sigma = (t - t_end) / (t_start - t_end)
# Convert endpoint prediction to velocity
x_end_pred = self.model(x, t, c)
sigma_safe = sigma.clamp_min(sigma_min)
return (x - x_end_pred) / sigma_safe
@torch.no_grad()
def sample(self, c):
# Stage 0: low-resolution generation
x = delta_n * torch.randn(low_res_shape)
x = heun_solver(self.velocity, x, c, stage=0)
# Distribution-preserving stage transition
x = stage_transition(x)
# Stage 1: full-resolution refinement
x = heun_solver(self.velocity, x, c, stage=1)
return x

MODEL CONFIGURATION
We implement PixelMoE at Base, Large, and Huge scales, with the detailed configurations sum-
marized in Table 7. For 256 × 256 generation, all models use a patch size of 8; we use a patch
size of 16 at 512 × 512. All models adopt the E32A4 MoE configuration unless otherwise specified,
where each image token activates 4 experts from a pool of 32 routed experts. As the backbone scales
from Base to Huge, we increase its depth and hidden dimension together with the capacity of both
routed and shared experts. A wide DDT head is appended to each backbone for stage-wise endpoint
prediction, using 2, 2, and 4 blocks for the Base, Large, and Huge models, respectively. Across
all scales, the first four backbone blocks remain dense, while the remaining backbone blocks adopt
sparse MoE layers; the DDT head retains dense FFNs.

ADDITIONAL TRAINING DETAILS
We summarize the detailed training configurations of PixelMoE in Table 8. Unless otherwise speci-

Controlled Dense Baselines.
For controlled comparisons, each dense baseline follows the same

Regularization.
For the final PixelMoE-H-E32A4 models, we introduce additional regularization
after epoch 200. Following LlamaGen (Sun et al., 2024), we use a crop range of 1.1, where each
training image is first resized to 1.1× the target resolution and then randomly cropped to the target
size. We additionally apply dropout with a rate of 0.2 to the output of each self-attention module
and feed-forward network. These modifications increase the diversity of training views and provide
stronger regularization during the later stage of training.
Optimization.
We use gMuon (Zhang et al., 2026) for eligible matrix-valued parameters, includ-
ing the routed-expert matrices, while AdaLN layers, the final prediction layer, timestep and class
embedders, and the remaining parameters are optimized with AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019).
We use a learning rate of 2 × 10−4 with a global batch size of 1024. After a 5-epoch warm-up, we
keep the learning rate constant and apply cosine decay to 2 × 10−5 over the final 40 epochs. We
train with bfloat16 mixed precision and maintain two EMA copies with decay rates 0.9996 and
0.9999.
Hierarchical Flow Training.
We use the same two-stage pixel-space hierarchical flow matching
schedule for both resolutions, with Stage 0 denoising at half spatial resolution and Stage 1 at full
resolution. We set the Stage 0 endpoint to te0 = 0.75 and use ρ = −1/3 for the noise correlation.
Under the cross-stage transition derived in Section B, these settings determine the Stage 1 starting
noise level as ts1 = 6/7. The relative sampling frequency of the two stages is determined by
a global Logit-Normal distribution with (µg, δg) = (0.8, 0.8), with the probability mass in the
overlapping stage interval divided equally between the two stages. The stage-relative timestep is
then independently sampled from a Logit-Normal distribution with (µl, δl) = (0.6, 0.8). Under the
resulting stage intervals, this yields sampling probabilities p(s = 0) ≈0.231 and p(s = 1) ≈0.769.

Model Selection.
