# PrT-Flow: Probability-Tractable Flow Matching for Reinforcement Learning 

Congzhong Sun * Ning Tan †

School of Computer Science and Engineering, Sun Yat-sen University 

Abstract 

Diffusion models and Flow Matching can represent flexible distributions over robot actions, but many reinforcement learning algorithms also require the probability of each action. We introduce PrT-Flow (Probability-Tractable Flow Matching), which computes probability density while retaining multimodal, multi-step action generation. Its Jacobian-Tractable Transformer (JTT) makes each sampling step invertible and enables efficient computation of its Jacobian determinant. We train these finite steps with Conditional Euler-step Training (CET), which supervises their displacement over one step during sampling rather than their instantaneous velocity. We prove that, under certain assumptions, CET has the same minimizers as fitting the Euler-solver updates of Flow Matching. PrT-Flow achieves 96% success on Robomimic Lift after 300 epochs of imitation learning, matching the diffusion policy reference, and 96% success on OGBench Cube-Triple after 600k online steps. These results demonstrate that invertible networks with tractable probability densities can learn effective robot policies and support SAC-style actor– critic training. Our code is available at https://github.com/Sunlight-zero/PrT-Flow .

# 1 Introduction 

Learning robot manipulation from demonstrations requires a policy that maps observations, such as camera images or robot states, to actions. For a given observation, several motions may accomplish the same task, yet their average may not correspond to any valid motion. Therefore, the policy should represent a distribution over actions. With action chunking, it predicts a sequence of actions from the current observation and replans from a new observation after executing a specified number of actions. Given an observation c, a stochastic policy treats an action chunk A as a random vector following the conditional distribution π(· | c). Diffusion Policy learns this distribution from robot demonstrations using a conditional diffusion model (Chi et al., 2023). Flow Matching offers a related approach to probability transport, learning to move noise toward demonstration actions along a prescribed probability path (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). PointFlowMatch uses this formulation in place of diffusion to learn robot manipulation policies (Chisari et al., 2024). Both approaches use flexible generative models to capture the multiple valid behaviors that a robot may exhibit. Imitation learning (IL) and reinforcement learning (RL) provide complementary ways to learn robot policies: IL trains a policy to reproduce demonstrations, while RL uses task rewards to improve behavior through interaction. In the actor–critic framework widely used for continuous control, the actor represents the policy and the critic estimates state or action values. Many RL algorithms also require policy probabilities. Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) uses the log probability of a sampled action in its entropy objective (Haarnoja et al.,

Therefore, a generative policy must provide both action samples and their probabilities to support these algorithms. Flow Matching provides a sampling procedure, but computing the probability density of its actions requires additional work. Continuous normalizing flows track density changes by integrating the divergence of the velocity field (Chen et al., 2018). However, a robot policy generates actions through finitely many numerical steps, whose endpoint distribution may differ from that of the continuous flow. We seek to compute the density of each action chunk under this finite sampling procedure as the chunk is generated. We introduce PrT-Flow (Probability-Tractable Flow Matching; Figure 1), which parameterizes each sampling step with an invertible network whose Jacobian determinant is analytically tractable. The Jacobian-Tractable Transformer (JTT) combines causal conditioning with monotone scalar transformations. Each forward pass returns an updated sample and the corresponding determinant, allowing the policy to compute the probability of denoised actions using the change-of-variables formula. We train these steps with Conditional Euler-step Training (CET), which supervises their displacement over one sampling step. Each training example supervises a single step along an interpolation path between noise and a demonstration endpoint; at deployment, the policy composes the steps to generate an action chunk. PrT-Flow thus combines the computable probabilities of normalizing flows with local regression along Flow Matching paths, supporting multimodal, multi-step action generation while providing the action log probabilities used by SAC. The causal and invertibility constraints in JTT may affect its ability to represent effective behavior. We 2therefore evaluate PrT-Flow in both imitation learning and offline-to-online reinforcement learning on several benchmarks. PrT-Flow achieves high task success in these settings. We summarize our contributions as follows. 1. We propose PrT-Flow and its JTT architecture, which compute the probability density of an action during multi-step generation using tractable per-step Jacobian determinants. 2. We introduce CET, a training objective whose minimizers coincide with those of marginal Flow Matching over the same family of finite maps. We also show its equivalence to regression against Euler-solver updates of the marginal velocity field. 3. Our experiments on Lift and Cube-Triple show that the invertible architecture supports high success rates in both imitation learning and SAC-style online control. Our code is available at https://github.com/Sunlight-zero/PrT-Flow .

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Diffusion and Flow Matching Policies 

As discussed in the Introduction, Diffusion Policy generates action sequences by iterative denoising (Chi et al., 2023). Flow Matching and rectified flow learn velocity fields that transport noise to data (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023), and PointFlowMatch applies conditional Flow Matching to robot manipulation (Chisari et al., 2024). PrT-Flow retains multi-step generation to preserve the multimodal ability of Diffusion Policy and Flow Matching policies while providing the action probabilities needed for RL. 

2.2 Reinforcement Learning for Generative Policies 

DPPO fine-tunes diffusion policies with PPO over an augmented denoising decision process (Ren et al., 2025). Its PPO ratios use probabilities of individual denoising transitions, rather than the marginal probability of the final action chunk. FQL distills a Flow Matching behavior policy into a separate one-step actor optimized with Q-values (Park et al., 2025b). FPO constructs proxy probability ratios from conditional Flow Matching losses (McAllister et al., 2025), while RLDT improves a pretrained flow policy through transport updates (Lei et al., 2026). These approaches enable policy improvement without directly evaluating the exact probability of the final action samples produced by the multi-step generator. PrT-Flow instead provides the final action probability directly for policy optimization. 

2.3 Density Evaluation for Policy Optimization 

Normalizing-flow policies have been used in SAC (Ward et al., 2019; Mazoure et al., 2020) and dexterous fine-tuning with action-chunked critics (Yang et al., 2026). NFPO uses stabilized Real NVP couplings to provide action probabilities for PPO ratios (Shi et al., 2026); PrT-Flow instead learns multi-step maps through local Flow Matching regression with CET. SAC-Flow uses noise-augmented sampling and a joint probability density over intermediate states as an entropy surrogate for offline-to-online SAC (Zhang et al., 2025a). PrT-Flow obtains the final action density directly by applying deterministic, invertible Euler-solver steps after sampling the initial Gaussian noise. We mainly compare the performance of our methods to SAC-Flow in Section 5. GenPO++ uses a reversible solver with paired consecutive states, whose fixed Jacobian determinants cancel in PPO-style joint probability ratios (Hu et al., 2026). PrT-Flow computes the final action probability density without an auxiliary solver-history variable. 3FALCON learns interval transport through average-velocity regression, uses cycle consistency to encour-age invertibility, and evaluates discrete-map Jacobian determinants for Boltzmann sampling (Rehman et al., 2025). CET instead directly supervises finite maps against Euler targets at the actual sampling step size, while JTT guarantees invertibility by construction and computes determinants from analytic scalar derivatives. Related architectures include coupling and spline flows (Dinh et al., 2017; Durkan et al., 2019), causal Transformers (Zhai et al., 2025), and learned orthogonal projections (Dai and Seljak, 2021). JTT combines learned orthogonal coordinates with causal monotone splines and step-size conditioning. RegFlow regresses against precomputed endpoint transports (Rehman et al., 2026), whereas CET supervises local finite steps. Continuous normalizing flows and maximum-entropy Flow Matching RL track probability density through velocity-field divergence (Chen et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2025b), whereas PrT-Flow accumulates the log-determinants of its actual sampling maps.

# 4 PrT-Flow 

PrT-Flow generates action chunks by repeatedly transforming noisy actions and tracking the change in probability density at each step. The Jacobian-Tractable Transformer (JTT) implements these Euler-solver updates, returning the updated state together with the update’s Jacobian determinant. Both computations are differentiable with respect to the network parameters, allowing SAC to optimize the policy through sampled actions and their log probabilities. Conditional Euler-step Training (CET) learns the Euler-solver updates by supervising their displacements along demonstration paths. We first explain how to compute probability densities during sampling, then describe the network architecture and training procedure. 

4.1 Motivation 

Consider the Euler update from Section 3.3, xk+1 = xk + h v θ(xk, t k, c ). Viewed as a transformation of the state xk, it defines Fh(x, t, c ) = x + h v θ(x, t, c ), which we call an Euler-step . The sampler starts from Gaussian noise and applies K such transformations. If, for fixed h, t, c , each Fh(·, t, c ) is a piecewise continuously differentiable bijection with a nonzero Jacobian determinant almost everywhere, the probability density after one step follows from the change-of-variables formula:

4.2 Design Requirements for the Euler-step Network 

Section 4.1 derives the action probability density from the determinant of each Euler-step. To compute these determinants while learning from Flow Matching paths, we impose four requirements on the Euler-step network. 1. Identity at zero step size and a local velocity. The step size h specifies the duration of each transport step. A zero-length step must therefore leave the state unchanged: Fθ, 0(x, t, c ) = x. Without this condition, the transformation could produce a finite displacement even as the time interval tends to 6zero. Here the local velocity is induced by the directly parameterized finite map family. Wherever the derivative with respect to h exists at zero, the identity condition gives the expansion 

vθ(x, t, c ) = ∂hFθ,h (x, t, c )|h=0 , Fθ,h (x, t, c ) = x + h v θ(x, t, c ) + o(h). (10) Thus, the displacement divided by h approaches vθ as h → 0, connecting the learned finite update to a velocity field in the zero-step limit. 2. Global invertibility and spatial regularity. To apply the change-of-variables formula, we require the Euler update, for each fixed (t, c, h ), to be a piecewise continuously differentiable bijection of Rd with a nonzero spatial Jacobian determinant almost everywhere. Every output then has a unique preimage, allowing us to propagate its probability density through the full sequence of updates. 3. Tractable determinants during sampling. An invertible network does not necessarily provide a tractable determinant. For a typical neural network, computing the Jacobian determinant requires additional calculations beyond the forward pass. Our network must therefore compute the Jacobian determinant efficiently during the forward pass, without constructing the full Jacobian matrix, so that 

log | det JFθ,h | can be accumulated to obtain the sample’s probability density. 4. Numerically well-behaved transformations. A transformation can be analytically invertible yet sharply compress or stretch particular directions, making repeated updates and probability density gradients sensitive to numerical perturbations. The architecture and implementation should limit such directional distortions and allow stable evaluation of log-determinants and their gradients. This is a numerical design goal. We will describe how JTT addresses these requirements in the next section. 

4.3 Network Architecture 

One JTT feedforward computation consists of M sequential map heads . Each head combines a learned orthogonal coordinate frame, a Transformer conditioner, and monotone scalar splines. A spline is a one-dimensional function made of connected curve segments; we use it to transform each scalar coordinate monotonically. Figure 2 shows the complete feedforward calculation (A), the operations within a head (B), and the determinant calculation (C). A head first rotates the state x ∈ Rd into its learned frame, y = Qx , then embeds the right-shifted scalar sequence (0 , y 1, . . . , y d−1) as d tokens. Together with time and observation information, these tokens enter the Transformer, which predicts spline parameters for each coordinate. The splines transform the unshifted values yi, and Q⊤ rotates the result back to the original frame. A map head thus denotes a complete invertible transformation, distinct from the attention heads inside its Transformer. The Transformer predicts parameters, while the splines perform the state transformation. The conditioner can therefore use nonlinear representations of the state and observations, with invertibility guaranteed by the transformation it parameterizes. Causality specifies the dependency order among scalar coordinates of the generative state; all commands in an action chunk share the current observation condition c.

The Jacobian is lower triangular, so its determinant equals the product of the diagonal scalar derivatives, evaluated with the prefix held fixed. Since ηθ,i does not depend on yi, these entries require only the derivative of each scalar transformation with respect to its own input, without differentiating through the conditioner. Since all input coordinates are available during forward sampling, one Transformer pass predicts the parameters for all coordinates in parallel. We ensure a nonzero determinant by choosing scalar transformations with positive derivatives. Each coordinate uses a monotone rational-quadratic spline (RQS) (Durkan et al., 2019), which divides a fixed interval into bins and maps each input bin to an output bin with a rational-quadratic curve. The predicted bin widths, bin heights, and knot slopes control how the spline nonlinearly expands or contracts different parts of the coordinate range. Positive widths, heights, and slopes make the transformation strictly increasing within the interval; identity tails extend it to a bijection of R. Every scalar transformation is therefore bijective at a fixed prefix, as is the full causal transformation. Analytic RQS derivatives give the diagonal entries needed to evaluate its determinant. 

4.3.2 Step-Size Parameterization and Orthogonal Heads 

To obtain the identity at h = 0 , we parameterize the spline through deviations from its identity settings, controlled by the step size. We convert the conditioner’s raw outputs b into bounded parameter rates and scale them by h:

r(b) = R tanh( b/R ), ξh = h r (b) (12) Here R > 0 controls the rate magnitude, and ξh gives the parameter offsets for the current step. Zero offsets produce an identity spline: the input and output bin boundaries coincide, and every knot slope equals one. Hence h = 0 ensures ξh = 0 and Sθ, 0 = id for any network output. For h > 0, the predicted offsets change the spline’s shape. This parameterization embeds the identity condition from Section 4.2 directly in the architecture. 4.4 Training 

4.4.1 Conditional Euler-step Training 

Standard Flow Matching trains a velocity field along a continuous-time path, whereas our policy samples through a discrete sequence of finite maps. The identity condition Fθ, 0(x, t, c ) = x defines a local velocity, 

∂hFθ,h (x, t, c )|h=0 , wherever the derivative exists (Section 4.2). This derivative describes only the map family’s first-order behavior as h → 0.At sampling time, the policy applies the finite map at a fixed positive step size h = 1 /K . Two map families with the same derivative at h = 0 can therefore produce different displacements at the sampling step size: equal zero-step velocities do not imply equal finite updates. CET therefore supervises the finite displacement at the sampling step size. Dividing this displacement by h gives a prediction of the Flow Matching path velocity that requires only one forward map evaluation. No differentiation with respect to h is needed, and parameter gradients follow by ordinary backpropagation. 4.4.2 SAC-Style Policy Learning 

We follow an offline-to-online actor–critic schedule based on SAC-Flow (Zhang et al., 2025a). Each offline iteration performs two successive updates on the same batch. # 5 Evaluation 

Our experiments address three questions: can JTT learn effective imitation policies under its structural constraints; can the same policy support offline-to-online actor–critic learning; and how do finite-step supervision and learned coordinate frames affect task performance? Lift evaluates imitation learning and the two design choices, while Cube-Triple evaluates online learning and compares offline training configurations. 

5.1 Experimental Setup 

Benchmarks. For Robomimic Lift (Mandlekar et al., 2022), we use the low-dimensional Multi-Human demonstration dataset, comprising 27,582 training windows. The robot must grasp and lift a cube based on a 19-dimensional state observation, with each action chunk containing four seven-dimensional end-effector and gripper commands ( d = 28 ). OGBench Cube-Triple Task 4 (Park et al., 2025a) uses the cube-triple-play-v0 dataset and requires the robot to move three cubes to a target configuration. The policy maps a 46-dimensional state observation to a chunk of five commands, each with five control coordinates. Both tasks use To = 1 and Tp = Ta: the robot executes the full predicted chunk before replanning. 

Policies and training. PrT-Flow applies four orthogonal JTT heads per update, starting from a Gaussian base distribution and using a final tanh transformation to map outputs to the normalized action bounds. On Lift, we use 16 sampling steps with CET and evaluate the exponential moving average (EMA) weights after 300 training epochs. On Cube-Triple, we use four sampling steps and train the randomly initialized policy and critics for 30,000 paired offline iterations, followed by 600,000 primitive environment steps of online learning. The main configuration uses finite-step CET at the four sampling grid times and an online action-matching weight of λ = 100 . Appendix A gives the hyperparameters and action preprocessing details. 

Baselines and metrics. The Lift reference is a denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) policy trained on the same data, with the same action horizons and evaluation episodes. On Cube-Triple, we compare against Flow-G, the gated velocity policy from the SAC-Flow family (Zhang et al., 2025a), with sampling noise standard deviations σ = 0 .01 and 0.05 . All three runs use 30k paired offline iterations and 600k online primitive steps. Flow-G samples with a gated MLP and added noise, whereas PrT-Flow uses deterministic JTT updates; all three use the same raw demonstration corpus. Success rate is the fraction of episodes satisfying the environment’s success criterion, and mean return is the average undiscounted episode reward. 12 Each evaluation comprises 50 episodes, with a maximum of 500 steps per episode on Lift and 1,000 on Cube-Triple. We report Lift results after 300 training epochs and evaluate Cube-Triple every 10k online steps, reporting results at 600k steps.

5.4 Discussion 

The analysis shows how PrT-Flow computes the action probabilities defined by the finite-step sampling procedure used at deployment. JTT provides analytic Jacobian corrections through invertible finite maps, while CET supervises finite displacements at the deployment step size and relates this objective to marginal Flow Matching regression over the same map family. The experiments show that the invertible and causal structure needed for this computation still permits effective robot behavior: PrT-Flow achieves high imitation success on Lift and supports offline-to-online actor–critic learning on Cube-Triple with the same action generation and probability evaluation procedure. Thus, one policy can combine multi-step Flow Matching generation with an explicit probability density for the actions it executes. PrT-Flow and methods such as SAC-Flow differ in how they obtain policy probabilities. SAC-Flow intro-duces random perturbations into finite-step sampling to define probabilities over sampling paths. PrT-Flow applies deterministic, invertible maps after sampling the initial Gaussian noise, computing the probability density of the final action chunk directly using the change-of-variables formula. In our Flow-G experiments (Figure 3), changing only the sampling noise standard deviation from σ = 0 .01 to 0.05 produces markedly different learning trajectories and fixed-budget performance, indicating that the noise scale itself can substan-tially affect training. PrT-Flow does not need stepwise noise to compute action probabilities, so it requires no additional sampling noise scale σ for this purpose. Its high online control performance on Cube-Triple shows that explicit finite-step action densities can support effective policy learning without a stochastic path-based probability construction. This finite-step probability computation also provides a basis for extensions beyond SAC’s action-entropy objective. Inverting the transformations allows the same policy to re-evaluate a given action under updated parameters, making it naturally applicable to likelihood ratios, KL regularization, importance weighting, and replay-action rescoring. Extensions to longer prediction horizons, probability densities of executed action 15 prefixes, visually conditioned policies, and real robots would help establish the scope of probability-tractable Flow Matching as a general generative policy representation. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We introduced PrT-Flow to make the probability density of a robot’s executed action chunk directly com-putable while preserving multimodal multi-step Flow Matching generation. The central idea is to make the finite sampling procedure itself invertible with tractable Jacobian determinants, so that action probabilities can be computed directly from the sampling steps. JTT combines causal monotone splines and orthogonal coordinate transformations to accumulate density changes in the same forward pass that generates an action; CET supervises finite displacements at the deployment step size, aligning training with the sampling maps actually used. Proposition 4.2 establishes that, at a fixed step size and within the same finite map family, CET and marginal Flow Matching regression have the same set of minimizing parameters. The experiments show that the structural constraints needed to track probabilities do not prevent effective robot learning: PrT-Flow achieves 48 successes in 50 imitation-learning evaluation episodes on Robomimic Lift and serves as the action generator in offline-to-online SAC-style learning on OGBench Cube-Triple, also reaching 48 successes in 50 final evaluation episodes. These results provide evidence that a Flow Matching policy can retain multi-step generation while explicitly evaluating the probability density of the actions it executes. Constraining the finite sampling maps allows a single policy to provide both capabilities. PrT-Flow thus provides a broader basis for studying generative robot policies: the same finite-step model generates actions from noise and returns their explicit probability densities. Future work can exploit this property across a wider range of policy optimization methods. Finite-step generative policies with tractable probabilities may thereby offer a general policy representation that connects the expressive capacity of Flow Matching with likelihood-based reinforcement learning.
