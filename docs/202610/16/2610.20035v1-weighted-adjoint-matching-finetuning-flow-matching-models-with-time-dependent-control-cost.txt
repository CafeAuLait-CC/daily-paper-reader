# Weighted Adjoint Matching: Finetuning Flow Matching Models with Time-Dependent Control Cost 

Eliot Beyler Francis Bach 

Inria, Ecole Normale Supérieure, PSL Research University 

Abstract 

We introduce Weighted Adjoint Matching , a method to finetune a pretrained diffusion or flow matching model given a differen-tiable reward. We derive it from Adjoint Matching , which reformulates finetuning as a stochastic optimal control problem with a quadratic control cost. Building on the ob-servation that these models generate coarse features before fine details, we place a time-dependent weight on the control cost. Ahigh cost early in the generative process pre-serves balance and diversity in the coarse features, which carry most of the seman-tics, while a low cost at the end lets the model increase the reward by refining details. We prove that our method is equivalent to maximizing the reward under time-varying Kullback–Leibler regularization on the gener-ative paths. Experiments on Gaussian mix-tures and on MNIST show a better tradeoff between reward and mode balance than Ad-joint Matching. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Diffusion models [7, 10, 11, 14] and flow matching models [20, 17, 21] are now the state-of-the-art meth-ods for learning and sampling a distribution known from a large dataset of empirical samples. They work by approximating a denoising process, modeled as an ordinary or stochastic differential equation whose drift is learned with score matching [5]. In this article, we focus on the task of finetuning a pretrained flow match-ing model, denoting ptarget the distribution on Rd it samples from.

Coarse-to-fine generation in diffusion and flow matching models. To address this issue, we rely on the fact that for natural data such as images, diffu-sion and flow matching models generate coarse features (the outline of an image) before fine ones (details and textures) [34]. This phenomenon can be understood by looking at the frequency domain [23, 29]: for natural images the magnitude of the (discrete) Fourier trans-form decays with frequency, whereas it is flat for Gaus-sian white noise, so progressively adding noise erases

The fact that features of high magnitude are generated before those of low magni-tude can be understood within the literature on mode speciation . This approach to the question asks what it means for a feature to be “generated” at a given point in the process: past a certain time in the generative process, the feature is already determined, in the sense that almost all trajectories through the current state terminate in samples sharing it [22, 26, 38, 39]. In par-ticular, Li and Chen [31] give non-asymptotic results and analyze hierarchical data in which features live at different scales, showing that each feature emerges in a critical window of time, from features at large spatial scales to features at small spatial scales.

2 RELATED WORK 

Early works aim to increase the expected value of the reward Ep[r(X)] , often with some form of implicit reg-ularization to limit collapse, when a model only gener-ates very similar samples of high reward, but without characterizing the finetuned distribution. Xu et al. [24] introduce ImageReward , a model trained on human preferences to score images, together with a finetuning method (ReFL) that backpropagates the reward gradi-ent through a randomly selected step of the sampling process. Clark et al. [28] improve upon it by back-propagating through the last K steps of the process. Several methods (see, e.g., [27, 18]) cast the finetuning of a diffusion model as reinforcement learning, viewing the denoising chain as a Markov decision process (see [32] for a literature review). However, both Uehara et al. [33] and Domingo-Enrich et al. [35] discuss the fact that reinforcement learning with KL regulariza-tion at each step does not in general sample from (2). Uehara et al. [33] take this into account by tilting the initial distribution, which requires an auxiliary model. Domingo-Enrich et al. [35] prove that the solution of an SOC problem with quadratic control cost recov-ers (2), provided the base process uses a particular noise schedule. Another work by Potaptchik, Lee, and Albergo [47] progressively tilts the velocity field of the base generative model to match that of the tilted dis-tribution (2). Marion et al. [37] propose a general framework to finetune different sampling algorithms, including unadjusted Langevin sampling and diffusion models, and in particular for the objective (1). 

> method, we have full flexibility to choose the timesteps that we penalize more, e.g., modify the coarse features and preserve the details, or penalize a specific frequency range.

Some reward-finetuning methods treat the timesteps of the generative process non-uniformly as we do, but for reasons of efficiency or of gradient quality rather than to preserve diversity. As recalled above, Xu et al. [24] backpropagate the reward through a single, ran-domly selected step, chosen among the last steps of the sampling process, where they report the strongest reward signal. Clark et al. [28] likewise restrict the backward pass to the last K steps. He et al. [42] and Lee and Ye [44] also weight timesteps non-uniformly, but inside GRPO-style policy-gradient objectives that use only scalar rewards, and with the goal of stabi-lizing credit assignment across steps. Note also that Han, Razaviyayn, and Xu [36] regularize each step of the sampling process with its own KL coefficient in their mathematical derivations, but only use a fixed coefficient in their experiments. Closer to our motivation, Barceló, Alcázar, and Tobar [25] start from the same observation as we do, build-ing on the works on coarse-to-fine sampling in diffu-sion models recalled above, and argue that treating the timesteps differently, in particular the early ones, may increase the diversity of the finetuned model by pre-venting mode collapse. Similarly, after noticing that diversity mostly depends on early steps in the gener-ative process, Liu et al. [45] propose a GRPO-based finetuning method that enforces stronger regulariza-tion on those steps. Both these methods are nonethe-less based on a reinforcement-learning framework that only uses the reward as a scalar signal, and they do not characterize the finetuned distribution. Guo et al. [41] build like us upon adjoint matching. They replace the stochastic sampler by a deterministic one, which they claim to be more efficient, but therefore also lose the characterization of the finetuned distribution. They also truncate the finetuning to the last steps of the trajectory, motivated by efficiency rather than diver-sity. This differs from our approach: not computing the loss on the early timesteps does not enforce that the finetuned velocity field stays equal to the base one at those times, since a single neural network is shared across timesteps. Our method instead ensures explic-itly that the finetuned model stays close to the base model at the times where the weight w(t) is large. Finally, Domingo-Enrich and Han [40] generalize ad-joint matching to a large class of stochastic optimal control objectives, with control-dependent drift and diffusion coefficients and convex running costs. Our time-weighted objective (10) can be partially fitted into this framework, which recovers our weighted ad-joint matching loss only for a particular choice of the stabilizing weight λ(t). ∥x − y∥2 (see, e.g., [9, 19, 12, 46]). Every sample takes one proximal step on the re-ward, which roughly preserves the mode weights, but may drive samples out of the data manifold. For ex-ample, on MNIST with the brightness reward, the gra-dient of r is the constant white image: the background turns gray instead of the digits getting brighter. 

What is a good reward? Some rewards, such as the average luminosity on MNIST of Section 5.2, are very easy to define and to interpret, but have two caveats: they can be increased by going in a direction orthogo-nal to the data manifold (e.g., making the black back-ground of MNIST digits turn gray) and are not mean-ingful for comparing samples far apart (dark eights are brighter than bright ones). We believe that this cannot be easily solved by changing the reward. Re-wards are contextual, and the values have little mean-ing by themselves: one needs to add the knowledge of the base distribution. KL regularization addresses the first caveat, since it forces reward maximization to take place on the data manifold, but not the second: it has no notion of distance within the support, so mass can move between distant modes. Our method, using the link between spatial resolution and time in diffusion models, addresses the second caveat. 

Future work. We have focused on the case of un-conditional generative modeling and simple datasets for which balance could be evaluated precisely. Future work could be dedicated to scaling this work to con-ditional flow matching models on large image datasets beyond MNIST and to other types of data used in practical applications.
