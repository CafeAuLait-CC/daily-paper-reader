## ABSTRACT 

Pretrained latent spaces trade compression, reconstruction, and the learnability of the coordinates a downstream generator has to work in, which complicates adapting these spaces to different domains or tasks: continued training is expensive, and without a new alignment objective it often fails to improve downstream generation. We instead leave the encoder and decoder fixed and change only the coordinates between them, using an invertible bi-Lipschitz adapter aligned offline to a semantic teacher and frozen during class-conditional flow matching. Invertibility preserves compatibility with the pretrained autoencoder, while the bi-Lipschitz constraint limits how far alignment can distort its latent geometry. This provides a simple way to improve semantic organization while retaining the compression and recon-struction properties of the original latent space. On two medical imaging datasets, constrained latent adaptation improves feature metrics, seed stability and counter-factual edit strength, while remaining competitive on downstream classification. In contrast, unconstrained adaptation can become highly anisotropic and sensitive despite remaining invertible, highlighting the importance of controlling geomet-ric distortion. Our results suggest that carefully constrained post-hoc adaptation offers a parameter-efficient and tuning-free alternative to jointly modifying the representation during generative training. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Latent generative models, including latent diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022) and flow matching (Lip-man et al., 2023), operate on compressed representations produced by pretrained variational au-toencoders (VAEs) (Kingma & Welling, 2013). VAEs are typically optimized for a rate-distortion trade-off between compression and reconstruction fidelity (Alemi et al., 2018; Bozkurt et al., 2021). However, a representation that reconstructs well is not necessarily easy for a downstream model to learn (Skorokhodov et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2026; Dombrowski et al., 2026). Latent representation design therefore involves a third axis: downstream learnability . We define learnability as the ease with which a bounded-capacity learner can recover task-relevant structure from a representation under the measurement process used by the downstream pipeline. It depends not only on which information the representation retains, but also on how that information is arranged (Yao et al., 2025; Black Forest Labs, 2025). Fine-tuning the autoencoder can adapt the representation, but it also changes the decoder coordinates, requires a new latent cache, and adds a costly adaptation stage, with the adapted latents often still showing low learnability (Dombrowski et al., 2026). Meanwhile, methods such as REPA (Yu et al., 2025) align features inside the generative model during training. They improve the generator instead of the latent representation and repeat the alignment cost for each training run. We study a post-hoc alternative: adapt the latent coordinates once, then reuse them across flow-matching models. We place a spatial invertible adapter between the frozen autoencoder and the generator. The adapter is trained offline on cached latents using features from a frozen semantic teacher. Flow matching consumes the adapted latents, while the adapter inverse returns generated samples to the coordinates expected by the pretrained decoder. Under exact inversion, the encoder-decoder reconstruction mapping remains unchanged. For teacher alignment, we measure linear Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) (Kornblith et al., 2019), which only compares the relative similarity matrices of the two spaces. This matters because the adapted latent and the teacher embedding have different, very high dimensionalities and no shared coordinate system. We evaluate generation on two medical imaging datasets, using sample quality, semantic coverage, train-on-synthetic utility and counterfactual generation. Comparisons include the unchanged latent space, online generator alignment, autoencoder adaptation, and less constrained invertible maps. Our contributions are: 1. We propose a post-hoc latent adapter that is invertible and bi-Lipschitz by construction, aligned offline to a semantic teacher via CKA and frozen during flow matching. It leaves the autoencoder and the flow-matching objective unchanged, while a single scalar λ bounds its distortion in both directions with a closed-form certificate. 2. We make the adapter tuning-free: λ can be estimated from a necessary condition on teacher triplets and the teacher from label geometry on training data, both fixed before any generator is trained. On both datasets the teacher choice agrees with the downstream results, and the method works across an eightfold range of λ.3. On our tested datasets, the adapter is competitive with online generator alignment and autoencoder adaptation, has the lowest seed variance in domain FID among the adaptation methods, and produces stronger counterfactual edits, at 405 × fewer parameters than REPA’s projection head and a fraction of the compute of autoencoder fine-tuning. 4. We show that invertibility alone is insufficient: invertible maps without Lipschitz control become nearly rank-deficient and fail as generators, and adapting only the clean endpoint instead of the whole flow path gives good but unreliable runs. 2Preprint 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Prior work improves latent generation by modifying the encoder-decoder pair, aligning representations inside the generator, or transforming an existing latent space. We study the latter via the additional requirement that the transformation remains invertible and well-conditioned. 

Semantic alignment for latent generation. To semantically align the latents, Yao et al. (2025) and Xu et al. (2025) train visual tokenizers with semantic priors in VA-VAE and ReaLS, while in EQ-VAE, the autoencoder is fine-tuned with an equivariance regularizer (Kouzelis et al., 2025). Meanwhile, other semantic alignment procedures leave the VAE unchanged. One prominent example is REPA, which aligns intermediate denoiser states with features from a frozen vision encoder during generator training (Yu et al., 2025). Singh et al. (2025) modify REPA to emphasize the spatial structure of the teacher features. Unlike prior alternatives, we leave both the VAEs and the flow matching objective untouched. 

Invertible latent transformations. Invertible networks can be grouped into a few architectural families: coupling layers (Dinh et al., 2015; 2017; Kingma & Dhariwal, 2018), autoregressive maps (Kingma et al., 2016; Papamakarios et al., 2017), invertible residual blocks (Behrmann et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2019), and continuous-time flows (Grathwohl et al., 2019). Most are trained as normalizing flows, where invertibility is what makes the objective computable at all: maximum likelihood needs the change-of-variables term log | det J|, so the map has to be invertible and its Jacobian determinant tractable. Applied to latents, this buys richer variational posteriors (Rezende & Mohamed, 2015; Kingma et al., 2016) or a density fitted to the codes of a pretrained autoencoder (Xiao et al., 2019; B¨ ohm & Seljak, 2022). In each case the map transports one distribution onto another. In our case, the invertible map is used as a reversible change of latent coordinates and not as a density model. The adapter aligns encoded latents with a semantic teacher, while its inverse returns generated latents to the coordinates expected by the frozen decoder. Because the objective does not require unrestricted transport, we constrain the map to remain close to an isometry and limit the distortion of the pretrained latent geometry. To this end, we construct the adapter from invertible residual blocks (Behrmann et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2019) with joint control of expansion and contraction. Related work certifies monotone residual layers with separate lower and upper Lipschitz bounds (Wang et al., 2024). Meanwhile, we tie them to expose a single distortion budget.

## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

5.1 SETUP AND EVALUATION 

Datasets. Our main dataset is a single-label filtered subset of MIMIC-CXR (Johnson et al., 2019), consisting of eight disease classes split between 66,586 train samples, 8,261 validation samples and 8,361 test samples. As alternative medical modality, we additionally report results for a single-label slices split of CT-RATE (Hamamci et al., 2026), consisting of 13,309 train samples, 2,229 validation samples and 6,767 test samples across 15 disease classes. Both datasets exhibit heavily long-tailed class distributions. 

Metrics. On these datasets, we measure the sample quality using Fr´ echet Inception Distance (FID) (Heusel et al., 2018) and the diversity via the Image Retrieval Score (IRS) (Dombrowski et al., 2025). We report these measures in independent domain-specific feature spaces and domain-unspecific spaces, to capture both image realism and fidelity. The domain-specific extractors are a CheXNet-style DenseNet-121 classifier (Huang et al., 2017; Rajpurkar et al., 2017) for MIMIC-CXR and a DenseNet-121 classifier trained on the CT-RATE training split for CT-RATE. For general realism, we use DINOv2 features (Oquab et al., 2023). For downstream utility, we train a DenseNet-121 on the synthetic images and evaluate it on the real test set (TSTR). Since the datasets are long-tailed, we report macro F1 and balanced accuracy. For counterfactual edits, we use a TorchXRayVision (Cohen et al., 2022) DenseNet-121 as the oracle for edit strength. To measure minimality, we report LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) to the factual and the change of the non-targeted findings, and we measure target realism by the FID to real images of the target class. 

Training & inference. We use FLUX.2 latents for training the models, since Dombrowski et al. (2026) showed that they exhibit the best overall learnability among the representations they tested. The adapters are trained for 200 epochs, which is a crude heuristic choice, since the loss usually saturates after only a couple of epochs. However, adapter matching is cheap: the adapter only consists of 36 ,871 parameters (about 405 times fewer than the REPA projection head), so even 200 epochs take less compute than the autoencoder-adaptation baselines, which fine-tune the full autoencoder for 5 (VA-VAE, full fine-tuning) or 10 (ReaLS) epochs. Before flow-matching training, every latent representation, including the unadapted baseline, is centered and scaled per channel to standard deviation 0.5 using statistics from its training cache. This fixed affine preprocessing is inverted before applying g−1 

> θ

and decoding. The FM models are trained on MIMIC-CXR for 10 flow-matching epochs, while CT-RATE uses 30 

due to its lower number of samples. Each model is trained with seeds 0, 1 and 2, without classifier-free guidance. Every generator produces three seeded, class-balanced sets of 50 ,000 images per FM seed, which are used for the feature metrics and as TSTR training sets. 5.2 MAIN RESULTS

## 6 DISCUSSION 

The results show that a small, frozen change of coordinates is competitive with methods that retrain the autoencoder or alter generator training, while being more stable across seeds than the other adaptation methods. Three properties make our approach practical: it has a single hyperparameter with a direct meaning, the distortion budget λ, which can be estimated from the data before training and which works across an eightfold range. The teacher can likewise be chosen from training data alone, and on both datasets this choice agreed with the downstream results. Finally, the adapter is cheap: it is fitted once in a few GPU-hours, reused by every flow-matching run and inverted in under a millisecond, and it leaves the autoencoder and the flow-matching loss untouched. The ablations indicate where the stability comes from. Invertibility alone is not enough, since invertible maps without Lipschitz control become nearly rank-deficient and fail as generators, and adapting only the clean endpoint gives good but unreliable runs. Bounding the distortion in both directions and keeping the whole path in the adapted coordinates makes the gains repeatable. 

Limitations. We implemented the autoencoder-adaptation baselines with a medical teacher and their recommended settings, but more tuning could strengthen some of them, although needing it is itself a cost that can be avoided with our method. The adapter can only impose structure that its teacher carries, so it depends on a teacher with a good label geometry for the target domain, as the CT-RATE teacher comparison shows. Finally, the stronger counterfactual edits come with slightly larger off-target changes, so the adapter mainly improves edit strength. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We introduced an invertible bi-Lipschitz adapter that aligns pretrained latents with a semantic teacher offline and stays frozen during flow matching. On MIMIC-CXR and CT-RATE, it is competitive with generator alignment and autoencoder adaptation, more stable across seeds, and a small fraction of their cost. Its only hyperparameter can be estimated from the data, and its teacher can be selected before any generator is trained. Constrained post-hoc adaptation therefore offers a simple way to 9Preprint make a fixed latent space easier to learn, without retraining the autoencoder or changing the generative objective. ## A ADAPTER CONSTRUCTION 

Per-layer constants. Each residual branch ( ri) consists of ( 1 × 1) pointwise and ( 3 × 3) depthwise convolutions interleaved with SiLU activations. For pointwise convolutions, we rescale each weight matrix to a fixed Frobenius norm, which upper bounds its spectral norm. For depthwise convolutions, we rescale each kernel to a fixed ( ℓ1) norm, which upper bounds the induced convolution operator norm by Young’s inequality. Let ( LSiLU ≈ 1.1) denote the Lipschitz constant of SiLU (Zhang et al., 2026). We normalize every hidden convolution to have operator norm at most ( 1/L SiLU ); consequently, the composition of each hidden convolution with its subsequent SiLU activation is (1)-Lipschitz. The output convolution is instead normalized to have operator norm at most ( tanh λi). Multiplying these per-layer bounds therefore gives Lip( ri) ≤ tanh λi. This bound is enforced directly through the weight normalizations, requires no power iteration, and holds both at initialization and after every parameter update. 

Bijectivity of Rm. The singular-value characterization in Section 3 requires a diffeomorphism of 

Rm onto Rm, not onto its image, so injectivity alone is not enough. Contraction supplies the rest. For any target y the map u 7 → y − ri(u) contracts Rm with factor tanh λi < 1, so the Banach fixed-point theorem gives a unique u with u + ri(u) = y, and x = u/c i solves Bi(x) = y. Every block is therefore a bijection of Rm onto itself. The Cayley-parameterized mixers are orthogonal and hence isometries of Rm onto itself, so the composition inherits both invertibility and surjectivity. 

Numerical inversion. We invert each block by the fixed-point iteration b ← y − s r i(b) implied by the same contraction, capped at 50 steps with a tolerance of 10 −6, where s is the block’s gate. Table 8 gives the realized round-trip error and the per-sample latency.

## C TEACHER SELECTION AND QUALITATIVE SAMPLES 

C.1 TEACHER SELECTION 

We fixed the teacher of each dataset before training any adapter or generator, using only the training split. For every candidate we drew the same 8,000 single-label TRAIN images (seed 0) and measured three properties of its embedding. Label geometry is the linear CKA between the embedding and the one-hot label matrix, reported relative to a 32 × 32 pixel thumbnail (the pixel floor) and relative to the flattened FLUX.2 latents that the adapter receives. The second ratio bounds how much label structure a CKA objective can add to the latents. Retrieval is the same-class rate among the 10 

nearest neighbours, excluding neighbours from the same patient (MIMIC-CXR) or the same volume (CT-RATE); it is scored on queries that carry a finding ( 2,083 and 5,191 queries, random baselines 

0.047 and 0.100 ). Confound rate is the fraction of those neighbours that come from the same patient or volume, where lower is better. We selected the embedding that leads on label geometry and retrieval. Table 9 lists every candidate. The two datasets select different teachers. On MIMIC-CXR MedImageInsight leads MedSigLIP by a small margin in label geometry ( 6.18 × against 5.66 × the latents) and a large one in retrieval.
