## ABSTRACT 

In maximum entropy and behavior-constrained reinforcement learning (RL), the optimal policy is given by a Boltzmann distribution. Generating samples from this distribution can be challenging, as it is often complex and multimodal. While flow policies trained with flow matching provide a powerful framework for represent-ing such distributions, they traditionally rely on access to training data, which is generally unavailable in online RL. To address this limitation, we propose fixed-point flow matching (FFM), a data-free procedure for training and fine-tuning flow policies in RL. FFM leverages the target flow identity to formulate the actor up-date as a fixed-point iteration, utilizing a flow-matching-style regression objective where the critic’s action gradients serve as regression targets. We show that the optimal policy is a fixed point of this iteration. Through extensive experiments, we demonstrate that FFM effectively fine-tunes pretrained flow policies in the offline-to-online setting and enables efficient and accurate training of flow poli-cies from scratch in online RL. These results establish fixed-point flow matching as a principled approach for learning expressive flow-based policies without re-quiring training data. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Across a range of reinforcement learning (RL) formulations, the improved policy is available in closed form as a Boltzmann distribution of the current critic. In maximum entropy RL, π⋆(x | s) ∝

exp( Q(s, x )/λ ) is optimal for the policy improvement step of soft policy iteration (Ziebart et al., 2008; Haarnoja et al., 2017; 2018); in KL-regularized and behavior-constrained RL, which includes offline-to-online fine-tuning, the same expression appears tilted by a reference policy (Peters et al., 2010; Abdolmaleki et al., 2018; Peng et al., 2019; Nair et al., 2020). This target distribution can be complex and multimodal whenever several distinct action choices are viable, but the classical actor– critic recipe projects it onto a Gaussian due to the simplicity of the resulting optimization problem and the tractability of the policy’s log-likelihood (Haarnoja et al., 2018). Flow and diffusion models (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden, 2023; Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021) remove this representational restriction and have become the policy class of choice wherever a dataset is available (Chi et al., 2023; Reuss et al., 2023; Janner et al., 2022; Park et al., 2025b). Yet, the stable training objectives that are used to train these models are not directly usable for online RL due to a lack of samples from π⋆. Existing methods for training flow and diffusion models in the on-policy RL setting often require backpropagating the critic through the integrator (Wang et al., 2023; Ding & Jin, 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b; Celik et al., 2025; Le et al., 2026), whose memory cost grows with every integration step and quickly becomes prohibitive, or rely on importance-weighted regression (Ding et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2025; Dong et al., 2025), whose expo-nential critic weights have high variance and lead to unstable training updates. Moreover, methods for fine-tuning flow models typically use stochastic optimal control, which requires converting the flow into a diffusion model in order to use the adjoint method (Domingo-Enrich et al., 2025; Li & Levine, 2026; Dong et al., 2026b). The adjoint method stores each sampled trajectory and integrates an adjoint state backward along it, leading to complex and expensive update schemes.

• The target flow identity and target flow matching (Section 4). We derive an identity that ex-presses the velocity field of a Boltzmann target as a conditional expectation of the gradient of its log-density, and turn it into target flow matching, an alternative training objective to flow matching that shares its population minimizer but regresses onto the gradient of the target rather than onto the displacement of the data. • Fixed-point flow matching (Section 4). We instantiate FFM for maximum entropy RL and for offline-to-online fine-tuning. We validate both across 50 offline-to-online tasks and 24 online tasks (Sec-tion 6), where FFM is competitive with or better than the strongest available baselines. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Reinforcement learning with diffusion and flow policies. Diffusion and flow models entered RL through imitation learning and offline RL, where a dataset makes their standard training objectives 2Preprint applicable and the critic enters through weighting, guidance, or gradients through the sampling chain (Janner et al., 2022; Chi et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Hansen-Estruch et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2023; Kang et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024; Ding & Jin, 2024; Park et al., 2025b). In online RL there are no samples of the target policy, and existing methods differ in how the critic drives the actor update. One line backpropagates the critic value through the sampling chain (Wang et al., 2023; Ding & Jin, 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b), which is memory-intensive and unstable for many integration steps. A second line uses critic-weighted regression, interpreting the denoising loss as a variational bound or as a mirror-descent step (Ding et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2025; Dong et al., 2025), and a third estimates policy gradients through the denoising process, typ-ically under a trust region (Ren et al., 2025; McAllister et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025a; Le et al., 2026; Lv et al., 2026). Closest to FFM are methods that use the action gradient of the critic: DIPO (Yang et al., 2023) clones actions improved by gradient ascent on Q; QSM (Psenka et al., 2024) regresses the score onto ∇xQ evaluated at the noisy action, a plug-in approximation of the target score identity that is exact only at the clean end; and RFM (Li et al., 2026) estimates the exact posterior-mean target by self-normalized importance sampling, using Langevin–Stein control vari-ates to interpolate between Q-weighted noise averages and Q-weighted averages of ∇xQ, which allows it to train flow policies as well. Maximum entropy RL with generative policies additionally requires an entropy estimate: DACER (Wang et al., 2024) fits a Gaussian mixture, DIME (Celik et al., 2025) and MaxEntDP (Dong et al., 2025) use a lower bound and numerical integration, re-spectively, Sanokowski & Patil (2025) augment the MDP with the denoising steps, FLAG (Kim et al., 2026) uses importance sampling in the latent space of the flow, and AMDP (Thilges et al., 2026) amortizes the entropy-regularized update with adjoint matching. FFM obtains the entropy from the divergence of the velocity field it trains, using the instantaneous change of variables. 

Fine-tuning and offline-to-online RL. Reward fine-tuning of pretrained diffusion and flow mod-els has been cast as policy gradient over the denoising chain (Fan et al., 2023; Black et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025b) and as entropy-regularized stochastic optimal control (Uehara et al., 2024; Domingo-Enrich et al., 2025), with the KL divergence to the pretrained model as regularizer; Uehara et al. (2025) review the corresponding inference-time methods. The same behavior-tilted target governs offline-to-online RL with flow policies. FQL (Park et al., 2025b) distills the flow into a one-step policy that is trained by reparameterized critic gradients, DSRL (Wagenmaker et al., 2025) keeps the flow fixed and learns a policy over its latent noise, ReinFlow and FINO (Zhang et al., 2025a; Shin et al., 2026b) inject noise into the flow to obtain tractable likelihoods and exploration, and Q-Flow (Doo et al., 2026) propagates terminal values to intermediate states of the flow. QAM (Li & Levine, 2026) imports adjoint matching: the pretrained flow is converted into a memoryless SDE and critic gradients are transported backward along sampled trajectories by the lean adjoint ODE, and TRQAM (Dong et al., 2026b) stabilizes it with a path-space trust region. Concurrent work regresses velocity corrections onto critic gradients (Wang et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026; Ziakas et al., 2026) or steers a fixed flow policy at test time (Zhou et al., 2026; Ruan et al., 2026). FFM fine-tunes the pretrained flow directly in velocity space, requires neither SDE conversion nor adjoint simulation, and its behavior-tilted target has the KL-regularized optimum as a fixed point (Section E). Beyond the RL literature, FFM shares the fixed-point structure of the matching objectives used to train diffusion and flow models from unnormalized densities, and of adjoint matching; Section C discusses these methods, together with further critic-driven policy updates.

## 5 APPLICATIONS IN REINFORCEMENT LEARNING 

Offline-to-online RL. In offline-to-online RL, one commonly pre-trains a flow policy vref by flow matching Equation (6) and a critic by an offline method on a fixed dataset. The policy is then fine-tuned online, where it must stay close to the pretrained flow while it improves, since offline critics are reliable only near the data. This motivates the behavior-constrained objective of Section 3. Prior work for fine-tuning flow policies typically converts the flow to a diffusion model with a specific (memoryless) noise schedule and leverages stochastic optimal control to compute adjoint states, which yield a stable regression objective for training (Domingo-Enrich et al., 2025; Li & Levine, 2026; Dong et al., 2026b). In contrast, FFM requires neither a conversion to a diffusion model nor the computation of adjoint states. Keeping the policy close to the pretrained policy is commonly realized by considering the behavior-tilted target policy π⋆ ∝ pref eQ/λ , where pref is the terminal distribution of the pretrained flow policy (Uehara et al., 2024; Domingo-Enrich et al., 2025). Correlated exploration. A flow policy is a deterministic function of its prior draw, and hence, integrating Equation (5) from X0 = σ0ε yields the action x = Ψ θ (ε | s), whose only source of randomness is ε ∼ N (·; 0 , I ). Here, Ψθ (· | s) is simply the sampler of the flow policy: the map that takes a prior draw ε and returns the clean action obtained by integrating the ODE with the learned velocity field. Hence, exploration can also be shaped by correlating the prior draws across environment steps. Similar to colored-noise exploration for Gaussian policies (Eberhard et al., 2023), we draw the priors within an episode from a stationary Ornstein–Uhlenbeck chain with correlation time τou , i.e.,

We use it only for the hard-exploration tasks of Section D.4. Other colored noise processes, such as the pink noise of Eberhard et al. (2023), can be used as well, which we leave to future work. 

## 6 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate FFM in two settings: offline-to-online fine-tuning, and online on-policy maximum entropy RL, where we train a flow policy from scratch. For the offline-to-online setting, we focus on the ten OGBench (Park et al., 2025a) locomotion and manipulation domains totaling 50 tasks. For the online RL setting, we consider the GPU-accelerated MJX (Zakka et al., 2025) Playground and humanoid tasks, which result in 24 tasks in total. ## 7 CONCLUSION 

We introduced fixed-point flow matching, a data-free procedure for training flow policies using a flow-matching-style regression objective. Its basis is the target flow identity, which expresses the velocity field as a conditional average of Q-scores at clean actions and thereby replaces the data-derived targets of flow matching by critic gradients. Evaluating the resulting objective on samples from the current policy instead of the optimal policy yields a fixed-point iteration that has the optimal policy as a fixed point. The construction is conceptually simple, easy to implement, and needs no gradients through the sampling chain, no conversion of the flow into a stochastic differential equation, and no adjoint simulation. We consider two instantiations of the proposed framework: an online maximum entropy actor–critic algorithm, and offline-to-online fine-tuning against a behavior-tilted target. Across 50 offline-to-online tasks and 24 online tasks, both are competitive with or better than the strongest available baselines. 

Limitations. FFM requires a differentiable target. The regression target is built from the Q-score 

∇xQ(s, x ), so the critic must be differentiable in the action and the policy update is only as ac-curate as the critic’s gradient. This restricts FFM to continuous action spaces. Extensions to non-differentiable targets and discrete action spaces are left for future work.

## C EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

This appendix complements Section 2. The first paragraph places FFM in the family of matching objectives that train diffusion and flow models from an unnormalized target rather than from sam-ples, which is where its fixed-point structure comes from. The second collects work on critic-driven updates of diffusion and flow policies that Section 2 groups but has no room to discuss individually. 

Fixed-point methods for diffusion and flow models. Training a diffusion or flow model from an unnormalized density instead of samples is the problem of neural samplers. Early approaches minimize a path-space divergence by simulating the generative process (Zhang & Chen, 2022; Var-gas et al., 2023). Matching-based samplers instead regress onto a target computed from the en-ergy and its gradient: iDEM (Akhound-Sadegh et al., 2024), iEFM (Woo & Ahn, 2024), BNEM (OuYang et al., 2026), and EWFM (Dern et al., 2025) estimate the noised score, velocity, or energy by importance-weighted Monte Carlo, target score matching (De Bortoli et al., 2024), on which FFM builds, writes the noised score as a conditional expectation of the clean-data score but requires samples of the target, and tilt matching (Potaptchik et al., 2025) matches the velocity of a reward-tilted interpolant without reward gradients. These methods are iterative: the target is evaluated with samples of the current model and the regression is repeated, which is the fixed-point structure that Theorem 3 makes explicit for critic-driven targets. Adjoint matching (Domingo-Enrich et al., 2025) forms a second family of such regressions. Proposed for reward fine-tuning, it has since been used for sampling from unnormalized densities (Havens et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a; Mosso et al., 2026), for discrete generative models (So et al., 2026), for faster fine-tuning pipelines (Guo et al., 2026; Shin et al., 2026a), and in RL for policy extraction (Li & Levine, 2026; Dong et al., 2026b; Ghanem & Ghogho, 2026) and maximum entropy RL (Thilges et al., 2026); its theory has been connected to the stochastic maximum principle (Domingo-Enrich & Han, 2026) and unified with sampling (Domingo-Enrich et al., 2026). The bridge matching sampler (Blessing et al., 2026) shows that these matching objectives, including adjoint sampling and ASBS, are fixed-point iterations rooted in Nelson’s relation and stabilizes them by damping. FFM belongs to this family with two differ-ences: the target flow identity provides the regression target from critic gradients at clean actions, so no adjoint ODE is needed, and the target itself moves as the critic is learned. 

Further critic-driven policy updates. Sorted by how the critic enters the actor update, the meth-ods of Section 2 fall into a few families, and we record here the entries the main text omits. Methods based on backpropagation through time differentiate the critic through the entire sampling chain: besides those cited in Section 2, Lv et al. (2025) constrain the update relative to a behavior pol-icy. GenPO (Ding et al., 2025) instead applies a PPO-style objective (Schulman et al., 2017) to a diffusion policy whose likelihood is made tractable by a coupling construction. A second family avoids the chain by regressing onto improved actions : DIPO (Yang et al., 2023) and Li et al. (2024) improve the actions stored in the replay buffer by gradient ascent on Q and then clone them with the standard denoising loss. This is the closest relative of FFM among the action-target methods, and the difference is that its regression target is an action rather than a velocity, so the noise level of the interpolant is not accounted for. A third family leaves the denoising loss in place and weights it by the exponentiated critic value (Ding et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2025; Dong et al., 2025), of which one-step flow policy mirror descent (Chen et al., 2025) is the one-step variant, and flow map policies trained by mirror descent (Dong et al., 2026a; Gao et al., 2026) are the multimodal ones. ## D MAXIMUM -ENTROPY REINFORCEMENT LEARNING

Correlated exploration. A flow policy’s key benefit is that the action generation process is a de-terministic mapping up to the sampling of the starting sample. As discussed in Section 5, this allows us to apply a correlated exploration scheme, where the exploration is shaped by correlating the prior draws across the environment steps. To analyze the additional hyperparameter τou that is introduced by Equation (20), we have conducted experiments on single Playground tasks, where the initial instantiation of FFM, i.e., without correlated exploration, performs unsatisfactorily (see Figure 4). Figure 15 shows the learning curves on a subset of tasks that we have picked from the task suite to analyze the effect on performance for varying τou values. We can observe that for each task, there exists at least one τou value that outperforms the initial case without correlated exploration, and only for the FingerSpin and WalkerRun tasks, we observe that a too-high value yields decreased perfor-mance. Especially for the AcrobotSwingupSparse, HopperHop and HopperStand tasks, correlated exploration yields a significant improvement over the case without correlated exploration. In offline-to-online RL a fixed dataset D of transitions is available before any interaction. Two ob-jects are pretrained on it: a behavior flow pref , fit by flow matching Equation (6) to the dataset actions (its velocity field vref is the reference of Section 5), and a critic Q, fit by an offline method (here pessimistic TD, Algorithm 2). Fine-tuning then continues both while the dataset grows with online interaction. The difficulty is that an offline critic is trustworthy only near the data: its extrapolation errors off the support of pref are large and systematically optimistic, so an unconstrained improvement step chases them (Peng et al., 2019; Nair et al., 2020; Park et al., 2025b).
