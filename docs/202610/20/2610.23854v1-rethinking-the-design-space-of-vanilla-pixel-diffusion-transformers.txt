ABSTRACT
Pixel-space diffusion models have recently re-emerged as a compelling alterna-
tive to latent diffusion, avoiding the lossy latent bottleneck and separately trained
autoencoder. JiT shows that a vanilla diffusion transformer can generate directly
in pixel space by replacing the commonly used velocity prediction (u) with clean
image prediction (x0). In this work, we revisit this choice and introduce MFLOW,
an adaptive parameterization that learns to interpolate between x0 and u predic-
tion across principal directions of the data, which can be learned end-to-end with
diffusion model training. We find that the preferred parameterization varies sys-
tematically with the data representation and model capacity. Notably, increasing
model capacity shifts preference toward u prediction from x0 prediction. We
evaluate MFLOW across ImageNet, UCF-101 video, and large-scale text-to-image
generation, demonstrating competitive or improved generative performance across
diverse settings. In addition, we find that vanilla pixel diffusion transformers, ir-
respective of parameterization, retain a persistent high-frequency deficit relative
to real images. To address this, we introduce ConvRefiner, a lightweight convo-
lutional module trained post-hoc with adversarial loss while keeping the diffusion
backbone frozen. With only a small amount of additional training, ConvRefiner
restores the high-frequency statistics of generated images while preserving FID.

INTRODUCTION
Diffusion and flow-based models have become a leading paradigm for visual generation in recent
years. Modern versions of these models predominantly operate in the latent space, obtained from
compressing the pixel-space data with a separately-trained autoencoder. However, latent diffusion

models (LDMs) (Rombach et al. (2022); Peebles & Xie (2023); Ma et al. (2024); Esser et al. (2024);
Yu et al. (2025); Singh et al. (2026)) introduce their own limitations – a lossy bottleneck leads to
unfaithful reconstruction after compression, and there is a need for a separately trained compression
autoencoder, which is typically unaware of the downstream generative objective.
Recent works (Li & He (2026); Chen et al. (2026b); Yu et al. (2026); Mukhopadhyay et al. (2026);
Chen et al. (2025b); Ma et al. (2026b;c); Wang et al. (2026c)) have revived diffusion directly in pixel
space for image generation, removing the aforementioned drawbacks in LDMs. In particular, JiT (Li
& He (2026)), demonstrates that vanilla diffusion transformers can achieve competitive generative
performance directly on raw pixels, eliminating the need for pretrained autoencoders or complicated
architectural modifications. Its aggressive patchification keeps the sequence length manageable, e.g.,
its 16 × 16 patchification produces equivalent number of tokens as a 16× spatial compression VAE
followed by 1 × 1 latent patchification. At the same time, such aggressive patchification poses a
challenge in pixel-space diffusion as each token must model a high-dimensional block of raw pixels,
raising two questions: what prediction parameterization is best suited to such high-dimensional
patches, and can a vanilla large-patch transformer faithfully model fine-scale image details? In this
work, we aim to improve the pixel-space diffusion paradigm along these axes.
JiT addresses the first question with clean-image (x0) prediction. Under the manifold assumption,
clean data lies near a low-dimensional manifold, whereas noise and velocity span the ambient space,
making x0 easier for an undercomplete network to predict. However, sampling requires converting
x0 to u (velocity), which becomes ill-conditioned near the low-noise regime. AsymFlow (Chen
et al. (2026a)) reduces this problem by predicting u in a selected rank-r PCA subspace and x0 in
its complement, keeping the prediction target effectively in a low dimensionality. However, this
hard binary cutoff rank must be chosen for each setting through expensive training sweeps, e.g.,
AsymFlow needed 6 trainings to tune the rank-r. Where as Jin & Wang (2026) use a learnable but
globally shared interpolation between x0 and u, not reducing the effective noise dimensionality.
We introduce MFLOW, an adaptive parameterization with a continuous gain mi ∈[0, 1] for every
principal direction of data. Notably, this differentiable velocity-recovery formulation lets us jointly
train the generator and gains with the standard velocity-space flow-matching loss, letting model
learn the best combination of u and x0 per principal component. MFLOW therefore replaces the
AsymFlow’s manually selected rank and binary mask with a continuous parameterization that adapts
to the data representation, objective, and model capacity. Our experiments show that MFLOW can
be adapted not only to pixel-space image generation, but also to diverse paradigms, like pixel-space
video generation, text-to-image generation at multiple model capacities, and high-dimensional la-
tents like RAEv2 (Singh et al. (2026)). Further visualization of the learned direction-wise gains as
M-curves, a plot of mi’s in decreasing direction variance order, reveal interesting insights, such as
increasing model capacity shifts more directions toward velocity-like prediction.
Prediction parameterization alone does not resolve all limitations of vanilla pixel diffusion trans-
formers. Irrespective of the parameterization, we find that JiT-family models exhibit a persistent
high-frequency deficit relative to real images. This suggests a limitation of the minimalist large-
patch design, where patch tokens are projected directly back to pixels using MLP-layers without an
explicit local refinement pathway. We introduce ConvRefiner, a lightweight residual convolutional
module trained post hoc with perceptual and adversarial supervision while keeping the diffusion
backbone frozen. With only 5% post-hoc training, ConvRefiner brings the generated high-frequency
spectrum closer to that of real images while preserving FID. Overall, this work presents adaptive pre-
diction parameterization and fine-scale signal modeling as complementary directions for improving
vanilla pixel diffusion transformers.
To summarize, the key contributions are:
• We introduce MFLOW, a framework of continuous, direction-adaptive prediction parameteri-
zation whose gains are learned jointly with the generator under a standard velocity-space flow-
matching objective. We show MFLOW is a general framework and can be used for diverse
domains like pixel-space video generation, high-dimensional latents (like RAEv2) in addition
to pixel-space image generation.
• We identify a high-frequency deficit in vanilla JiT-style models and introduce ConvRefiner,
a lightweight post-hoc module that restores high-frequency details while preserving fidelity
matching the frequency spectrum of real images.

RELATED WORK
Pixel-space diffusion and prediction parameterization. Early diffusion models generated directly
in pixel space (Ho et al. (2020); Nichol & Dhariwal (2021); Dhariwal & Nichol (2021); Karras et al.
(2022)), while subsequent works improved the scalability of end-to-end pixel generation through
architectural and formulation advances (Jabri et al. (2023); Hoogeboom et al. (2023; 2025); Crowson
et al. (2024); Chen et al. (2025b); Mukhopadhyay et al. (2026)). Recent approaches have renewed
interest in pixel-space generation using vanilla or specialized transformer architectures (Li & He
(2026); Chen et al. (2026b); Yu et al. (2026); Guo et al. (2026)), with recent studies further exploring
its scaling to text-to-image generation (Jiang et al. (2026); Wang et al. (2026c); Ma et al. (2026b); Yu
et al. (2026); Ma et al. (2026c); Chen et al. (2025b)). In the context of our parameterization study,
JiT (Li & He (2026)) advocates clean-image prediction for high-dimensional pixel modeling, an
approach also adopted by recent large-scale pixel-space models such as Tuna-2 (Liu et al. (2026)).
k-Diff Jin & Wang (2026) and AsymFlow Chen et al. (2026a) further explore adaptive combinations
of clean-data and velocity prediction. Instead of deciding a predefined fixed formulation, which can
vary across architectures and datasets, MFLOW lets the model learn the best fit between clean image
and velocity prediction.
Details and frequency modeling. Several recent works study the difficulty of modeling fine-scale
information in vanilla pixel-space diffusion transformers with large spatial patch sizes, such as
16 × 16–32 × 32. Existing approaches use perceptual supervision (Ma et al. (2026c)), frequency-
aware objectives or flows (Degeorge et al. (2026); Pavasovic et al. (2026); Fan et al. (2026)),
frequency-specialized architectures (Ma et al. (2026b;a)), or improved information pathways (Guo
et al. (2026)). Concurrent work also shows that a frozen JiT model can recover additional high-
frequency detail through self-guidance (Fu et al. (2026)). Although some of these approaches also
improve training efficiency (Degeorge et al. (2026); Ma et al. (2026b)), they generally introduce
specialized architectural components or modify the training objective, and their generated frequency
statistics can still exhibit a high-frequency gap relative to real images (Degeorge et al. (2026); Ma
et al. (2026b); Pavasovic et al. (2026)). In contrast, our lightweight ConvRefiner is trained post
hoc while keeping the pretrained diffusion backbone frozen. It recovers the missing high-frequency
statistics without modifying the backbone or its original training procedure, while preserving FID.

Adaptive parameterization. Common non-adaptive parameterization/regression-targets include:
noise ϵ-prediction, velocity u-prediction , and clean signal x0-prediction. These are algebraically
equivalent but differ in conditioning. EDM (Karras et al. (2022)) introduces an input/output precon-
ditioning, where the network effectively predicts a noise-level dependent combination of clean data
and noise. However, JiT (Li & He (2026)) demonstrates that such a preconditioning substantially
underperforms x0-prediction for high-dimensional pixel patches using a vanilla transformer back-
bone, motivating the use of standard clean image prediction. More recently, k-Diff (Jin & Wang
(2026)) introduces a learnable scalar interpolation between prediction targets, showing that the pre-
ferred parameterization varies across pixel and latent representations. However, its globally shared
coefficient cannot account for directional differences within a representation.

Limitations of previous adaptive parameterizations. Chen et al. (2026a) note that the default
x0-prediction and k-Diff parameterization are less expressive. On the other hand, AsymFlow makes
hard-cutoff decision at a fixed rank r, and this chosen subspace is dataset/modality dependent, re-
quiring an expensive hyperparameter search over multiple parameterization setting. Furthermore,
restricting the noise term to the top-r (highest-variance) directions is arbitrary – e.g., we found that
PCA[9 : 16] can beat PCA[1 : 8] (FID 3.54 vs 3.59 on the B-level model trained on ImageNet).
Finally, the binary nature of the cutoff, while useful for interpretation, is not necessary: it extends
naturally to a continuous, per-direction weighting that softly mixes the clean-signal (x0) and veloc-
ity (v) prediction. Building on these insights, we introduce MFLOW, which learns a continuous,
direction-dependent parameterization, allowing the prediction target to adapt across directions with-
out requiring a hard subspace partition or costly search for parameterization.

SIGNAL MODELING
4.1
MOTIVATION
JiT lacks high-frequency but some other pixel diffusion models do not. JiT’s minimalist archi-
tecture lacks an explicit pathway dedicated to fine-grained signal details, like skip connections (Bao
et al. (2023); Hoogeboom et al. (2023; 2025)) or parallel refinement networks (Chen et al. (2026b);
Yu et al. (2026)), motivating an investigation into whether this simplicity introduces limitations in its
generated frequency distribution. We study the frequency spectrum of the images generated by mod-
els we trained which share the JiT architecture. We use radially averaged power spectrum (RAPS)
for this analysis. For context, images on a pixel grid can have at most 0.5 cycles/pixel horizontal
or vertical frequencies (fx,fy). However, in the case of radial averaging, the radial frequencies fr
can reach till the diagonal frequency of 0.707 (=

observe that all these models lack radial frequencies in the range of 0.5 −0.7. Recent concurrent
works (Pavasovic et al. (2026); Degeorge et al. (2026); Fu et al. (2026)) have also observed this. As
seen in Fig. 6, we tried investigating other LDMs and pixel diffusion backbones. We find that nei-
ther VAE-reconstruction/generations of DiT (Peebles & Xie (2023)), REPA-E (Leng et al. (2025)),
nor the generations of PixelDiT (Yu et al. (2026)) (which as a pixel level pathway) suffer from this
issue, indicating that it might be a JiT backbone specific problem, more explicitely the use of high
patchidication (and unpatchification) of 16 −32× without any intra-patch local operation.

EXPERIMENTS
We extensively evaluate our proposed method on class-conditional ImageNet image generation to
validate its effectiveness, as summarized in Table 1. Then we delve into individual components –
MFLOW and ConvRefiner. We further show that MFLOW can be extended to video generation and
scaled to text-to-image generation. Finally, we ablate the design decision of each component.
Datasets and Evaluation.
For class-conditioned image generation experiments we train on
ImageNet-1K (Russakovsky et al. (2015)). By default we show results on the standard 256 × 256
resolution images with the training and evaluation similar to Li & He (2026). For evaluation we use
FID@50K (Heusel et al. (2017)). We conduct class-conditioned video generation experiments on
UCF-101 (Soomro et al. (2012)) dataset. We use 16 frames of 128× 128 resolution videos for train-
ing and evaluation following Wang et al. (2025). Similar to images, FVD@10K (Unterthiner et al.
(2018)) serves as our video generation evaluation metric. For text-to-image generation, we train on
internal dataset of around 900M images (pretraining) and 33.5M (SFT), and evaluate on standard
benchmarks of GenEval (Ghosh et al. (2023)), DPG-Bench (Hu et al. (2024)), and PRISM-Bench
(Fang et al. (2026)).

MFLOW Ablations. We ablated multiple axes of choices for MFLOW on ImageNet B-level model.
We clamp the initializations to [0.05, 0.95], as otherwise the sigmoid function will blow up M.
In terms of loss parameterization we see that using uG loss over u loss significantly deteriorates
performance, bringing FID from 3.66 to 4.88. Training on uG creates a trivial incentive to reduce
mi toward x0-prediction without improving the recovered transport field (Appendix A.3). Subse-
quently, by varying the M initialization to the normalized-variance (‘norm var’) as described in 3.2,
we see slight improvement over ’asym8’ initialization, FID reduces from 3.84 to 3.80. Given that
‘norm var’ has a smoother dependence on the PCA direction’s variance, we choose it as default.
We also experiment with learning-rate of M parameters, and see that having a higher learning rate
multiplier seems to help a bit bringing FID down from 3.80 to 3.74. We use this final setting (init
‘norm var’, u-loss, learnable M w/ 10× lr-multiplier) as our default unless specified otherwise.

ConvRefiner Ablations. Our preliminary control experiment showed that JiT trained normally for
5% more epochs does not improve the frequency spectrum. In Fig. 11 (left) we investigate the effect

of number of blocks (b ∈2, 4, 8) and the weight of the adversarial loss (w ∈0.005, 0.01, 0.02).
Firstly, we see that only using the refiner without the adversarial loss helps a bit. However, the
frequency spectrum gets closer to real with more blocks and more GAN weight. Favoring a smaller

readily available convolutional refiner choice DiP patch-detailer(Chen et al. (2026b)) does improve
the high-frequency and even overshoots with the GAN loss. However, it degrades the base JiT’s FID
3.42 to 3.90 and 4.46 respectively, bolstering our choice of ConvRefiner.

DISCUSSION
Figure 12: Learned M values. Variations in the MFLOW gain curves across multiple axes: objec-
tives – w/ vs w/o LPIPS, domains – images vs videos, representations – pixel vs latent x0-prediction
models, model capacities – 1B vs 13B-MoE.
M-curve Analysis. Given the learned nature of the gain parameters mi, we analyze how they
vary across objectives, domains, representations, and model capacities. In Fig. 12, we visualize the
learned mi values ordered by decreasing PCA variance, which we call an M-curve. Here, mi = 0
corresponds to x0-prediction and mi = 1 to u-prediction; the ‘norm var’ initialization is shown in
gray.
1. On ImageNet (a), adding LPIPS drives all but the leading few directions toward x0-
prediction while also improving FID (Table 2), suggesting less need for u-like prediction
beyond the dominant PCA directions.
2. Moving to videos (b), the number of directions with non-negligible gains shrinks further,
showing that the preferred parameterization varies across domains. We discuss in detail
about the behaviour of LPIPS on M-curve in A.4.
3. With RAEv2 (Singh et al. (2026)) (c), whose latent model uses x0-prediction, the gains
collapse almost entirely toward zero, recovering an approximately pure x0 parameteriza-
tion. Yet forcing u-prediction on a small leading subspace changes FID only marginally
(Table 10), indicating that a low-rank u subspace can still be tolerated.
4. Finally, in text-to-image generation (d), increasing model capacity shifts more directions
toward u-prediction. Together with the RAEv2 result, this suggests that model capacity
influences how much u-prediction a model can accommodate, whereas the statistics of the
data representation determine where that u-prediction is most useful.
In particular, for a fixed budget of N u-predicted directions, the allocation should remain data-
dependent, with the leading high-variance PCA directions providing a natural choice. MFLOW
can exploit increased capacity or adapt to domain change, without requiring a manually chosen rank.
7
CONCLUSION
We revisit two design questions in vanilla pixel diffusion transformers: how their prediction pa-
rameterization should adapt to the data, and whether their minimalist architecture captures high-
frequency. We introduce MFLOW, a direction-adaptive parameterization that learns a continuous
11


interpolation between clean-data and velocity prediction across principal data directions, avoiding
the need for manual subspace selection, saving parameterization search cycles. Our analysis of
the learned M-curves further shows that the preferred parameterization changes systematically with
both data statistics and model capacity. Beyond parameterization, we identify a persistent high-
frequency deficit in JiT-style models and address it with ConvRefiner, a lightweight post-hoc convo-
lutional module that restores high-frequency statistics while preserving generative quality. Together,
these results suggest that both adaptive prediction targets and explicit treatment of fine-scale signal
structure are important ingredients for scaling simple pixel-space diffusion transformers.

A.4
SURPRISING BEHAVIOUR LPIPS ON THE LEARNED M-CURVE
Figure 12(a) shows that LPIPS substantially changes the prediction parameterization learned by
MFLOW. Without LPIPS, the gains decrease approximately monotonically with PCA variance:
high-variance directions receive more u-like prediction, while lower-variance directions become
increasingly x0-like. This behavior is consistent with the normalized-variance initialization. With
LPIPS, however, the leading gains become non-monotonic. Using zero-based indexing, PC0 and
PC1 retain high gains, PC2–PC3 exhibit a pronounced dip, and PC4 rises again.

One possible explanation follows from the conditional-mean behavior of squared-error regression.
In a finite-capacity model, a more x0-like parameterization can shrink uncertain components toward
their conditional means. For a spatially varying component, such shrinkage may primarily smooth
a local gradient or texture. For a patch-global component, however, it can coherently alter the
brightness, contrast, or color of the entire patch. Because LPIPS is applied to the recovered clean
image, it may make these perceptually coherent shifts more costly. The learned parameterization
responds by retaining a larger velocity-like component for the patch-global modes, thereby reducing
their tendency toward mean reversion.
The controlled comparison establishes that adding LPIPS produces the non-monotonic M-curve and
that the unusually high gains align with patch-global PCA modes. The mean-reversion explanation
is a plausible interpretation of this alignment rather than a causal mechanism directly established by
the ablation.
A.5
A CAPACITY-CONSTRAINED VIEW OF x0- AND u-PREDICTION
The choice between clean-data and velocity prediction involves two competing considerations.
Clean-data prediction avoids asking the model to reproduce full-dimensional Gaussian noise, which
can be advantageous when a high-dimensional representation has much lower intrinsic dimension.
22


Velocity prediction, on the other hand, provides better numerical conditioning near the clean end-
point. Our RAEv2 experiment illustrates this trade-off particularly clearly.
RAEv2 predominantly favors clean-data prediction. In our clean-latent RAEv2 setup, the pure
x0-prediction baseline obtains an FID of 1.05, while AsymFlow with r = 8 and MFlow obtain

Fig. 12(c) move almost entirely toward zero, recovering an approximately pure x0 parameterization.
These results indicate that, for this representation, model, and training budget, the network prefers
to avoid predicting noise in most latent directions.
At the same time, the AsymFlow result is only 0.03 FID behind the pure-x0 baseline, despite using
full velocity prediction in its leading eight PCA directions. This result does not show that velocity
prediction is superior in this setting; pure x0-prediction remains best in Table 10. It instead shows
that a small velocity-prediction subspace can be accommodated at little measured cost, even in a
representation that strongly favors x0-prediction overall.

as shown by the non-monotonic LPIPS M-curve in Fig. 12(a). We also observe that a non-leading
PCA block can outperform the first eight directions in one ImageNet pilot. MFlow therefore initial-
izes gains using normalized variance but lets the model modify both the effective velocity budget
and its direction-wise allocation through end-to-end training.
A.6
RADIALLY-AVERAGED POWER SPECTRUM
To quantify how faithfully a model reproduces the spatial-frequency content of natural images, we
compare the azimuthally-averaged 2D power spectrum of generated and real images. Each image is
converted to luma (Y = 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B), mean-subtracted to remove the DC compo-
nent, and multiplied by a separable 2D Hann window to suppress boundary leakage. We compute
the squared magnitude of its 2D DFT, P(f) = |Fg(f)|2, center it (fftshift), and azimuthally average
it, i.e., average over all orientations at each fixed radius r = ∥f∥, to obtain a 1D profile P(f) as
a function of radial frequency f (cycles/pixel). Averaging P(f) over N = 4000 images gives the
RAPS of a distribution.
A.7
REFINERS
For our ConvRefiner we use a t-conditioned PatchGAN discriminator (Isola et al. (2017)) (NLay-
erDiscriminator, 3 layers, ndf=64) with a hinge adversarial loss, applied to the refined ˆx0. We have
two types of batches – all-t batch and near-clean batch (t ∼U(0.9, 0.98)). For the all-t batch the
flow-matching and LPIPS(t ≥0.3) losses are normally applied. For the near-clean batch, additional
GAN loss is applied with a 0.02 weight, if not otherwise specified, and the flow-matching and LPIPS
loss are downweighted by a factor of 0.25. For Attention Refiner we used a 2 blocks of swin-style
(Liu et al. (2021)) windowed attention residual refiner in the clean image space, as a drop in replace-
ment for the ConvRefiner. Note that DiP patch-detailer also takes as input xt apart from the model
output.

ConvRefiner reveals a complementary evaluation limitation.
It substantially closes the high-
frequency spectral gap and brings the generated spectrum closer to that of real images, yet this
improvement produces little or no change in FID. This suggests that FID is relatively insensitive
to the local frequency statistics modified by the refiner. Consequently, spectral fidelity and FID
should be viewed as complementary rather than interchangeable measures; texture-sensitive metrics
and human evaluation are needed to determine how strongly the recovered high-frequency content
translates to perceived image quality.
