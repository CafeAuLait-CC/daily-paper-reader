ABSTRACT 

Text-conditioned 3D human–object interaction (HOI) generation remains challeng-ing because individually plausible human and object motions do not necessarily form a coherent interaction. We argue that a key limitation lies in conventional skeleton-based representations, which compactly describe body kinematics while leaving surface-level interaction geometry largely implicit, often requiring addi-tional contact-specific regularizers. We propose ULIFlow, a framework that explic-itly models continuous human–object relations within a unified latent space. To this end, we derive continuous marker–object relations from sparse body-surface mark-ers and treat them as an explicit interaction modality alongside human motion and object dynamics. A Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) autoencoder is then designed to jointly model these heterogeneous streams while preserving their modality-specific structures. Importantly, we let the relation stream directly participate in human and object reconstruction, encouraging the shared latent representation to preserve both individual dynamics and the interaction geometry that coordinates them. To further achieve variable-length generation, we introduce a blockwise Flow Forcing scheme with global sequence-progress conditioning, enabling progressive continuation from fixed-length training windows while maintaining consistent long-horizon dynamics. Extensive experiments demonstrate that ULIFlow consistently outperforms prior work in overall generation quality and human–object coordina-1arXiv:2610.24286v1 [cs.CV] 16 Oct 2026 tion, while preserving coherent interactions over requested durations beyond the training horizon. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Text-conditioned 3D human–object interaction (HOI) generation aims to synthesize human motion and object dynamics from a language description and has attracted increasing attention (Li et al., 2024; Cha et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026b). In particular, generating one interaction counterpart, as demonstrated by OMOMO (Li et al., 2023) and DAViD (Kim et al., 2025), has shown promising results. However, joint generation of plausible human and object motions remains challenging and relatively underexplored. Since individual plausibility does not ensure interaction coherence, simply combining the generated object and human motion trajectories may exhibit spatial misalignment or frequent interpenetration, which we define as a lack of coordination .One straightforward way to improve generation coordination is to impose hand-crafted rules or kine-matic constraints (Diller & Dai, 2024; Li et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2025). Although such regularization promotes physical consistency, such formulations often depend on task-specific assumptions and re-quire substantial manual effort. To reduce this reliance on heuristics, recent methods instead learn human motion and object dynamics jointly, through interaction tokenization (Geng et al., 2025) or modality-aware fusion (Wang et al., 2026a). Despite these advances, the synthesized sequences can still exhibit physical artifacts such as interpenetration, suggesting that jointly modeling the two tra-jectories with prior techniques is insufficient to ensure coherent interaction. In particular, while this strategy has proved effective for human–human motion generation (Shafir et al., 2024), ex-tending its success to HOI remains challenging: the distinct kinematic structures of humans and objects complicate their geometric coupling. Consequently, existing formulations may still require interaction-specific priors to regularize the generated interactions (Zeng et al., 2025). We argue that the key reason joint modeling for HOI fails lies in the long-standing practice of adopting only skeleton-based representations for encoding human dynamics. As skeletal representations provide a compact description that primarily captures internal body structure, the required contact cues, which by contrast occur on the human surface, remain implicitly represented. Consequently, contact information between humans and objects is weakly encoded in the resulting latent space, which therefore necessitates additional contact modeling (e.g., regularization (Xu et al., 2023)) to reduce interaction artifacts. In this paper, we ask: can we jointly model human motion, object dynamics, and their continuous geometric relations in a shared latent space, such that complex contact patterns can be learned directly from data without contact-specific regularization? Our core insight is that a powerful latent space for HOI requires explicit modeling of interaction geometry to handle diverse and evolving contact patterns. However, as the widely adopted skeletal representation is naturally limited in expressiveness, it cannot sufficiently characterize the rich geometric relations required for modeling diverse contact patterns. To this end, we propose Unified Latent Human–Object Interaction Flow (ULIFlow), which models continuous human–object dynamics in a unified latent space under the auto-encoding paradigm. Specifically, we introduce a complementary representation, body-surface markers (Zhang et al., 2021), to compensate for the limited geometric expressiveness of skeletal representations. Based on these markers, we explicitly derive continuous marker–object relations, including relative positions and velocities, and treat them as a distinct interaction modality alongside human and object dynamics. To accommodate the heterogeneous structures of human, object, and relation modalities, we design our model as a Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) autoencoder (Liang et al., 2024) that combines modality-specific transformations with shared attention, preserving the characteristics of each stream while learning their cross-modal dependencies. Notably, we couple human and object decoding with the relation stream to dynamically guide the reconstruction of both trajectories using explicit spatiotemporal cues. As the resulting latent space is jointly informed by individual dynamics and explicit interaction geometry, it enables coordinated human–object motion to be modeled without additional contact-specific regularization. Another challenge is to generate interactions of a requested duration from fixed-length training windows. To enable variable-length synthesis, we adapt the Diffusion Forcing mechanism (Chen 2et al., 2024) to our subsequent Flow Matching stage. Concretely, we divide each training window into temporal blocks with different noise levels, enabling the model to progressively continue an interaction from partially resolved motion. As shown in Fig. 1, our design allows the generated HOI sequences to have arbitrary length while maintaining coherent long-term dynamics. Extensive experiments demonstrate that ULIFlow consistently outperforms prior methods in overall generation quality and human–object coordination. These advantages remain evident in long-horizon generation, where ULIFlow preserves coherent interactions as the requested duration extends beyond the fixed training window. Our contributions are as follows: • We propose ULIFlow, a unified latent framework for text-conditioned HOI generation at any requested duration. By explicitly modeling continuous human–object geometry, ULIFlow learns coordinated dynamics directly from data without contact-specific regularization or heuristic interaction designs. • We introduce a Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) autoencoder that jointly encodes human, object, and relation modalities via modality-specific transformations and relation-aware decoding. • We design a blockwise Flow Forcing scheme to align finite-window training with progressive rollout, enabling stable HOI generation beyond the training horizon. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Generative modeling for HOI. Recent HOI generation methods have increasingly adopted diffu-sion models for the conditional synthesis of human and object motion (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024). Despite their strong generative ca-pability, diffusion-based HOI modeling remains susceptible to interaction artifacts. (Xu et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Cha et al., 2024). To improve interaction fidelity, existing approaches prevalently intro-duce contact- and affordance-aware guidance (Kulkarni et al., 2024; Diller & Dai, 2024) or explicitly model kinematic and relational dependencies (Zeng et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2025). While these ad-vances substantially improve human–object consistency, they generally rely on dedicated interaction modeling or guidance mechanisms in addition to the underlying motion generator, which can be time-consuming to explicitly create the rules. Along this direction, HOI-Diff (Peng et al., 2025) learns an auxiliary affordance model to predict contact regions as guidance, while LIGHT (Wang et al., 2026b) derives cross-modal guidance from asynchronous denoising. These approaches reduce manual specification of guidance, but do not necessarily eliminate contact-specific supervision. In particular, (Wang et al., 2026b) still employs a contact loss based on binary labels derived from geo-metric thresholds. Our method instead learns human–object coordination from continuous geometric supervision without contact-specific regularization. 

Human motion representations. Prior methods for text-conditioned motion generation (Guo et al., 2022; Tevet et al., 2023) and HOI modeling (Xu et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2025) commonly adopt joint-based representations to compactly describe body kinematics. However, joints primarily characterize body kinematics and provide limited information about the body surface where physical interaction occurs. A common way to recover richer body geometry is to use parametric human models, such as SMPL (Loper et al., 2015), which reconstruct a posed body surface from compact pose and shape parameters (Petrovich et al., 2021; 2022). For example, GOAL (Taheri et al., 2022) samples body-surface vertices to characterize human–object proximity during whole-body grasping. To retain surface information more compactly, marker-based representations describe human motion using sparse points distributed over the body surface. MOJO (Zhang et al., 2021) demonstrates that such markers capture motion information unavailable from skeletal joints, while subsequent work extends them to scene-aware motion generation (Zhang & Tang, 2022; Zhao et al., 2023) and HOI modeling (Wu et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2025). These studies establish sparse body-surface markers as a compact representation for retaining interaction-relevant surface information. 

Unified multimodal modeling. Recent work has demonstrated the effectiveness of unified multi-modal modeling across diverse tasks. For example, Unified-IO 2 (Lu et al., 2023) unifies multimodal perception and action prediction, while Chameleon (Team, 2025) jointly supports text–image under-standing and generation. Prior efforts explore joint interaction tokenization (Geng et al., 2025) and modality-aware fusion (Wang et al., 2026a), yet joint modeling alone does not ensure consistent contact, leaving misalignment and interpenetra-tion as unresolved concerns. Our method builds on the insight that explicitly modeling continuous human–object relations in the latent space enables joint learning of individual dynamics and geomet-ric coordination, which addresses interaction inconsistencies by using relational cues to guide the reconstruction of both trajectories. 

Variable-length generation. Prior work has explored variable-length synthesis in human motion (Barquero et al., 2024; Han et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2025; Zhuo et al., 2025) and video genera-tion (Song et al., 2025; Cai et al., 2026). Such flexibility is also important for HOI, since even the same interaction can be performed over different durations. However, extending an interaction re-quires human and object motion to remain coordinated throughout the generated sequence. To meet this requirement, we adapt Diffusion Forcing (Chen et al., 2024) to blockwise flow matching within our unified HOI latent space. By learning continuation from partially resolved histories and condi-tioning local windows on global sequence progress, our method generates coordinated interactions of the requested duration from fixed-length training windows.

Tokenization. To jointly encode human motion, object dynamics, and their geometric relations, we organize H, O, and R into structured token streams. Given that human motion and relation features are distributed over multiple body-surface markers, learned body-region queries aggregate marker-level observations into compact region tokens (Jaegle et al., 2021), while dedicated tokens retain root and foot information as well as root–object and object–ground relations. For the object stream, each rigid pose is represented by a temporal token, whereas the static geometry G is summarized using a fixed set of geometry queries (Prokudin et al., 2019). As a final step, all tokens are projected into a common feature space while retaining their modality identities and temporal positions, yielding the human, object, and relation streams XH , XO , and XR, respectively. 

Unified latent space modeling. Since human motion, object dynamics, and human–object relations exhibit different structures and statistical distributions, directly mixing their tokens can obscure modality-specific information. Following recent unified multimodal architectures (Esser et al., 2024; Niu et al., 2026; Agarwal et al., 2026), we therefore build the autoencoder with Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) blocks (Liang et al., 2024), which retain modality-specific transformations while exchanging information through shared attention. Within the learned latent space, we model interaction generation using Flow Matching (Lipman et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022) and parameterize the conditional velocity field with a DiT (Peebles & Xie, 2023). During long-horizon rollout, fixed-length training windows may correspond to different stages of the complete interaction, creating inconsistent temporal context between training and inference. Accordingly, we introduce a normalized sequence-progress condition p ∈ [0 , 1] to specify the global position of the current window within the requested duration. By using the same progress definition during training and inference, the model retains consistent sequence-level temporal context throughout generation. 

Training schedule. To learn variable-length continuation from fixed-length training windows, we introduce a blockwise forcing schedule for Flow Matching. Unlike Diffusion Forcing (Chen et al., 2024), which assigns noise levels at the token level, our schedule assigns each temporal block an independently sampled shared flow time τb ∼ U (0 , 1) , where 0 and 1 denote the noise and data endpoints, respectively. This exposes the model to partially resolved causal histories. Under block-causal attention, each block uses only its available causal context, while the sequence-progress condition p maintains a global temporal reference. Together, these designs align fixed-window training with long-horizon rollout.

Inference. During inference, we initialize an ordered sequence of latent blocks from Gaussian noise according to the requested duration T . We integrate the predicted velocities using a staggered schedule, allowing earlier blocks to reach the clean endpoint before later blocks. To extend generation while keeping the model context fixed, we progressively shift the window forward, retaining recent completed blocks as history and introducing new noisy blocks. As the window advances, we update the progress condition p according to the window’s global position within the requested sequence. We then decode the completed latents in overlapping windows, assemble the outputs in temporal order, and crop the sequence to exactly match T frames. Further details of conditional generation are provided in Sec. A.2 of the Appendix.

Metrics. Following standard practice in text-to-motion generation (Guo et al., 2022), we evaluate generated HOI sequences in terms of realism, text–interaction alignment, and diversity. We report Fr´ echet Inception Distance ( FID ) to measure feature-distribution similarity, R-Precision and Multi-modal Distance (MM-Dist) to assess text–interaction alignment, and Diversity and Multimodality (MModality ) to characterize variation across generated interactions. To further evaluate human–object coordination, we measure body–object penetration using the penetration ratio together with the mean, 95th-percentile, and maximum penetration depths. For long-horizon generation, we evaluate the standard generation metrics on fixed-length subsequences extracted from extended rollouts and assess temporal stability around block boundaries using Peak Jerk and Area Under the Jerk (AUJ) (Cai et al., 2026). We additionally report human and object jerk statistics over the generated sequences to characterize motion stability. For metrics marked with →,values closer to the ground-truth statistics indicate better agreement with real motion dynamics. Full metric definitions and evaluation protocols are provided in Sec. 5 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we introduce ULIFlow, a framework for text-conditioned HOI generation that jointly models human motion, object dynamics, and continuous interaction geometry in a unified latent space. Derived from body-surface markers, these continuous relations allow ULIFlow to represent interaction geometry as an explicit modality alongside human and object dynamics. Through modality-specific processing and relation-aware decoding, the MoT autoencoder supports coordinated reconstruction without contact-specific regularization, while blockwise Flow Forcing and sequence-progress conditioning enable variable-length rollout beyond the training horizon. A.1 STRUCTURED AUTOENCODER 

Token construction. Considering that the marker-level human and relation fields are substantially denser than the rigid-object state, we first reduce them to comparable token sets without removing their spatial organization. At each frame, eight learned body-region queries aggregate the 77 surface markers, while separate root and foot tokens retain global translation and support information. This construction yields 11 human tokens per frame. Given the rigid structure of the object, we use one dynamic pose token per frame and four static geometry tokens obtained from its Basis Point Set (BPS) descriptor (Prokudin et al., 2019). To preserve the correspondence between motion and relation features, eight relation queries aggregate marker-level relations and two additional tokens retain the object–ground and root–object relations, yielding 10 relation tokens per frame. Finally, learned identity encodings distinguish marker regions, relation types, entities, and modalities, while marker-to-part assignments and marker–object proximity guide the corresponding query aggregation. 

Continuous relation fields. To instantiate the continuous relation representation defined in Sec. 3, the first 77 relation entries preserve a one-to-one correspondence with the human markers. An additional local entry applies the same construction to the object–ground pair, while a separate global entry records the root–object offset, its norm, and their first- and second-order temporal differences. Since these local, ground, and global quantities follow different numerical scales, we normalize the three groups independently before tokenization. This keeps the relation pathway aligned with the body surface while preventing any one field from dominating the query aggregation. 

Modality-aware encoding. To permit cross-modal exchange without forcing identical feature transformations, we share attention across routes and retain route-specific normalization and feed-forward layers. Generative target and conditioning streams. To separate the state being generated from information available at inference, we use the fixed autoencoder only to construct the Flow Matching target. Its reconstruction latent Zrec is mapped to Zwhite by the whitening transform above, and the complete whitened field, including its dynamic tokens and four static latent-geometry tokens, is treated as the generated state. Importantly, these latent-geometry tokens are part of the target and remain distinct from the object-geometry condition computed directly from the input G. Given the different structures of language and shape, text prompts are encoded by a frozen FLAN-T5-XL encoder (Chung et al., 2022), while sixteen learned queries aggregate the object BPS descriptor into a separate geometry-condition stream. The noisy latent, text, and geometry streams are then projected to a common model width before entering the conditional transformer. 

Conditional modulation. Considering that latent state, language, and geometry play different roles, the conditional DiT retains route-specific adaptive normalization and feed-forward layers across its 12 blocks while using shared attention for cross-stream exchange. Shared query, key, value, and output projections provide the common exchange path, whereas zero-initialized route-specific residual projections allow each stream to specialize during optimization. To inject global semantic and geometric context, pooled text and geometry features modulate the latent route together with its token-wise flow time. Since flow time does not identify where a window belongs in the requested sequence, a separate progress condition is computed from the normalized window center and injected through the text and geometry routes. Shared attention then communicates this global reference to the latent route. The same definition is used for sampled training windows and sliding inference windows. Finally, temporal RoPE is restricted to latent tokens that carry physical frame indices, leaving text and static geometry conditions without temporal rotation. 

Blockwise training schedule. To expose the model to causal histories containing both clean and noisy latent regions, we group dynamic tokens by temporal anchor, with eight consecutive anchors in each block. Every block independently samples τb ∼ U (0 , 1) , while the static latent-geometry tokens receive a separate flow time and the text and geometry conditions remain unperturbed. Mij = 1 ⇐⇒ j ∈ C or b(j) ≤ b(i),

where b(i) denotes the temporal block containing latent token i and C contains all text and geometry-condition tokens. Given that tokens within one anchor range represent the same local interval, attention remains bidirectional inside each block. To support classifier-free guidance without changing the target latent field, both conditions are retained with probability 0.7, while text-only, geometry-only, and unconditional examples are each sampled with probability 0.1.

Autoencoder configuration. For reproducibility, the autoencoder uses a model width of 256 and four attention heads. Its shared latent field has 708 tokens of dimension 16. The MoT encoder has six layers, the decoder has eight MoT layers, and the temporal densifier has four layers. To optimize this model, we use 100,000 steps of AdamW (β1, β 2) = (0 .9, 0.95) , weight decay 10 −4, a peak learning rate of 10 −4, 2,000 warm-up steps, and cosine decay to 10 −5. The covariance regularizer is activated after 10,000 steps and linearly reaches its full weight at step 30,000. We estimate the ZCA transform with ϵ = 10 −6. The noise-robust decoder is then fine-tuned for an additional 30,000 steps, with 500 warm-up steps and cosine decay from 2 × 10 −5 to 5 × 10 −6. Both phases use a per-GPU batch size of 6 without gradient accumulation on three NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5080 GPUs, giving an effective batch size of 18. The ZCA transform and decoder fine-tuning are initialized from the final autoencoder checkpoint. We retain the decoder checkpoint with the lowest validation reconstruction drift under latent perturbations of σ = 0 .125 .

Conditional-flow configuration. The conditional flow model uses width 384, 12 layers, and six attention heads. To learn the velocity field, we optimize it for 110,000 steps with AdamW 

(β1, β 2) = (0 .9, 0.95) , weight decay 10 −2, and learning rate 10 −4. The learning rate increases linearly during the first 2,000 steps, remains constant through step 100,000, and follows a cosine decay to 10 −5 over the final 10,000 steps. We maintain an exponential moving average of the trainable model with decay 0.9999 . To reduce training memory and computation, all models use bfloat16 mixed precision. Unless stated otherwise, inference uses Euler integration with S = 32 , w = 4 , and 

h = 1 , corresponding to a 128-frame temporal context with one fully denoised history block retained as causal context. Training uses a per-GPU batch size of 16 with two-step gradient accumulation on three NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5080 GPUs, yielding an effective batch size of 96. The conditional flow model is trained with the selected noise-robust decoder checkpoint and the fixed ZCA transform. All reported generation results use the EMA weights from the final 110,000-step checkpoint. 

B EVALUATION DETAILS 

B.1 BASELINE ADAPTATION PROTOCOL 

To evaluate methods with different human representations in a common space, we convert their released outputs to the official InterAct 77-marker layout. For CHOIS, the generated SMPL-X parameters are passed through its body-model forward process. For HOI-Diff and LIGHT, their generated 22- and 52-joint trajectories are fitted to SMPL-H using the corresponding released fitting procedures. Since Text2HOI generates only articulated hands, its generated wrist states and hand poses drive a neutral SMPL-X carrier whose remaining body degrees of freedom are fixed. In every case, the 77 markers are selected using the official InterAct marker vertex indices. We decode each generated object trajectory with the corresponding baseline’s released transformation convention and use the InterAct object samples when constructing evaluator inputs. When a baseline and InterAct use different world frames, a single rigid transformation estimated from their initial human and object states maps the generated sequence to the InterAct coordinate system. No future ground-truth frames are used by this alignment, and the generated temporal evolution is otherwise unchanged. All converted records are stored at 30 fps and evaluated with the same frozen evaluator described below. B.2 INTER ACT FEATURE -S PACE PROTOCOL 

Following the official InterAct evaluation protocol (Xu et al., 2025), we use its frozen marker–BPS evaluator, training-set normalization statistics, and BPS basis for all feature-space metrics. At each frame, the evaluator input concatenates the 3D coordinates of 77 body-surface markers with 128 BPS displacement vectors computed from the translated object basis to its nearest surface points, yielding a 615-dimensional representation. The official motion and text encoders map a generated interaction and its caption to 256-dimensional features. We retain the standard definitions of FID, R-Precision, MM-Dist, Diversity, and MModality used by InterAct and text-to-motion evaluation (Guo et al., 2022). FID compares the Gaussian statistics of generated and reference motion features. R-Precision ranks motion features by their Euclidean 17 distance to a text feature within retrieval pools of 32 samples and reports Top-1, Top-2, and Top-3 accuracy. MM-Dist is the mean distance between matched text and motion features, while Diversity averages the distance between 300 randomly sampled pairs of generated motion features. For MModality, we generate 30 samples for each evaluated condition and average 10 randomly drawn within-condition pairs. All other feature metrics are reported as the mean and sample standard deviation over three independent generation runs with seeds 20260622, 1, and 2. MModality is evaluated from one repeated-sampling run, with uncertainty estimated by 1,000 condition-level bootstrap resamples. Outputs from adapted baselines are first converted to the common 77-marker representation and then evaluated by the same frozen InterAct evaluator. Each adapted method is scored on all conditions supported by its released model and data subset. B.3 PHYSICAL AND TEMPORAL METRICS 

Body–object penetration. For each valid frame t and body marker j, we query its signed distance to the posed object mesh. Let qt,j = max(sdf Ot (pHt,j ), 0) , where the signed distance is positive inside the object. The penetration ratio is the fraction of valid marker queries for which qt,j > 0. The mean, 95th percentile, and maximum penetration depths are computed over the penetrating queries and converted from meters to centimeters. We use the available decimated object meshes and uniformly sample approximately 48 frames per sequence for signed-distance queries. Sequences without a valid object mesh are excluded from this metric. 

Relation coordination. To measure contact without using the reference object trajectory for the generated motion, we compute marker–surface distances independently for each generated human– object pair and each ground-truth pair. Let dt = min j,k ∥pHt,j − sOt,k ∥2 and ct = 1[dt < ϵ ], where 

ϵ = 2 cm. Contact recall is the fraction of ground-truth contact frames for which the generated pair also satisfies ct = 1 , and contact distance is the generated dt averaged over those reference contact frames. Hand-contact metrics use the same definitions with the marker set restricted to the hands. To distinguish reduced penetration from simply keeping the object away from the body, we additionally measure the fraction of object travel occurring across consecutive frames for which neither endpoint is in contact. Finally, we summarize relation dynamics for each sequence by the temporal mean of |dt − dt−1| and report the 1-Wasserstein distance between the generated and ground-truth distributions. 

Motion stability. For a temporally aligned point trajectory xt,j , we compute the third-order finite difference 

∆3xt,j = xt,j − 3xt−1,j + 3 xt−2,j − xt−3,j .

The per-frame jerk is the mean Euclidean norm of this quantity over points, reported in cm/frame 3.Human jerk is evaluated on the 77 body-surface markers, while object jerk is evaluated on temporally corresponding object surface points. We report the temporal mean, the maximum as Peak Jerk, and the sum over valid frames as AUJ. Since realistic motions need not minimize jerk, these statistics are compared with their ground-truth counterparts rather than ranked monotonically. B.4 LONG -H ORIZON PROTOCOL 

To assess local quality within extended rollouts, we divide each valid sequence into complete, non-overlapping 128-frame subsequences. Each subsequence inherits the caption of its parent interaction and is evaluated with the same InterAct feature-space protocol. We additionally extract 64-frame windows centered at every 128-frame continuation boundary, using 32 frames on each side. Incomplete subsequences and boundary windows are discarded. FID and Diversity are computed over the boundary windows, while Peak Jerk and AUJ are computed from their human marker trajectories. The values in the main paper are averaged over the same three generation seeds used for the standard feature metrics.
