ABSTRACT
Flow-matching models generate samples through iterative velocity predictions, but
typically do not revisit these predictions using the intermediate representations that
produced them. We introduce CORRFLOW, a self-correcting flow-matching frame-
work that reuses this information through a lightweight refiner. Under rectified-flow
interpolation, the error of the one-step clean prediction equals the realized velocity
residual scaled by the noise level. Motivated by this identity, the refiner learns
to predict residual corrections from the base model’s clean prediction, condition-
ing, noise level and hidden states from the same forward pass. The correction
is added to the base velocity before each sampling update, so subsequent predic-
tions are made from the corrected state. This requires no additional base-model
evaluations and only one lightweight refiner evaluation per step. Our theory gives
conditions under which feature reuse outperforms raw-input affine predictors, re-
finer training outperforms continued base-model training at matched compute,
and deployed correction lowers velocity error under its own sampler law. Ex-
periments on class-conditional image generation, text-to-video generation and
image-to-video generation show consistent improvements over the corresponding
base models, including gains in visual quality and physical consistency. Project

INTRODUCTION
Flow-matching models (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023) generate samples by learning a velocity
field that transports noise toward the data distribution. Their simple regression objective has shown
strong results in image (Esser et al., 2024), video (Wan et al., 2025), 3D (Xiang et al., 2025) and
robotic action (Black et al., 2024). Yet generated content still exhibits structural errors, semantic
mismatches and physical inconsistencies (Zheng et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025; Motamed et al.,
2026; Zhang et al., 2025a). Recent approaches improve generation quality through large-scale
training (Esser et al., 2024), representation alignment (Yu et al., 2025), self-supervised feature
learning (Chefer et al., 2026) and reward-based optimization (Yuan et al., 2026). They focus on
improving the model’s ability by learning only from the final answer, which is limited. This limitation
can be attributed, in part, to the fact that the model must predict a single velocity to determine the
next sample state, without any opportunity to review and revise its own predictions.
We therefore exploit the property that learning should be “refine-in-loop”, in the sense that if a student
makes a mistake, e.g., in an examination, they should revisit the answer they wrote, the question
they were asked, and the reasoning they followed. By examining these pieces of context together,
the student can trace a mistake and determine how to revise it, rather than struggling with a final
answer (Madaan et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2025). Inspired by the success of self-refinement in large
language models (LLMs) (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023), we apply this principle to flow
matching. A flow-based generator already computes analogous information at each sampling step: an
estimate of the clean sample→answer, the generation conditions→question and intermediate hidden
states→internal state. The challenge is to define a correction target that can be supervised during
training and predicted from these quantities without access to a clean reference during generation.
In this paper, we introduce CORRFLOW, a self-correcting flow-matching framework that attaches a
lightweight refiner to the base generator. Our core insight is that the model’s one-step clean prediction
provides a concrete object for the review, with a direct mathematical connection to the correction we
seek (Chen et al., 2022). Under rectified-flow interpolation, the error between this prediction and the

In practice, the refiner is a transformer that predicts the velocity residual from three sources of
information: i) the current answer, given by the one-step clean prediction, ii) the circumstances,
given by the generation condition and noise level, and iii) the internal state, given by hidden states
from the base model (Epstein et al., 2023). During joint training, clean samples and their sampled
noise provide residual targets for the refiner, while the base model and refiner optimize separate
objectives. During generation, the predicted residual adjusts the velocity used by the sampler before
each state update. All refiner inputs reuse the same base-model forward pass, and each correction
requires one additional refiner evaluation.
We empirically apply CORRFLOW to a wide range of applications, including image generation,
text-to-video generation and image-to-video generation. Figure 1 illustrates how this review shifts
the model’s clean prediction through a correction of its velocity on image generation. We find that
training a refiner improves the base model’s performance on aesthetic quality and physics consistency
of generated content. The strong performance across multiple tasks and datasets shows that velocity
residual correction is generic. Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce CORRFLOW, a self-correcting flow-matching framework that learns velocity
residuals from the base model’s current prediction, and applies the resulting corrections
within the sampling loop.
• We theoretically establish conditions under which reusing base-model representations out-
performs raw-input affine predictors, refiner training achieves greater residual-risk reduction
than continued base-model training at matched compute, and deployed correction lowers
velocity error under its own sampler-induced state law.
• We evaluate CORRFLOW on image and video generation benchmarks, demonstrating im-
provements in visual quality and physics consistency.

RELATED WORKS
Improving Flow-Matching Generative Models. Many approaches have been proposed to improve
flow-matching generative models. For representation learning, REPA (Yu et al., 2025) aligns hidden
states with pretrained visual features, while iREPA (Singh et al., 2025) emphasizes their spatial
structure. VA-VAE (Yao et al., 2025) aligns VAE latents with visual features, REPA-E (Leng et al.,
2025) jointly tunes the VAE and diffusion transformer through alignment and RAE (Zheng et al.,
2026a) generates directly in pretrained representation spaces. Other methods reuse features available
2

within the generative model: SRA and LayerSync (Jiang et al., 2026; Haghighi et al., 2026) use
deeper features to guide shallower representations, and SRA2 (Wang et al., 2026a) draws on existing
VAE features. Reward-based methods Flow-GRPO and DanceGRPO (Liu et al., 2025; Xue et al.,
2025c) use stochastic sampling for online reinforcement learning, while AWM (Xue et al., 2025b)
and DiffusionNFT (Zheng et al., 2026c) incorporate rewards into flow-matching objectives through
advantage weighting and paired generations, respectively. CORRFLOW takes a complementary
approach: it trains a lightweight added-on refiner to predict velocity residuals from the base model’s
current prediction and hidden states. These residuals directly correct the velocity within the sampling
loop, without requiring external representation teachers or reward models for refiner training.
Physics-Aware Video Generation. Several recent works have explored physics-aware video gen-
eration with flow-matching models. WISA (Wang et al., 2025), PhysVid (Pathak et al., 2026)
and PhyParam (Li et al., 2026) inject hierarchical, temporally local, or instance-level physical
conditions. PhysGen (Liu et al., 2024) and PSIVG (Foo et al., 2026) use simulated dynamics as
guidance. Representation-based methods transfer or jointly learn motion, geometry, semantics and
latent dynamics, including VideoREPA (Zhang et al., 2025c), PhysAlign (Xiong et al., 2026), Video-
JAM (Chefer et al., 2025), PHANTOM (Shen et al., 2026), DreamWorld (Tan et al., 2026) and
MMPhysVideo (Lin et al., 2026). Other approaches use language or feedback, with PhyT2V (Xue
et al., 2025a) and DiffPhy (Zhang et al., 2025b) introducing inferred physical rules, PhysMaster (Ji
et al., 2025) and PhyGDPO (Cai et al., 2025) applying pairwise or groupwise preference learning and
WMReward (Yuan et al., 2026) steering denoising trajectories with a latent-world-model reward at
inference. In contrast, CORRFLOW learns to correct the base model’s own velocity residuals from its
predictions and hidden states, without explicit physical parameters or external physics evaluators.

3.2
REFINER INPUTS
Focusing on the current answer in isolation is insufficient, because the same prediction may be correct
under one condition and a mistake under another, or an artifact that must be repaired at high noise
may be a legitimate detail at low noise. Therefore, the refiner is given three views of the moment the
mistake was made:
The current answer. The one-step clean prediction ˆx0 of Equation 5, tokenized according to the
sample structure to form the refiner’s trunk tokens. This is the base model’s current estimate
of the clean sample.
The circumstances. The conditioning tokens c that encode the task requirements, and the noise
level σ, which identifies the current position along the denoising trajectory.
The internal state. The base model’s hidden states {hℓ}ℓ∈L, where L indexes transformer blocks
selected uniformly across the model’s depth. These readouts capture different stages of the
computation that produced vθ, and they record how the prediction was assembled rather
than only what it was.

3.3
REFINER ARCHITECTURE
CORRFLOW is designed to be a lightweight add-on transformer to a base flow-matching model,
which estimates the realized velocity residual r. It has its own parameters, totaling approximately
10% of the base model’s parameter count. It carries the aforementioned information as inputs.
Token embeddings. Given the clean prediction ˆx0 (eq. (5)), the refiner frefiner first tokenizes it
according to the sample structure into N tokens and projects them to the refiner width d, obtaining the
answer tokens tans ∈RN×d. Our image and video implementations use patches for this tokenization.
To keep the refiner and base model on a common clock, the refiner also consumes the base model’s
own noise-level embedding eσ at the current noise level σ.
We also construct the context tokens tctx ∈R(Nc+N)×d from the conditioning c and the hidden states
{hℓ}ℓ∈L, where Nc is the number of conditioning tokens. In particular, a linear projection maps c
to the conditioning tokens tc ∈RNc×d. At each token position, we concatenate the hidden states
from the selected layers along the feature dimension and project them to the refiner width d using a
multilayer perceptron with a GELU activation,

Alternating-Attention blocks. We build our lightweight add-on transformer with a stack of B
Alternating Attention Blocks (AABs), making the refiner focus on the current answer while consulting
the context in an alternate manner. Specifically, even-indexed blocks apply self-attention to the
answer tokens tans, using rotary position embeddings defined on the token coordinates of the sample
representation. Our image and video implementations use two-dimensional spatial coordinates
and three-dimensional spatiotemporal coordinates, respectively. Odd-indexed blocks apply cross-
attention to the context tokens tctx, which lets each token incorporate the task requirements and
the base model’s intermediate representations. Each attention layer is followed by a feed-forward
network. Following the base model’s adaptive-normalization design, each block uses shift, scale and
gate parameters derived from the noise-level embedding eσ. Finally, a modulated linear head projects
the updated answer tokens to residual tokens, which are reassembled into the sample’s original shape.

EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate CORRFLOW on a wide range of tasks, including class-conditional image generation,
text-to-video generation and image-to-video generation. The image experiments test whether learned
velocity corrections improve an established flow-matching model, while the video experiments
examine visual quality, temporal consistency and physical plausibility. Red, orange and yellow
highlight the best, second-best and third-best scores in each column, respectively.
5.1
IMAGE GENERATION
Settings. We study class-conditional generation on ImageNet at 256 × 256 resolution with 1,000
classes. Our base model is built upon RAEv2 with a frozen DINOv3-L-based representation au-
toencoder and a pretrained diffusion transformer. Starting from its 80-epoch checkpoint, we jointly
fine-tune the base transformer and train a randomly initialized refiner for another 80 epochs on the
ImageNet training split, denoted by 80 + 80 in Table 1. The refiner has 10 transformer blocks with
width 768 and 12 attention heads, and reads hidden states from base transformer blocks 3, 11, 19 and
27, using one-based indexing. We retain the base model’s training objective and train the refiner to
predict the realized velocity residual with squared error and a cosine-direction term weighted by 0.1.
Both networks use GMuon optimizer with a global batch size of 1,024. The evaluation settings are
identical to those in Singh et al. (2026).

6
CONCLUSION
We introduced CORRFLOW, a self-correcting flow-matching framework that learns velocity residuals
from the base model’s clean prediction, conditioning and hidden states. A lightweight refiner
corrects the velocity within the sampling loop. Our theory identifies conditions under which feature
reuse improves residual prediction, refiner training outperforms continued base-model training at
matched compute, and the deployed correction lowers own-law velocity error. We demonstrate that
CORRFLOW achieves state-of-the-art performance across multiple benchmarks including image
generation, text-to-video generation, and image-to-video generation. We hope CORRFLOW will be a
useful tool for the community to advance research in flow-matching-based generative modeling.

Meaning of the certificate.
The constant KQ converts the correction gain into a bound on expected
trajectory displacement. The constants LV and LA translate this displacement into bounds on the
change in squared baseline residual and residual–correction alignment, respectively. The first-order
margin M = 2A0 −LV KQ balances the alignment benefit along the baseline trajectory against
the bound on baseline-error change caused by trajectory displacement. The condition M > 0
ensures that the alignment benefit exceeds this displacement cost. The second-order coefficient
N = G2 + 2LAKQ accounts for correction magnitude and the bound on alignment change along
the shifted trajectory. Choosing κ < M/N makes the certified first-order benefit dominate these
second-order costs. The resulting bound accounts for both the correction and its induced trajectory
shift, establishing lower velocity error under the corrected sampler’s own state law. Appendix C.4
next analyzes how velocity corrections accumulate into endpoint displacement.

IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
We expand the settings in Sections 5.1–5.3, covering model configurations, optimization, inference
and evaluation.
D.1
IMAGE GENERATION
Model configuration.
We build on the released ImageNet-256 RAEv2 checkpoint (Singh et al.,
2026) trained for 80 epochs. The representation autoencoder uses the DINOv3-L encoder with
seven-layer feature aggregation and a pretrained ViT-XL decoder. We keep the encoder, decoder
and latent normalization statistics fixed throughout adaptation. Each image is represented by a
16 × 16 grid of 1,024-dimensional latent vectors. The base transformer processes these vectors with
latent patch size 1, using 28 blocks of width 1,440 with 20 attention heads, followed by a two-block
diffusion head of width 2,048 with 16 attention heads. Its internal guidance branch reads the features
after block 8. The refiner uses 10 blocks of width 768, 12 attention heads and an MLP expansion
ratio of 4. It takes the predicted clean latent, noise-level and class embeddings and hidden states from
base blocks 3, 11, 19 and 27, with block indices counted from one. We initialize the refiner from
scratch and zero-initialize its output projection, so its initial velocity correction is zero.
Training.
The base retains its flow-matching objective, including supervision of the internal
guidance branch. Training noise levels follow the base configuration: we draw a logit-normal variable
with underlying Gaussian mean 0 and standard deviation 1, then apply the same factor-8 shift used
for sampling below. The refiner minimizes the mean squared error to the realized velocity residual,
together with a cosine-direction term of weight 0.1. Its overall loss weight is 1. We detach the
residual target and all base-model inputs to the refiner, so the refiner loss updates only the refiner and
the base is updated by its own objective. Both networks use GMuon with momentum 0.95, Nesterov
updates, zero weight decay and gradient-norm clipping at 1. The base and refiner learning rates warm
up from zero over 0.2 epochs to 2 × 10−5 and 4 × 10−4, respectively, then decay linearly to 2 × 10−6

Preprint
and 4 × 10−5 at the end of the 80-epoch adaptation. We train with a global batch size of 1,024 on
eight NVIDIA H200 GPUs, using bfloat16 mixed precision, 64 images per GPU per microbatch and
two gradient-accumulation steps. The full 80-epoch adaptation comprises 100,080 optimizer steps
and takes approximately 42 hours on a single node, including periodic evaluation and checkpointing.
Class-conditioning dropout is 0.1, and exponential moving average (EMA) weights are maintained
separately for both networks with decay 0.9995.

D.2
TEXT-TO-VIDEO GENERATION
Model configuration.
We build on Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B (Wan et al., 2025), keeping its pretrained
video variational autoencoder (VAE) and UMT5-XXL text encoder fixed while updating the full base
transformer and the refiner. The base transformer has 30 blocks of width 1,536 with 12 attention
heads. The VAE maps an 81-frame video at 480 × 832 resolution to a latent tensor with 16 channels,
21 temporal positions and spatial size 60 × 104. Both networks use latent patches of size 1 × 2 × 2,
giving 32,760 tokens in the same spatiotemporal order. The refiner has 10 blocks of width 1,024, 16
attention heads and an MLP expansion ratio of 4. Its blocks alternate between self-attention with three-
dimensional rotary position embeddings and cross-attention to text and base-model features. The base
model’s noise-level embedding modulates each refiner block through adaptive layer normalization.
We read hidden states after base blocks 4, 12, 20 and 30, corresponding to zero-based code indices 3,
11, 19 and 29. At each token position, we concatenate the four 1,536-dimensional states and fuse them
with a 6144 →1024 →1024 MLP with a GELU activation. We project the 4,096-dimensional text
embeddings to the same width, add learned segment embeddings to distinguish text from hidden-state
features and concatenate the two token sequences before layer normalization. The refiner processes
the one-step clean prediction and maps its output back to a velocity residual of the original latent
shape. It contains 146.58 million parameters, approximately 10.3% of the 1.419-billion-parameter
base transformer. The combined transformer and refiner contain 1.566 billion parameters, excluding
the frozen VAE and text encoder.
Data and preprocessing.
The final training mixture contains 46,572 WISA-80K clips and 20,000
curated OpenVidHD clips, for 66,572 video–text pairs. The WISA subset retains clips with visual-
quality scores of at least 4.0 and motion scores of at least 0.1. For WISA, we concatenate the
video caption with its physical-law annotation, following the dataset’s level-B conditioning. For
OpenVidHD (Nan et al., 2025), we use the supplied caption. We shuffle the concatenated datasets
without additional source reweighting. Each training example contains 81 frames sampled uniformly
across the complete clip, preserving the temporal extent of the depicted event. Short clips reuse

sampled frame indices to obtain the same length. We resize frames while preserving their aspect ratio
and center-crop them to 480 × 832 before VAE encoding.
Training.
We jointly train the base model and refiner for five epochs on the processed 66,572-clip
dataset using AdamW, with learning rates of 5×10−6 and 10−4, respectively, momentum coefficients
(0.9, 0.999) and weight decay 0.01. The learning rates start at one-third of these values for five
scheduler steps and then remain at the nominal rates. Training uses four NVIDIA H200 GPUs with a
global batch size of 16, bfloat16 mixed precision and gradient checkpointing, taking approximately
201 hours including checkpointing. The base uses the scheduler-weighted flow-matching loss, while
the refiner minimizes residual mean squared error plus a cosine-direction loss weighted by 0.1, with
unit overall refiner-loss weight. All three benchmarks use the resulting paired weights.

VBench.
We use the released VBench prompt suite and evaluators (Huang et al., 2024) to measure

VideoPhy2.
We evaluate the 590-prompt test set, including its 180-prompt HARD subset, with
the released VideoPhy2 automatic evaluator (Bansal et al., 2026). Generation uses the benchmark’s
expanded captions, while scoring uses the original action captions. The evaluator assigns ratings
from 1 to 5 for physical commonsense (PC) and semantic adherence (SA). The PC and SA columns
report the percentage of ratings at least 4, and Joint reports the percentage satisfying both thresholds.
Joint therefore measures whether the requested event is depicted with plausible physics, combining
two requirements that the separate scores assess individually.
FinePhyEval.
We evaluate all 65 FinePhyEval prompts using their released question
graphs (Pothiraj et al., 2026). These graphs decompose each event into object existence, action exe-
cution and physical behavior. Our local evaluation uses GPT-5.5 to answer the fixed questions from
sampled video frames in the two-step PQSG question-answering procedure. A failed prerequisite
propagates failure to dependent questions, so a physics judgment also requires the relevant objects
and actions to be present. Object, Action and Physics are the fractions of satisfied questions in each
category after this propagation, with all questions retained in their respective denominators. Overall
is the corresponding fraction across all questions, weighting categories by their question counts. All
four scores lie between 0 and 1.

this clean image latent and assign its tokens noise level zero in the base transformer. Both the base

to the refiner, so each network is updated by its own objective. Training begins on 16 NVIDIA H200

Physics-IQ Verified.
The image-to-video evaluation covers 66 physical scenarios from three camera
viewpoints, yielding 198 image–text pairs. We retain the first 80 frames of each generated video for
the benchmark’s five-second evaluation at 16 frames per second. The released evaluator compares
generated continuations with recorded reference videos at 540 × 960 resolution, using motion masks
and RGB frames without an LLM judge. Spatial measures the intersection-over-union of regions that
exhibit motion anywhere in the clip. Spatio-temporal measures motion-mask overlap at corresponding
frames, while Weighted Spatial compares how frequently each pixel belongs to a moving region.
MSE measures the mean squared difference between generated and reference RGB frames.
For each scenario and viewpoint, the evaluator normalizes these measurements by the corresponding
comparison between two real recordings of the same event. The three overlap metrics are divided by
this real-to-real reference, while MSE uses the inverse ratio so that higher is better. Each normalized
component is clipped to [0, 1], and Verified Score averages the four components and then the scenario–
viewpoint pairs. Table 4 reports all scores on a 0–100 scale. All rows in Table 4 use the benchmark’s
setting without LLM support.

and generate a single sample per condition, without Best-of-N selection. Because Yuan et al. (2026)

decrease. These discrepancies suggest practical saturation of FID as a perceptual ranking signal in
this regime near 1, where small score differences are an unreliable standalone indicator of generation
quality. FID compares only the means and covariances of Inception-v3 features, so improvements
in object structure and fine detail need not yield a lower score. Jayasumana et al. (2024) document
