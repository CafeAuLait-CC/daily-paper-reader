## ABSTRACT 

Human responses to sound depend not only on its semantic content, but also on the evolving spatial relationship between source and listener, conveyed through spa-tial audio and its temporal dynamics. Modeling human reactions to such dynamic spatial audio is important for character animation, human behavior analysis, and robotics. As of yet, existing audio-driven motion generation methods primarily focus on non-spatial or static acoustic conditions, leaving this important problem largely underexplored. To bridge this gap, we formulate the novel task of dy-namic spatial-audio-driven human motion generation and introduce two motion-captured datasets. We introduce the Dy namic Spatial Audio and Motion dataset (DySAM) as the first dataset and benchmark for this task, providing synchro-nized binaural audio, 3D human motions, and moving sound source trajectories. Given the importance of dynamic spatial cues of sound sources, we further pro-vide the Dy namic Spatial Audio Localization dataset (DySAL), an auxiliary bin-aural localization dataset that pairs diverse acoustic events with head poses and moving source trajectories to support spatial audio perception. For benchmark-ing, we propose MOMO for human Mo tion generation from Mo ving Sounds, a diffusion-based framework guided by three coupled principles: localization, un-derstanding, and generation. MOMO performs localization by estimating head-centric spatial direction cues of the moving source directly from raw binaural au-dio, avoiding reliance on ground-truth source coordinates. It performs understand-ing by extracting frame-wise audio-content representations, and performs genera-tion by conditioning motion diffusion on both semantic features and dynamic spa-tial cues. Moreover, MOMO incorporates frame-wise motion correction during denoising, enabling temporally adaptive motion synthesis under evolving audio-spatial cues. Experiments show that MOMO outperforms existing baselines in motion fidelity, audio-motion alignment, and physical plausibility, demonstrating the potential of dynamic spatial audio for embodied motion generation. Figure 2: Diverse Reactions to Moving Sounds. Human responses to sound arise from integrating semantic mean-ing with spatial audio cues and the temporal dynamics of sound sources . A barking dog approaching from behind may elicit avoid-ance, whereas a speeding car approaching may prompt reorienta-tion. Crucially, the same sound can evoke different actions depend-ing on its source’s location and motion relative to the listener. Mod-eling this perception–action coupling supports context-aware vir-tual humans and embodied agents in character animation, virtual reality, human–computer interaction, and embodied intelligence. Existing human motion datasets and methods have mainly focused on conditions such as action labels (Guo et al., 2020), text descrip-tions (Guo et al., 2022), music (Zhuang et al., 2022; Li et al., 2021; Tseng et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023; 2024; Luo et al., 2024), and speech (Ao et al., 2022; 2023; Alexanderson et al., 2023). Recently, to the best of our knowledge, MOSPA (Xu et al., 2025) introduced the first spatial-audio-driven human motion dataset, SAM, along with a corresponding motion generation method, showing that spatial audio cues are crucial for generating realistic human motions. However, the SAM dataset mainly focuses on a static sound source, where the sound source position remains fixed within each sequence. Moreover, MOSPA requires the ground-truth absolute position of the sound source as a condition for motion generation, which is difficult to obtain in real-world scenarios and limits its ap-plicability to embodied agents. Therefore, dynamic spatial-audio-driven human motion generation, which models human motion responses to time-varying spatial audio from moving sound sources, remains largely underexplored, especially in practical settings without access to ground-truth sound source positions. To address this gap, we introduce DySAM, a dataset for dynamic spatial-audio-driven human motion generation. DySAM contains synchronized binaural audio, 3D human motion, and dynamic sound source trajectories captured in an indoor environment. Human motion and sound source trajectories are recorded using a Vicon motion capture system (Vicon, 2026), while binaural audio is captured by high-sensitivity microphones near the actors’ ears, approximating the audio perceived by the actors. The main dataset contains 2,336 ten-second audio-motion sequences, totaling 23,360 seconds (ap-proximately 6.5 hours), with motion at 30 FPS and audio at 48 kHz. It covers 42 audio types and 109 unique audio sources, including human sounds, animal sounds, vehicles, and environmental sounds, with 8 subjects and a binary genre label indicating sensitive or insensitive reactions. In addition, we provide DySAL, an auxiliary sound source localization dataset of 11,730 seconds, pairing binaural audio of diverse acoustic events with listener head 6DoF poses and frame-wise moving sound source annotations to support dynamic spatial audio perception. Motivated by the fact that human reactions to spatial sound depend on both where the sound comes from and what the sound is, we formulate spatial-audio-driven motion generation around three cou-pled functional components: localization , understanding , and generation . Guided by this design principle, we propose MOMO, a diffusion-based framework for generating human motion condi-tioned on dynamic spatial audio. For localization, MOMO estimates frame-wise head-centric spatial cues of sound source from raw binaural audio using a sound source localization network, eliminating the reliance on privileged ground-truth source coordinates. For understanding, a pretrained CLAP audio encoder (Wu et al., 2023) encodes the binaural audio to obtain frame-wise audio-content features. For generation, MOMO employs a diffusion-based motion generator conditioned on the predicted spatial cues, CLAP audio features, and genre label. Since human motion needs to con-tinuously adapt to the evolving spatial cues of the sound source, while self-attention only provides implicit interactions between motion tokens and condition tokens, we further introduce a Frame-wise Motion Correction module in the Transformer denoiser to explicitly refine motion tokens with frame-wise conditions, along with a Correction Strength Predictor that adaptively controls the cor-rection magnitude for each frame. By using head-centric DoA estimated directly from binaural audio, MOMO provides a more prac-tical formulation for spatial-audio-driven motion generation in embodied robot settings. Compared with MOSPA (Xu et al., 2025), which relies on ground-truth global sound source coordinates, our egocentric formulation avoids privileged source-location annotations and better reflects how embod-2ied agents infer dynamic sound direction from their own auditory observations. Extensive quantita-tive and qualitative experiments show that MOMO outperforms existing baselines, producing more realistic motions that respond to dynamic spatial-audio cues. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce two datasets: DySAM, to the best of our knowledge the first dataset for dynamic spatial-audio-driven human motion generation, and DySAL, an auxiliary sound source localiza-tion dataset collected in indoor reverberant environments. • We propose MOMO, a practical framework for generating human motion from binaural audio without requiring ground-truth source coordinates. It integrates sound source localization, CLAP-based audio content encoding, and diffusion-based motion generation to model human responses to moving sound sources. • Extensive quantitative and qualitative experiments show that MOMO generates natural, plausi-ble, and spatially consistent human motion, better aligning with real motion and outperforming existing baselines. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Conditional Human Motion Generation. Conditional human motion generation has been exten-sively studied under various conditions, such as action labels (Guo et al., 2020), textual descriptions (Guo et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023; Dabral et al., 2023), trajectories (Cai et al., 2026), and motion language models that treat human motion as a foreign language (Jiang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). These methods generate diverse and natural motions from semantic conditions, but they generally do not consider audio, let alone its spatial attributes. In contrast, our work focuses on audio-driven conditions with explicit spatial information. 

Audio-Driven Human Motion Generation. Audio-driven human motion generation has been studied mainly in two directions. The first is music-driven dance generation (Tang et al., 2018; Zhuang et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022; 2023; Siyao et al., 2022; Tseng et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2024), which generates choreography that matches the rhythm and style of the music. The second is speech-driven gesture synthesis (Ginosar et al., 2019; Yoon et al., 2020; Ao et al., 2022; 2023; Alexanderson et al., 2023), which generates co-speech gestures from speech content and prosody. Most of these methods use monaural or stereo audio and ignore the spatial attributes of the sound source. MOSPA (Xu et al., 2025) is the first to study spatial-audio-driven human motion generation with binaural audio, but it assumes a static sound source position. Different from MOSPA, we consider dynamic spatial audio, where the source moves over time, and the DoA changes accordingly. 

Spatial Sound Understanding. Estimating the direction of a sound source from binaural signals is a classic problem in acoustic scene analysis, for instance using binaural cues combined with deep learning (Garc´ ıa-Barrios et al., 2022; Min et al., 2025). Recent works also model room acoustics and room impulse responses to support sound source localization in complex indoor scenes (Chen et al., 2022; Luo et al., 2022; Ratnarajah et al., 2022; Um et al., 2025; Lan et al., 2024; 2026). In this paper, we adopt a sound source localization network to predict the head-centric DoA from binaural audio, which serves as the spatial condition for motion generation. This makes our framework practical in real-world scenarios where ground-truth source coordinates are unavailable.

Data Capture Setup. We collect the data in an indoor environment with natural reverbera-tion using a Vicon motion capture system (Vi-con, 2026), which records both human motion and the trajectory of a moving sound source (as shown in Fig. 3). We use an omnidirectional speaker to play the dynamic audio. We choose binaural audio as the audio modality, follow-ing the SAM dataset (Xu et al., 2025), as it is a common form of spatial audio that aligns with human auditory perception and can be readily applied to virtual characters and robotic platforms. Binaural audio is recorded using two microphones placed near the actor’s ears, allowing the signals to approximate the auditory input the actor perceives during capture. Specifically, we use Sound Professionals MS-TFB-2 microphones (The Sound Professionals, 2026) and a Deity PR-2 recorder (Deity Microphones, 2026) for binaural audio recording. The audio recorder is synchronized with the mocap system using timecode, ensuring temporal alignment among binaural audio, human mo-tion, and sound source trajectories. 3.1 DYSAM D ATASET 

Our main dataset, DySAM, is collected for dynamic spatial-audio-driven human motion generation, with visualization snapshots provided in Fig. 4. Each sequence consists of a 10-second binaural au-dio clip, the corresponding 3D human motion stored in the SMPL-X format (Pavlakos et al., 2019), and the trajectory of a moving sound source in the world coordinate system. As summarized in Tab. 1, DySAM contains 2,336 synchronized audio-motion sequences, amounting to 23,360 seconds (approximately 6.5 hours) of paired data. The captured motions are represented as SMPL-X model (Pavlakos et al., 2019). The audio’s sampling rate is 48,000 Hz, while the motion is represented at 30 FPS for network training. DySAM covers 42 audio types and 109 unique audio sources, spanning diverse daily-life acoustic events such as animal sounds, vehicles, alarms, music, and human speech. It was collected with 8 actors, including 5 female and 3 male. Each sequence is annotated with one of two reaction genres, sensitive or insensitive, with an approximately balanced 1:1 ratio. 3.2 DYSAL D ATASET 

We additionally collect DySAL, an auxiliary sound source localization dataset, using the same motion capture setup. It provides abundant spatial supervision for training the SSL network, enabling robust estimation of dynamic head-centric DoA cues from binaural audio. It contains 11,730 seconds, approximately 3.3 hours, of binaural audio paired with frame-wise sound source positions and the listener’s head 6DoF pose in the world coordinate system. These spatial cues can facilitate broader studies of spatial audio perception, such as absolute source localization and direction-of-arrival estimation. To ensure the diversity of acoustic events, we collect raw audio from AIST (Tsuchida et al., 2019), ESC-50 (Piczak, 2015), and UrbanSound8K (Salamon et al., 2014), covering a wide range of music, environmental, human, animal, mechanical, and urban sounds. ## 4 METHOD 

We propose MOMO, a diffusion-based framework for dynamic-spatial-audio-driven human motion generation. In real world, human reactions to spatial sound depend on both spatial direction and audio content. Accordingly, MOMO consists of three coupled functional components: localiza-tion , understanding , and generation , as illustrated in Fig. 5. For localization, a sound source lo-calization (SSL) network estimates a frame-wise head-centric direction-of-arrival (DoA) sequence 

D = {dt ∈ R3}Tt=1 from raw binaural audio, T is the max length of motion (Sec. 4.1). For under-standing, a pretrained CLAP audio encoder extracts temporally aligned audio content representa-tions A = {at}Tt=1 from the mono audio. For generation, a transformer-encoder-based conditional diffusion model synthesizes motion through frame-wise correction conditioned on D, A, the genre label g, and the diffusion timestep τ . Rather than relying solely on attention mechanism, it incor-porates Frame-wise Motion Correction and Correction Strength Predictor , allowing the denoiser to adaptively determine the correction magnitude for each frame under dynamic spatial-audio condi-tions (Sec. 4.2). We formulate motion generation as conditional denoising diffusion (Ho et al., 2020). Given noisy motion xτ at diffusion step τ , the denoising network predicts the clean motion sequence x0 condi-tioned on audio content, DoA, genre, and timestep embeddings. For audio content, we average the binaural signal into a mono waveform and feed it into a pretrained CLAP encoder (Wu et al., 2023). The extracted hidden features are pooled over channel and frequency dimensions and temporally aligned with the motion sequence, producing frame-wise audio features A = {at}Tt=1 .Building on the Transformer encoder of Xu et al. (2025), we project xτ into motion tokens 

m = {mt}Tt=1 and concatenate them with condition tokens derived from audio features, DoA features, genre, and the diffusion-step embedding. The concatenated tokens are fed into the Trans-6former encoder for conditional denoising. Since the attention mechanism only provides implicit interactions between motion tokens and the frame-wise condition tokens, we further introduce a 

Frame-wise Motion Correction module to explicitly correct motion tokens based on frame-wise conditions, together with a Correction Strength Predictor that adaptively determines the correction strength. 

Frame-wise Motion Correction. To explicitly leverage both global and frame-wise conditions, we apply a residual correction module based on Adaptive Layer Normalization (AdaLN) on after each Transformer encoder layer to refine the intermediate motion token, which is formulated as 

m′ = m + α ⊙ δ, (1) where δ = {δi}Ti=1 denotes the frame-wise correction, and α = {αi}Ti=1 is an frame-wise correc-tion strength. The modulation condition c = {ci}Ti=1 consists of four components: the timestep embedding t, the genre embedding g, the frame-wise audio feature A, and the frame-wise DoA fea-ture D. The unified condition sequence is obtained by summing these components at each frame, with the global timestep and genre embeddings broadcast to all T frames: ci = et + eg + ai + di.This condition sequence is then used to modulate the motion tokens. ## 5 EXPERIMENT 

Experimental Setup. We split the main DySAM dataset into train/validation/test sets with a ra-tio of 8:1:1. For training the sound source localization network, we transform the global sound source position into head-centric unit direction vector using the head 6DoF pose, and use it as the ground-truth DoA. Regarding metrics, we use FID, R-precision, Diversity (Guo et al., 2022), Av-erage Pairwise Distance (APD) (Dou et al., 2023), and a physics-based skating ratio, as detailed in Appendix D. We introduce a multi-branch motion-audio-source evaluator for feature extraction, aiming to better measure the spatial alignment between the generated motion and the moving sound source; details are provided in Appendix C. We compare with diffusion-based and VQ-based base-lines, including EDGE (Tseng et al., 2023), POPDG (Luo et al., 2024), MOSPA (Xu et al., 2025), and MoMask++ (Guo et al., 2025). A.1 MOTION CAPTURE DESIGN 

At the beginning of each clip, the dynamic sound source, i.e., a speaker, starts from one of four azimuthal regions relative to the actor: front-left, rear-left, rear-right, and front-right. This design enables us to capture diverse human reactions to sounds arriving from different directions. The trajectory of the sound source is designed to be either straight or curved. Moreover, the sound source either moves toward the actor and reaches the actor’s position, or passes by without contact. These two movement patterns elicit fundamentally different responses. For example, an actor may react differently when a dog or a car rushes toward them than when it merely passes by. We further define two reaction styles: insensitive and sensitive, to represent different levels of re-sponsiveness to the same acoustic event. For instance, when hearing an explosion, an insensitive actor may remain largely unreactive, whereas a sensitive actor may immediately move away from the sound source. The two reaction styles are approximately balanced in the dataset. A.2 MOTION CAPTURE AND AUDIO RECORDING SETUP 

The data are collected in a typical indoor environment with natural room reverberation. The motion capture system consists of 20 Vicon cameras installed around the capture space, recording both the human body motion and the sound source trajectory at 120 Hz. For the moving sound source, we use a cylindrical omni-directional speaker to play the audio, ensur-ing stable sound radiation in different directions as the speaker moves. For binaural audio recording, a high-sensitivity microphone is placed near each ear of the actor to capture the audio perceived by the actor as faithfully as possible. The two microphones are connected to a Deity PR-2 recorder (De-ity Microphones, 2026), which is synchronized with the Vicon system in advance using timecode. This setup ensures accurate temporal alignment between the recorded binaural audio and the corre-sponding motion capture data. For intuitively understand our data processing pipeline, we provide four visualization examples of the DySAM dataset in Fig. A1. 

Motion processing. During capture, the actor wears a motion capture suit with multiple reflective markers attached to the body, represented by the green points in Fig. A1 The Vicon system exports the captured data as C3D files, where each file stores the world coordinates of all body markers for every frame. We use Mosh++ (Mahmood et al., 2019) to fit each C3D sequence into the SMPL-X parameters. The fitted SMPL-X motion is then canonicalized by rotating the human body in the initial frame to face the positive y-axis and move the center of the body to the origin. For training the motion generation model, we further convert the SMPL-X motion into the 272-dimensional motion representation following (Xiao et al., 2025). 

Sound source location processing. To track the position of the sound source, i.e., the speaker, we attach eight reflective markers around the speaker (orange points in Fig. A1), which approximately correspond to the eight vertices of a cuboid. For each C3D sequence, we use the speaker markers in the initial frame as reference markers. We then estimate the speaker translation in each subsequent frame by solving the rigid alignment between the reference markers and the current-frame markers using singular value decomposition (SVD). The estimated translation is used as the global sound source location in our dataset, represented by the blue sphere in Fig. A1. In practice, some speaker markers may be missing in certain frames due to occlusion or tracking failure, making SVD-based estimation unreliable or infeasible. For these frames, we use Kalman filtering to smoothly interpolate and complete the missing sound source locations. 

Head-centric DoA processing. To train the sound source localization network, we further compute the head-centric sound source location. Specifically, for each frame, we perform forward kinematics on the fitted SMPL-X body model to obtain the translation and rotation of the head joint, i.e., joint 15. We then transform the global sound source location into the head coordinate system using the estimated head pose, yielding the head-centric sound source location as the direction-related supervision signal.

We provide additional implementation details of the sound source localization network used in Sec. 4.1. The SSL network estimates a head-centric direction of arrival from binaural audio. ## C EVALUATOR IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

Inspired by the evaluator design from (Xu et al., 2025), our evaluator is a motion–condition match-ing network , which measures how well a motion sequence corresponds to a given spatial audio condition. It maps both modalities into a shared latent space, in which the R-precision metrics are computed by nearest-neighbor retrieval and the FID is computed between the feature distributions of the ground-truth and the generated motions. Different from (Xu et al., 2025), whose condition only contains the acoustic content, our condition additionally involves the trajectory of the sound source, so that the evaluator is sensitive to the spatial correspondence between the moving sound source and the human reaction. 18 Network architecture. As illustrated in Fig. C2, the evaluator consists of a motion autoencoder and three bidirectional GRUs (Bi-GRUs), each with 4 layers and a hidden size of 512. The feature of a Bi-GRU is obtained by concatenating its last forward and backward hidden states, which yields a 1024-dimensional feature vector. The motion autoencoder follows a transformer encoder–decoder structure. The input motion se-quence x ∈ RT ×272 is first projected to a 512-dimensional latent space and added with a positional encoding, and is then encoded by a 4-layer transformer encoder with 4 attention heads and a 1024-dimensional feed-forward layer. The encoder output is averaged over time into a single latent vector and replicated along the temporal axis, which serves as the query of a transformer decoder of the same configuration attending to the encoder output. A final linear layer maps the decoder output back to the 272-dimensional motion space to produce the reconstructed motion ˆx. The output of the motion encoder is further encoded by a Bi-GRU to obtain the motion feature. The condition branch consists of an audio encoder and a sound source encoder. The audio encoder takes the traditional features described in Appendix E. The sound source encoder takes the absolute position of the sound source in the world coordinate system s ∈ RT ×3, projects it to 64 dimensions and encodes it with a Bi-GRU. The resulting audio and sound source features are concatenated and projected by a linear layer into the condition feature, whose dimension matches that of the motion feature. 

Training objective and details. The evaluator is optimized with two objectives. The motion au-toencoder is supervised by a reconstruction loss Lrecon , which encourages the latent space to pre-serve the motion content. The condition and motion representations are aligned through contrastive learning using the InfoNCE loss LInfoNCE (van den Oord et al., 2018). During training, we randomly zero the sound source feature of an entire sequence with a probability of 0.3, producing audio-only conditions. This enables the evaluator to also apply to settings where the sound source trajectory is unavailable, while the audio condition is always retained. We train the evaluator for up to 1,000 epochs using the Adam optimizer (Kingma & Ba, 2014) with a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5 and a batch size of 32. The evaluator is trained on all sequences of DySAM. 

## D EVALUATION METRICS 

Fr´ echet Inception Distance (FID). The FID is computed between the motion features of the ground-truth motions in the test set and those of the generated motions, where both sets of fea-tures are extracted by the frozen motion encoder of the evaluator. Gaussian distributions are fitted to the two feature sets, and the Fr´ echet distance between them serves as a metric for assessing the quality and fidelity of the synthesized motions. 

R-precision. We compute the R-precision within batches of 32 sequences of the test set, following the protocol of (Guo et al., 2022). For each batch, the conditions and the motion features are L2-normalized, and the cosine similarities between all condition–motion pairs are ranked, where the pool of each condition consists of its matched motion together with the other 31 in-batch motions as distractors. The R-precision is then obtained by determining the probability that the matched motion appears among the top-1, top-2 and top-3 ranked positions, and is averaged over all the batches. The metric is computed in both the condition-to-motion and the motion-to-condition retrieval directions. The query condition used in our experiments consists of the ground-truth binaural audio and the ground-truth sound source trajectory, independently of the condition adopted by each generator, so that all compared methods are evaluated against the same query set. The Real Motion row in the main tables corresponds to using the ground-truth motions as the retrieval target. 

Skating Ratio. We compute the skating ratio from recovered world-space foot joints, using the left and right foot joints indexed as 10 and 11 in the 22-joint SMPL protocol. For each foot, we compute its horizontal velocity on the ground plane at 30 Hz and smooth it with a 5-frame window. A foot is considered planted if it remains within 5 cm above the floor in adjacent frames and its smoothed horizontal speed is below 0.30 m/s. We count a valid contact frame as skating only when neither foot is planted. Airborne frames, where both feet are above 5 cm, and penetration frames, where any foot is below the floor plane, are excluded from the skating ratio and reported separately as float and penetration ratios. Our skating ratio formulation is modified from GMD (Karunratanakul et al., 2023), which marks a frame as skating when either foot is close to the ground and moves faster than 0.50 m/s. This can falsely flag frames where one foot is stably planted while the other foot briefly moves or drags near the floor, since the original metric evaluates each foot independently and ignores whether the body has a valid support foot. In contrast, our metric detects the absence of any stable support foot. This support-foot criterion avoids counting normal swing-foot motion as skating when the other foot stably supports the body. To verify the metric behavior, we evaluate both definitions on the ground-truth motions of DySAM. As shown in Tab. D2, the original formulation reports a skating ratio of 13.9% on ground-truth data, which is inconsistent with the fact that these motions are captured from real humans and should contain little foot skating. In contrast, our formulation gives a much lower ratio of 1.26%, indicating that the revised metric better reflects actual foot skating artifacts rather than false positives caused by normal foot motion or representation noise. For each audio case, participants watch the videos with the corresponding dynamic spatial audio and select the best generated result under each evaluation aspect. This indicates that MOMO generates motions that are not only more natural and fluent, but also better aligned with dynamic spatial-audio cues.
