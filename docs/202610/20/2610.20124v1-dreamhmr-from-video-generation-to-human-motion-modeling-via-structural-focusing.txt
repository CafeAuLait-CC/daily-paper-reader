ABSTRACT
Human mesh recovery (HMR) provides a geometric foundation for understanding
human motion, but accurately reconstructing both body motion and fine hand ar-
ticulation from videos remains challenging, especially in real-world scenes where
occlusion and partial visibility are common. Video generation pretraining offers
rich spatial and temporal priors for this task, but its focus on visual synthesis
leaves a gap between these representations and explicit human geometry. We
present DreamHMR, a general framework that adapts video generation priors for
complete body-and-hand reconstruction. It follows a global-to-local strategy, first
grounding video representations in human geometry and then refining body and
hand detail with reliable local evidence. Global Body Representation Transfer
trains the video backbone to predict complete human renderings, turning its na-
tive prediction task into a means of learning surface correspondence and posed
geometry. Local Joint Feature Enhancement combines contextual estimates with
reliable local evidence to recover fine body and hand detail, while limiting inter-
ference from occluders and background. Experiments on five datasets with four
video backbones demonstrate strong reconstruction performance across backbone
families. Under a unified evaluation protocol, our best model outperforms the best
existing methods on all reported metrics of 3DPW, EMDB-1, and RICH, reduc-
ing body MPJPE by 4.2%, 4.7%, and 6.1%, respectively. In more challenging
occlusion settings on held-out BEDLAM and EgoBody sequences, DreamHMR
reduces overall body and hand MPJPE by 7.0% and 10.0%, respectively. Our code
will be made publicly available.

INTRODUCTION
Human mesh recovery (HMR) reconstructs the 3D human body from visual observations, providing
a geometric foundation for understanding and modeling human motion. Its estimates support a wide
range of downstream applications, from structured motion analysis for action recognition (Goel
et al., 2023) to motion references for humanoid robot teleoperation and imitation learning (He et al.,
2024; Fu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2026). The field has advanced rapidly, with
progress in monocular parametric body estimation (Kanazawa et al., 2018; Kolotouros et al., 2019),
temporal modeling from video (Kocabas et al., 2020), and large-scale learning from diverse im-
agery (Goel et al., 2023). Despite these advances, recovering complete body and hand motion from
real-world videos remains challenging, as occlusion and partial visibility leave incomplete and un-
even visual evidence across body parts and frames.
Recovering motion from such observations requires both inferring missing structure and identifying
reliable local evidence. Information from visible body parts and neighboring frames can help con-
strain unobserved body and hand configurations. However, local features sampled near a joint may
correspond to an occluder or the background rather than the target person, so incorporating them
indiscriminately can introduce misleading cues. A reconstruction model therefore needs to combine
contextual inference of the complete body with selective use of local observations for detailed body
and hand recovery.

Video generation pretraining offers a promising starting point for learning such contextual repre-
sentations (Team Wan et al., 2025). By learning to synthesize videos, these models capture spatial
appearance and temporal dependencies within a shared representation, offering potentially useful
cues for reasoning about body and hand motion under partial observations. However, their training
objective targets visual synthesis rather than explicit human pose and surface geometry. Transferring
these representations to human reconstruction therefore requires adapting them to the underlying
structure of the human body.
To this end, we present DreamHMR, a framework that adapts video generation representations for
complete body-and-hand reconstruction through geometric supervision and evidence-gated local
refinement. First, Global Body Representation Transfer trains the video backbone to predict render-
ings of complete SMPL-X bodies (Pavlakos et al., 2019), turning its video prediction objective into
a proxy for learning human structure. Canonical surface colors encode correspondence across poses,
while shading conveys pose-dependent geometry, providing supervision for body regions even when
they are absent from the observations. Second, Local Joint Feature Enhancement refines contextual
body and hand estimates using features sampled around predicted joints. Supervised evidence scores
predict target-person support at these locations and weight the local updates, limiting the influence
of features drawn from occluders or background.
Experiments spanning five datasets and four video backbones demonstrate strong reconstruction per-
formance, with gains extending across backbone families. On standard benchmarks, our Wan-5B
model outperforms the best existing methods across all reported metrics under a unified evaluation
protocol, reducing body MPJPE by 4.2%, 4.7%, and 6.1% on 3DPW, EMDB-1, and RICH, respec-
tively. In challenging occlusion settings on held-out BEDLAM and EgoBody sequences, it reduces
overall body and hand MPJPE by 7.0% and 10.0%, respectively.
DreamHMR makes the following contributions:
• We bring pretrained video generation priors to complete body-and-hand reconstruction
through Global Body Representation Transfer, offering a new approach to recovering hu-
man motion from occluded and incomplete observations.
• We introduce Local Joint Feature Enhancement to recover fine body and hand detail from
reliable local evidence, complementing global reconstruction within a general framework
applicable across different video backbones.
• Extensive experiments on five datasets and four video backbones demonstrate that
DreamHMR outperforms the best existing methods on standard HMR benchmarks, with
body and hand MPJPE reduced by 7.0% and 10.0%, respectively, in challenging occlusion
settings.

RELATED WORK
Human reconstruction from images and videos.
Human mesh recovery estimates parametric
bodies from images (Kanazawa et al., 2018; Kolotouros et al., 2019), with video models adding
temporal context (Kocabas et al., 2020; Goel et al., 2023). PromptHMR-Vid combines full-image
features with a box-prompted temporal transformer (Wang et al., 2025); GENMO learns a motion
distribution for generation and estimation (Li et al., 2025). Whole-body methods recover articulated
bodies and hands (Pavlakos et al., 2019; Lin et al., 2023b; Li et al., 2023; Baradel et al., 2024;
Moon, 2026), scale training data and model capacity (Cai et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2026; Yang et al.,
2026); dedicated models recover detailed hand meshes (Pavlakos et al., 2024; Potamias et al., 2025).
DreamHMR uses a shared video generation representation for body-and-hand recovery.
Generative representations for reconstruction.
Image diffusion features support correspon-
dence estimation (Tang et al., 2023; Stracke et al., 2025), while video diffusion features support
motion and geometry tasks (Vélez et al., 2025; Nam et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026). DPMesh
conditions a text-to-image diffusion backbone on 2D observations for occluded mesh recovery (Zhu
et al., 2024), and ADHMR optimizes diffusion-based mesh predictions with preference data (Shen
et al., 2025). DreamHMR adapts video generation features with complete human renderings to
encode surface correspondence and posed geometry for body-and-hand recovery.

Reconstruction under occlusion.
Occluded HMR addresses body reconstruction with incomplete
observations (Khirodkar et al., 2022). PARE aggregates neighboring regions with part-guided atten-
tion (Kocabas et al., 2021), and Divide and Fuse merges part meshes from partially visible im-
ages (Luan et al., 2024). Human and joint tokens structure decoding (Sun et al., 2024), while local
image-aligned features refine hands (Potamias et al., 2025). Nearby features may describe back-
ground or another person; DreamHMR learns evidence scores under supervision to weight their
updates.

METHOD
DreamHMR recovers complete body-and-hands motion through Global Body Representation Trans-
fer, which adapts video features with complete rendered human structure, and Local Joint Feature
Enhancement, which refines anatomical states with local image evidence.

GLOBAL BODY REPRESENTATION TRANSFER
Global Body Representation Transfer injects complete projected human structure into the video
representation over the full image canvas, including regions outside the observation mask.

LOCAL JOINT FEATURE ENHANCEMENT
The structured decoder combines global anatomical states with local joint descriptors: states summa-
rize complete body-part context, while descriptors locate joints and retrieve nearby image evidence.
Part and Joint Feature Focus.
Let Fk be the adapted DiT feature map at latent frame k, projected
to the decoder dimension d = 512, with K = 21 latent frames. Four learned part queries and 52
learned joint queries extract part and joint information from shared features through cross-attention:

TRAINING AND INFERENCE
We first adapt the DiT with rendered-video flow matching, then jointly train the backbone and struc-
tured decoder for parameter recovery using
Lrecovery = LSMPLX + λsupportLsupport,
(10)

Training mixes full, static, and moving observations; Appendix A details losses and freezing.
At inference, the adapted DiT processes the VAE-encoded RGB once at τ = 0, conditioned on
masks, box tracks, and fixed text context. The structured decoder maps its features to SMPL-X
parameters and presence, following the same feature path used in parameter-recovery training.

EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate DreamHMR on standard human mesh recovery benchmarks and complete body-and-
hand recovery under occlusion across four video backbones. Ablations examine rendering supervi-
sion, local evidence mechanisms, and temporal context. Our code is available in the supplementary
material.
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
We train on BEDLAM (Black et al., 2023), BEDLAM2 (Tesch et al., 2025), EgoBody (Zhang et al.,
2022), ARCTIC (Fan et al., 2023), InterCap (Huang et al., 2022b), and IDEA400 (Lin et al., 2023a),
using full and spatially masked 81-frame windows. Occlusion evaluation and ablations use separate
holdouts of 90 BEDLAM and 30 EgoBody sequences each, disjoint from training and validation.
We compare PromptHMR-Vid (Wang et al., 2025), GENMO (Li et al., 2025), SMPLest-X (Yin
et al., 2026), 3DB-DINOv3 (Yang et al., 2026), and Hand4Whole++ (Moon, 2026), and instantiate
DreamHMR with Cosmos-2B (NVIDIA et al., 2025), LTX-2B (HaCohen et al., 2024), Wan-1.3B,
and Wan-5B (Team Wan et al., 2025). Methods share observed RGB and target tracks; video meth-
ods use full windows, while image methods use track crops without temporal smoothing. We report
MPJPE, Procrustes-aligned MPJPE (PA-MPJPE), and per-vertex error (PVE) on 3DPW test (von
Marcard et al., 2018), EMDB-1 (Kaufmann et al., 2023), and RICH test (Huang et al., 2022a). Oc-
clusion results report pelvis-relative body and wrist-relative hand MPJPE. Errors are in millimeters.
Appendices A and B detail backbones, observation construction, alignment, and aggregation.
4.2
EVALUATION ON WIDELY USED HMR BENCHMARKS
DreamHMR attains the lowest error on every metric of the widely used human mesh recovery bench-
marks. In Table 1, Wan-5B improves on every metric: MPJPE by 2.3 mm on 3DPW, 2.9 mm on
EMDB-1, and 3.2 mm on RICH, with PVE reductions of 1.3, 3.1, and 3.5 mm. All four DreamHMR
backbones outperform the best external 3DPW body MPJPE result.
Figure 3 shows how this advantage appears in real scenes. In the boxed close-up, SMPLest-X (Yin
et al., 2026) lays the fingers flat against the torso, leaving the bowl unheld, while DreamHMR keeps
the fingers wrapped around the food and the bowl rim. See Appendix D.3 for temporal comparisons.

achieves the lowest Full-input errors, with larger gains under occlusion.

CONCLUSION
DreamHMR adapts video generation representations for complete body-and-hand recovery under
occlusion. Complete human renderings transfer surface correspondence and posed geometry into
the backbone, while evidence-gated local features refine contextual body-part estimates. Across

Tracking and supervision.
A frozen HumanArt YOLOX-X detector with ByteTrack provides the
target tracks from each observed RGB sequence. The presence label is positive when the observation
region contains at least 32 visible target-person pixels. A joint receives a positive evidence label
when its ground-truth projection lies in both the observation region and the target’s visible instance
mask. Evidence supervision uses frames with reliable, aligned visible-instance masks; other samples
contribute their available geometric and 2D labels. Complete body-and-hand geometry remains
supervised wherever the corresponding annotations are available.
Evaluation sets.
The occlusion evaluation and ablation holdouts each contain 90 BEDLAM and
30 EgoBody source sequences. Each sequence supplies two source windows; each window yields
one full, eight static, and eight dynamic observations, giving 4,080 windows per holdout. The two
holdouts are disjoint from each other, training, and validation, with identities, recording sessions,
source motions, and scenes separated across splits. EgoBody examples use third-person videos with
body-and-hand annotations. Evaluation on widely used benchmarks uses the official 3DPW test,
EMDB-1, and RICH test sets with their original RGB inputs.
Shared inputs and output alignment.
All compared methods receive the same observed RGB
and target tracks. Video methods process complete windows; image methods process per-frame
crops from the shared tracks. We use released inference models without test-time flipping or addi-
tional temporal smoothing. SMPLest-X uses the official H40 weights, and 3DB-DINOv3 includes
its released hand decoder. Public body evaluation uses 14/24/24 joints for 3DPW/EMDB-1/RICH,
respectively, with common joint regressors and frozen topology mappings for SMPL-X, MHR, and
SMPL-X/MANO predictions. MPJPE and PVE use pelvis alignment; PA-MPJPE additionally fits
a similarity transform. Under occlusion, body MPJPE uses 21 non-root joints relative to the pelvis;
hand MPJPE uses 15 joints per hand relative to the wrist, with equal left/right weighting.

Video generation pretraining provides a useful initialization for reconstruction under a fixed task-
training budget. Table 9 compares pretrained and randomly initialized DiTs within the same Wan
architecture after 10,000 task-training steps, with all 81 frames evaluated. The paired runs share task
data, the structured readout, evidence gating, and optimization settings; the corresponding pretrained
VAE and text encoder are retained in both runs. Pretrained initialization reduces body/hand MPJPE

evaluates the two representation choices, which differ in architecture, pretraining, and temporal
encoding.
