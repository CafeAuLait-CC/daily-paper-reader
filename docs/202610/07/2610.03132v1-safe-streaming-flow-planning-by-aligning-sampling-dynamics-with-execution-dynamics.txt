Abstract: Generative planners based on diffusion /flow matching can learn to synthesize long-horizon trajectories from demonstrations. However, real-world deployment requires (i) enforcing safety constraints during execution and (ii) tight online replanning at fast execution rates. Prior safe diffusion /flow plan-ners generate the agent’s full trajectory at once , while repeatedly perturbing in-termediate states to satisfy safety constraints. This approach is not only com-putationally intensive, but also introduces distribution shift since the learned sampling dynamics is distinct from the system’s execution dynamics. We pro-pose SafeStreamingFlow , a goal-conditioned planner that aligns flow sam-pling dynamics with execution dynamics by sequentially integrating a learned state vector field with hierarchical state prediction. Importantly, we need to enforce safety constraints only for the executed step via high order con-trol barrier functions. Across navigation, racing, and locomotion benchmarks, 

SafeStreamingFlow reduces planning latency and improves safety compared to existing methods, while maintaining competitive goal-reaching success. Code is available on our project page: https://jang-seunghwan.github. io/SafeStreamingFlowPlanning/ .

Keywords: Safe Planning, Flow Matching, Control Barrier Functions 

1 Introduction 

Generative models [1, 2] that have driven rapid progress in image and language generation are in-creasingly being adapted to robotic tasks, particularly diffusion [3, 4, 5] and flow matching [6]. Deploying such models for robot planning, however, requires more than producing a plausible tra-jectory. Most diffusion/flow planners typically generate an entire horizon as a single object in tra-jectory space and refine it through K denoising/sampling steps, where each step updates all H states in the trajectory [3]. In a closed-loop setting, this full-horizon procedure must be repeated at every control cycle to incorporate state feedback and new observations, generating H × K intermediate states per cycle [7, 8]. Another key challenge is enforcing safety constraints imposed at inference time, possibly never seen during training, in a manner that is both reliable and compatible with closed-loop execution. An especially promising approach to certified safety is to incorporate control barrier functions (CBFs) [13, 14] into the sampling procedure by solving quadratic pro-grams (QPs) at inference time; the constraints then hold whenever each QP is feasible. However, because intermediate sampling states are not physically executed, enforcing safety during sampling can disrupt dynamical consistency of the generated trajectories and induce local-traps , discontinu-ous trajectories that remain safe but infeasible to execute [6, 15]. In addition, safety enforcement further increases the computational burden because CBF-based cor-rections are applied across both the horizon length H and sampling iterations K, resulting in (H × K)

QP solves to generate a single trajectory. Streaming-based formulations [8, 7] have been introduced for real-time execution by aligning sampling time with execution time. However, existing stream-ing approaches are developed primarily as policies for action imitation, leaving both the system’s execution dynamics and start–goal-conditioned planning unaddressed. We propose SafeStreamingFlow , a safe streaming planner that aligns the generative process with execution (Figure 1). Instead of sampling at the full trajectory level, SafeStreamingFlow 

integrates a learned state vector field in physical time and produces a one-step-ahead reference. In this one-step prediction, however, the learned field tends to become a local inertial extrapola-tor rather than a goal-conditioned planner; we suppress this with a hierarchical state predictor that sequentially updates position then velocity. The one-step-ahead reference is then tracked by a PD controller, and the resulting nominal control input is filtered through high order control barrier func-tions that minimally modify the executed input to satisfy safety constraints that were never seen during training. 2 Related Work 

Generative models such as diffusion [1] and flow matching [2] have recently been adopted for robot policy learning and planning by modeling multi-modal state/action distributions from expert demon-strations [3, 16, 17]. Diffusion-based planners synthesize long-horizon plans through iterative de-noising in trajectory space, while flow matching reduces the number of sampling steps via ODE-based generation [18]. More recently, streaming formulations have been proposed to better align generation with closed-loop control by producing incremental updates [19, 7, 8]; however, these methods are primarily formulated as action policies and do not directly address the system’s execu-tion dynamics or finite-horizon, start–goal-conditioned planning. Safety in generative planning is commonly incorporated either through guidance [11, 12, 20], such as classifier/classifier-free guidance [9, 10] and CBF-shaped objectives [21], or through CBF-based safety filters embedded in the sampling pipeline [6, 15]. While guidance-based methods generally lack formal guarantees, safety filters provide them under their modeling assumptions: Hamilton– Jacobi reachability [22] scales exponentially with the state dimension and model predictive safety filters [23] solve a horizon-length problem at every step, whereas CBF filters [13, 14] need a single optimization per control cycle. However, trajectory-level CBF correction can introduce computa-tional overhead, local traps, and dynamical inconsistency. In contrast, SafeStreamingFlow ex-tends streaming flow to start–goal-conditioned state space planning and enforces safety constraints only at the executed step, aligning generation, execution, and safety filtering in physical time.

4.2 Hierarchical State Predictor (HSP) 

We now specify the parametric form of Fθ from (8). Exposing the full state (pt , vt ) to a single network admits a kinematic shortcut : for locally bounded acceleration, 

pt+∆t = pt + ∆t vt + O(∆t2), (14) so a predictor can drive training loss down by carrying over vt rather than learning goal-conditioned progress from c = ( xs, xg) (ablated in Appendices F and G). 5 Experiments 

We compare SafeStreamingFlow against safe generative planners in both nominal and safe variants: guidance-based (classifier guidance, CG [9]), diffusion-based (Diffuser/SafeDiffuser [3, 15]), and flow-matching-based (FM/FlowMatcher and SafeFM/SafeFlowMatcher [6]). We evaluate open- and closed-loop planning on four environments: Maze2D [25] (Section 5.1), F1TENTH [26] (Section 5.2), MuJoCo Hopper [25] (Section 5.3), and a ROS2/Gazebo warehouse navigation [27] (Section 5.4). Throughout, H denotes the discrete horizon length (i.e., the number of transitions) and K the num-ber of sampling steps a trajectory-level baseline used to generate a full trajectory. We note that SafeFlowMatcher uses K+1 because of its two-phase structure [6]; the specific values of H and 

K used per task are listed in each results table. In contrast, SafeStreamingFlow emits one state per sampling step, requiring H sequential forward passes irrespective of K. 6 Conclusion and Limitations 

We presented SafeStreamingFlow , a start–goal-conditioned planner that streams one-step state updates by integrating a learned vector field in physical time. Because sampling unfolds in phys-ical time, a constraint on the sampling dynamics is a constraint on the executed state under the system model, making safety enforcement dynamically consistent for a generative planner. A naive streaming predictor exploits a kinematic shortcut in second-order systems, which we resolve with hi-erarchical state prediction. This formulation yields three benefits: discrete-time HOCBFs reduce the number of optimization solves from O(HK ) to O(H), extend to closed-loop execution for free, and avoid local-trap artifacts. Across four environments (Maze2D, F1TENTH, Hopper, Warehouse Nav-igation), SafeStreamingFlow improves execution-time safety, smoothness, and latency over safe baselines while remaining competitive on goal-reaching. 

Limitations: Several limitations remain. First, because SafeStreamingFlow transports data to data rather than noise to data as in conventional flow matching, it fundamentally requires richer demonstration data; our contracting conditional flows alleviate this requirement but do not remove it. Second, by enforcing safety through CBFs, SafeStreamingFlow inherits their classical lim-itations: the quadratic barrier restricts the obstacle geometries handled efficiently, and valid barrier functions and their gains must be specified in advance. Third, as noted after Theorem 1, we do not establish recursive feasibility, and feasibility is lost even in a simple setting: on Maze2D Large, the filter occasionally finds no solution, and a few closed-loop rollouts violate the barrier. Finally, all evaluations are in simulation; real-robot deployment is needed to validate practicality and may expose further sim-to-real gaps. 9Acknowledgments

H Multimodality 

This section shows experimentally that SafeStreamingFlow produces multimodal plans by adding small noise to the initial position, as in StreamingFlow[7]. At test time, we sample 

p0 ∼ N (ps, σ 2test I) and keep the conditioning (xs, xg) unchanged; with σtest = 0, the plan is unique. Figure 6 shows 50 open-loop plans on Maze2D Large for one start–goal pair with σtest = 0.02, with-out the safety filter: the plans split into two routes that pass a wall block on opposite sides (43 and 7 plans). Noise at the start yields several plans, but this alone does not make the planner multimodal: if the direction of the perturbation decided the route, the plans would only carry the input noise. 19 We therefore test whether the route can be predicted from the perturbation, on the 19 groups of 50 plans that produce more than one route (two start–goal pairs, three training seeds, and 

σtest ∈ { 0.005 , 0.02 , 0.05 , 0.1}). In 15 of these groups, the route cannot be predicted from the direction of the perturbation. Plans that take different routes start together and split partway through the plan: the distance between them grows to 9 .3 times its initial value (median), against 2 .9 times for plans on the same route, and reaches half of its maximum at 36% of the horizon. The perturbation may bias the route, but the route stays unpredictable from its direction: the flow turns small differences in the start into a choice between routes, so the noise yields distinct routes rather than shifted copies of one plan.
