Abstract 

Probabilistic forecasting is important for predicting complex dynamical systems because intrinsic randomness and incomplete observations can cause the same observed state to evolve into multiple plausible futures. While flow matching is a flexible approach for probabilistic forecasting, it is computationally expensive. Streaming flow (SF) reformulates this approach to model temporal evolution efficiently by learning a continuous velocity field directly in physical time. However, SF learns a deterministic velocity field. Thus, it provides only a single future trajectory for a given fixed initial state and observation history. To overcome this limitation, we introduce Variational Streaming Flow (VSF). Our approach learns a latent distribution that is conditioned on the dynamics of interest. In turn, this enables probabilistic forecasting. Importantly, we retain the computational efficiency of SF by generating in physical time. Across deterministic and stochastic dynamical systems, VSF demonstrates superior predictive accuracy and distributional fidelity. We demonstrate the advantage for both long-horizon rollouts exceeding 1,000 steps, and settings with bifurcating dynamics. Moreover, VSF can be integrated into existing Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA)-based world models as a plug-and-play predictor to improve temporal dynamics and goal-directed success rate in navigation, motion planning, and manipulation.

Forecasting dynamical systems over long horizons remains challenging. Even when the dynamics are deterministic, small measurement or prediction errors can compound over a long rollout (Zhang & Gilpin, 2025). Predicting the entire future trajectory at once can avoid this recursive error accumulation, but this becomes an increasingly difficult learning task as the forecast horizon grows. Many real-world systems are also stochastic, which means that there can be many different future trajectories for the same sequence of observations (Pfister et al., 2026). For example, in weather forecasting (Feng & Fu, 2013; Sandu et al., 2019; Price et al., 2025), unresolved atmospheric processes can lead to different storm tracks from similar observations. In these settings, predicting just a single trajectory is insufficient for forecasting or planning. This motivates probabilistic forecasts that represent a distribution over possible futures (Chua et al., 2018; Porcher et al., 2026). Flow matching provides a flexible way to learn probabilistic forecasts over entire future trajecto-ries (Lipman et al., 2024; Kollovieh et al., 2025). However, generating a forecast requires integrating a learned vector field along an auxiliary flow time that is separate from the physical evolution of the system. Streaming and physical-time formulations instead evolve the predicted state directly through physical time, making long-horizon forecasting more efficient (Jiang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026). However, a standard non-latent physical-time model with a deterministic velocity field produces only one future from a fixed observed state. To address this limitation, we introduce Variational Streaming Flow (VSF), a probabilistic extension of physical-time flow forecasting. VSF augments the physical-time velocity field with a latent variable that is sampled once for each forecast and kept fixed throughout the rollout. This preserves the computational efficiency of streaming flow (SF) while improving long-horizon forecasting accuracy, as shown in Fig. 1. Different latent samples can also generate different future trajectories from the same observed state, allowing VSF to represent multiple possible futures. During training, we use an encoder to infer the latent variable from the observed future transition and regularize its distribution toward a fixed prior (Guo & Schwing, 2025; Eijkelboom et al., 2024). At inference, we only need to sample a latent from the prior and keep it fixed throughout the rollout. We evaluate VSF on deterministic and partially observed stochastic dynamical systems, including chaotic systems and settings with branching futures, testing both predictive accuracy and distributional fidelity. On deterministic systems, VSF improves long-horizon predictive accuracy over DeepAR (Salinas et al., 2020), diffusion- and flow-based forecasters, SF (Jiang et al., 2025), and Chronos-2 (Ansari et al., 2025). On stochastic Rössler and Lorenz systems, VSF more accurately recovers the conditional distribution of long-horizon futures. We further use VSF as the predictor in a JEPA-based world model (Maes et al., 2026); see Fig. 2. Across four robotics tasks, VSF improves average goal-directed planning success by 7.34% 

over LeWM’s deterministic predictor while retaining one velocity-field evaluation per imagined transition. Our main contributions are as follows. • We introduce Variational Streaming Flow , a variational latent variable formulation of physical-time flow modeling in which one latent draw conditions a full forecast rollout. It is motivated by a limitation of non-latent physical-time flows near bifurcations, where small differences in state can make the learned dynamics highly sensitive to perturbations (see App. A). • We develop a variational learning framework for latent-conditioned physical-time velocities (see Section 4). We derive a conditional-velocity ELBO and introduce an energy-based reconstruction over multiple latent samples. Under posterior–prior alignment, we show that the resulting reconstruction controls the energy distance between predicted and true velocity distributions. • We evaluate VSF on deterministic and stochastic forecasting and in an action-conditioned world model. # 2 Related Work 

Probabilistic and generative forecasting. Probabilistic time-series forecasting has traditionally modeled future uncertainty through autoregressive conditional distributions—using either deterministic states like DeepAR (Salinas et al., 2020) or stochastic latent variables like the Variational RNN (Chung et al., 2015). CLOVER (Olivares et al., 2015) further refines traditional parametric forecasting by reparameterizing the coherent learning objective to better optimize distribution matching. TimeGrad instead replaces these parametric one-step distributions with an autoregressive diffusion model (Rasul et al., 2021). More recently, pretrained time-series models have enabled forecasting across datasets without task-specific training. Lag-Llama provides a probabilistic decoder-only model (Rasul et al., 2023), while Chronos models scaled and quantized time-series values with language-model architectures (Ansari et al., 2024). Other foundation models instead use direct multi-horizon prediction, including TimesFM (Das et al., 2024), Moirai (Woo et al., 2024), and the multivariate and covariate-conditioned Chronos-2 (Ansari et al., 2025). Such models can also provide strong zero-shot forecasts for chaotic systems (Zhang & Gilpin, 2025), but they do not explicitly parameterize the underlying evolution as a physical-time dynamical system (Karaouli et al., 2025). A complementary line of work uses diffusion and flow matching to model conditional future distributions (Erichson et al., 2025). Rolling Diffusion adapts the diffusion process across forecast lead time (Ruhe et al., 2024), TSFlow introduces Gaussian-process priors for conditional flow matching (Kollovieh et al., 2025), and flow-based forecasting has been improved through informed probability paths (Lim et al., 2025), autoregressive conditional flows (ElGazzar & van Gerven, 2025), and Bayesian-filtering-inspired transport between successive predictive distributions (Huang et al., 2026). 

Streaming and physical-time flows. Another line of work removes the distinction between generative flow time and temporal evolution. Streaming Flow Policy treats an action trajectory itself as the flow trajectory, enabling actions to be generated and executed incrementally (Jiang et al., 2025). For sequential data, Lim et al. (2026) show that empirical flow matching can be interpreted as a memory-augmented continuous-time dynamical system whose velocity field is constructed from historical transitions. Most directly, ODEWorld introduces Physical-Time Flow, which learns a latent velocity field whose integration directly follows physical time (Liu et al., 2026). 

Stochastic world models and probabilistic planning. Predictive uncertainty has long played a role in model-based control (Watter et al., 2015; Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2025). PETS combines probabilistic dynamics ensembles with trajectory sampling (Chua et al., 2018), while PlaNet learns latent dynamics with both deterministic and stochastic components and plans through imagined trajectories (Hafner et al., 2019). Recent visual world models instead often predict directly in learned feature spaces. Positioning. Streaming Flow (SF) formulates sequence generation directly in physical time (Jiang et al., 2025), but its standard construction represents residual stochasticity through the evolving physical state; an auxiliary-latent extension is also possible. Variational flow matching provides a mechanism for representing multimodal velocity fields by inferring latent variables from observed target dynamics (Guo & Schwing, 2025; Eijkelboom et al., 2024). We bring these ideas together in Variational Streaming Flow (VSF) , a conditional latent variable formulation of physical-time flow modeling for probabilistic forecasting. During training, a variational encoder associates observed future dynamics with latent codes, while at inference a latent drawn from the prior conditions a complete physical-time rollout from the observed state. Thus, we view VSF not as the first latent SF model, but as a variational latent variable formulation of SF for probabilistic forecasting. Table 1 summarizes this positioning. This combination also raises a question specific to physical-time forecasting: why is it useful to represent future-specific information outside the evolving physical state? We illustrate one such mechanism through a simple bifurcation example in App. A.

This is appropriate when the latent variable fully separates the different future dynamics. In practice, however, different future continuations can still remain compatible with the same context and latent value. # 5 Experimental Results 

In this section, we evaluate VSF on fully observed deterministic systems (Section 5.1), partially observed stochastic systems (Section 5.2), and as a predictor for model-based planning (Section 5.3). Additional experimental details and ablations are provided in App. F–L. 

Baselines. For the dynamical-system forecasting experiments, we compare VSF with eight baselines: (1) Block diffusion, a conditional diffusion model that generates the forecast horizon jointly; (2) AR diffusion, an autoregressive diffusion model that generates one future state at a time; (3) Rectified flow, which autoregressively generates one future state at a time by integrating a rectified-flow velocity field (Liu et al., 2022); (4) DeepAR (Salinas et al., 2020), an autoregressive recurrent probabilistic forecaster; (5) SF (Jiang et al., 2025), a deterministic physical-time flow; (6) SF (perturbation), which introduces forecast diversity through Gaussian perturbations of the initial state; (7) Chronos-2 (Ansari et al., 2025), a pretrained universal time-series forecaster; and (8) Chronos-2 (calibrated), which applies post-hoc calibration to the Chronos-2 predictions. 

## 5.1 Deterministic Forecasting 

We evaluate fully observed deterministic forecasting on the double pendulum, Rössler, and Lorenz systems using 1,000-step rollouts. We report CRPS for the forecast ensemble and RMSE and MAE for its mean; detailed definitions are provided in App. F. 9VSF produces accurate forecasts with samples concentrated around the ground-truth trajectory. As shown in Table 2, VSF achieves the lowest CRPS, RMSE, and MAE across all three systems, while providing up to a 7× speedup over AR diffusion. The qualitative forecasts in the Appendix (Fig. 15) further show that its sampled trajectories remain close to the reference trajectory throughout the rollout. Chronos-2 is less accurate in these experiments. One possible factor is the short forecasting context of only 10 time steps. This is consistent with Zhang & Gilpin (2025), who use 512 context points in their experiments on chaotic systems and report improved zero-shot forecasting performance for Chronos as the context length increases.

## 5.2 Stochastic Forecasting 

We evaluate partially observed stochastic forecasting on the Rössler and Lorenz systems. For each initial state, we generate multiple trajectories by independently sampling an unobserved dynamical parameter. Each trajectory is deterministic once this parameter is fixed, but the model does not observe it. The same observed history can therefore lead to different future trajectories, including trajectories that separate into different dynamical regimes. We evaluate these forecast distributions using conditional mean error (CME), conditional standard-deviation error (CSE), Wasserstein distance ( W1), and energy distance (ED). For the values reported in our tables, W1 and ED treat each complete rollout as a single vector, so they compare distributions over whole trajectories and account for temporal structure. In the rollout-error plots, W1

and ED are instead computed pointwise in time. Detailed metric definitions are provided in App. F. 

VSF best matches the conditional distribution of future trajectories. As shown in Table 2, VSF achieves the lowest value for every metric on both stochastic systems. The trajectory ensembles in Figures 4 and 16 show that VSF captures both the spread of the reference futures and the geometry of the underlying dynamics. Block diffusion and DeepAR cover a narrower part of the distribution, while AR diffusion produces diverse futures but is more prone to rollout drift. Perturbing the initial state of SF introduces some diversity, but this inference-time perturbation is not learned by its training objective. Unperturbed SF produces only a single trajectory, yielding a CSE of 1.

## 5.3 World Model Predictor 

We next evaluate VSF as an action-conditioned predictor in a JEPA-based world model. The goal is to test whether its physical-time forecasting formulation can improve prediction and planning in a learned representation space. We freeze LeWM’s pretrained JEPA representation (Assran et al., 2023; Maes et al., 2026) and train only the predictor on offline observation–action trajectories, following the action-conditioned training setup of V-JEPA 2 (Assran et al., 2025). Future latent states are used as teacher-forced training targets. We compare LeWM’s AR predictor with SF and VSF predictors while keeping the JEPA encoder, CEM planner, and planning budget fixed. We report latent-trajectory RMSE along the action sequences selected by CEM and planning success on PushT, Cube, TwoRoom, and Reacher. The learned JEPA representation also evolves smoothly over time, as illustrated in Fig. 6, making physical-time prediction a natural fit for this latent space. Details of the action-conditioned VSF extension are provided in App. E. 

VSF improves both latent prediction and planning success. As shown in Table 4, VSF reduces latent-trajectory RMSE relative to the AR predictor on all four tasks and achieves the lowest RMSE on PushT, TwoRoom, and Reacher. It also achieves the highest planning success on every task, improving average success by 7.34% over the AR predictor. The successful Cube rollout in Fig. 6 provides a qualitative example, with the predicted latent trajectory closely following the ground truth. VSF retains LeWM’s budget of one velocity-field evaluation per imagined transition. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We introduced VSF, a probabilistic extension of SF for forecasting directly in physical time, where one latent variable conditions an entire rollout. This allows VSF to model different possible futures while preserving the direct physical-time generation of SF. We train the latent-conditioned velocity field with variational inference and an energy-based reconstruction over multiple latent samples. We 11 also provide a population-level guarantee connecting this objective to how well the model captures the distribution of possible local dynamics under prior sampling. Empirically, VSF improves long-horizon forecasting on deterministic and partially observed stochastic systems, including the quality of the predicted future distribution, and improves planning success as an action-conditioned world-model predictor, while retaining one velocity-field evaluation per imagined transition. 

Limitations and future work. VSF relies on adjacent states providing a meaningful local direction of motion for learning the physical-time velocity. This assumption is most reliable when the dynamics vary smoothly in physical time. Abrupt impacts, discontinuities, or strong observation noise can make the local velocity poorly resolved. VSF also uses explicit forward Euler integration, which can accumulate discretization error for coarse step sizes or rapidly varying learned fields (Hairer et al., 1993); solver choice can also affect the effective dynamics learned by neural ODEs (Zhu et al., 2022). Future work could therefore explore hybrid or jump-aware latent dynamics (Jia & Benson, 2019), controlled or rough differential equations (Kidger et al., 2020; Morrill et al., 2021), and higher-order or adaptive Runge–Kutta solvers (Queiruga et al., 2020).

This motivates VSF. The proposition and discussions above identify a bottleneck of standard non-latent SF, and the idealized latent construction shows how that bottleneck can in principle be removed by carrying future-specific information outside the physical state. This observation motivates VSF. Rather than requiring the physical state alone to carry all residual uncertainty needed for later bifurcation, VSF conditions the physical-time velocity field on an additional latent variable sampled once per forecast. The variational framework of VSF provides a principled way to associate observed future dynamics with learned latent codes during training, while the learned prior provides the corresponding randomness at inference. The goal is therefore to learn a distribution of coherent physical-time dynamics in which distinct future con-tinuations can be represented without requiring increasingly sensitive separation in the physical state itself. 20 B A variational objective for clean streaming trajectories

## G.3 World-Model Datasets 

We use the four offline visual-control datasets in LeWM (Maes et al., 2026). Trajectories contain 

224 × 224 RGB images, continuous actions, and auxiliary simulator state. Following LeWM, we sample images every five environment steps and form four-frame clips with a five-action block per transition. The predictor uses only frozen LeWM image embeddings and actions. Auxiliary state is used only to restore evaluation starts and goals and compute success. 

PushT. In this continuous 2D task, a blue point agent pushes a T-shaped block to a demonstrated future configuration (Chi et al., 2025). Block interactions are limited to pushing. The goal specifies both pusher and block poses. Following LeWM, we reuse noisy DINO-WM expert replay (Zhou et al., 2025) comprising 20,000 episodes averaging 196 steps. 

Cube. OGBench-Cube is the single-object variant of the OGBench manipulation benchmark (Park et al., 2025). A UR5e arm with a parallel-jaw gripper is controlled through end-effector displacement, yaw, and gripper commands to pick up a cube and place it at a target location. The dataset contains 10,000 200-step episodes collected with the benchmark’s scripted collection heuristic. Success depends on the cube center reaching its target, regardless of cube orientation. 

TwoRoom. A red point agent starts at a random position in one room and navigates to a sampled target in the other through the single doorway (Sobal et al., 2025). The dataset contains 10,000 episodes averaging 92 steps, generated by a noisy doorway-then-target heuristic. 

Reacher. Reacher is based on the DeepMind Control Suite planar two-link arm (Tassa et al., 2018). We use the DINO-WM/LeWM qpos-match variant, in which the goal is a complete future joint configuration. The dataset contains 10,000 200-step episodes collected with a Soft Actor–Critic policy. 31 H Experimental Setup 

## H.1 Forecasting 

Baseline models. We compare VSF with eight baselines: Block diffusion, which jointly denoises a future block; AR diffusion, which denoises one state at a time; Rectified flow (Liu et al., 2022), which denoises one state at a time; DeepAR (Salinas et al., 2020); SF (Jiang et al., 2025); SF (perturbation), which perturbs the normalized initial state at inference; frozen Chronos-2 (Ansari et al., 2025); and Chronos-2 (calibrated), our post-hoc affine calibration of its predictive quantiles. 

Method-specific settings. 

• Block diffusion. A conditional one-dimensional U-Net jointly denoises a length-20 target window comprising the anchor and 19 future states. We commit 5 predicted states as a block before reconditioning. A blockwise autoregressive strategy is also used in video generation (Voleti et al., 2022; Ho et al., 2022). We use 100 training noise levels, x0 prediction, and DDIM (Song et al., 2020) with 20 denoising steps. • AR diffusion. Following TimeGrad’s autoregressive forecasting formulation (Rasul et al., 2021), we denoise one future state at a time. Our implementation uses a four-layer, four-head causal Transformer (Peebles & Xie, 2023) of width 128 with diffusion v-prediction. We use 100 training noise levels and DDIM with 20 denoising steps per predicted state. • Rectified flow (Liu et al., 2022) uses a four-layer, four-head transformer with width 128, as in AR diffusion. It autoregressively generates one future state at a time using velocity prediction and 10 denoising steps per predicted state. • DeepAR (Salinas et al., 2020) is an autoregressive probabilistic forecasting model based on recurrent neural networks. We use a two-layer LSTM with hidden width 128 and a diagonal Gaussian output distribution with minimum scale 10 −4.• SF and SF (perturbation). SF (Jiang et al., 2025) uses the thin-tube path with σ0 = 0 .4 and 

k = 10 and uses a U-Net backbone following the original paper and similar to block diffusion, and advances ten states before reconditioning, except on stochastic Lorenz where it advances one state. The perturbation variant modifies the normalized initial state once, at the start of the rollout. We select the standard deviation of the perturbation noise between 0.1 and 0.01 depending on the performance. • Chronos-2. Chronos-2 is a pretrained time-series foundation model that directly returns predictive quantiles over a requested forecast horizon (Ansari et al., 2025). We keep its parameters frozen and use its full-horizon quantile forecasts. Chronos-2 (calibrated) is our post-hoc variant, not part of the standard Chronos-2 method. • VSF. VSF uses the same conditional one-dimensional U-Net as SF and adds a four-dimensional trajectory-level latent at the network bottleneck. A two-layer MLP maps this latent to the bottleneck feature width. After the middle residual blocks and before upsampling, we fuse the latent feature with the time- and history-conditioned bottleneck activation using a weighted sum. We fix the latent fusion using 90% latent features and 10% bottleneck features, while all skip connections and the original SF conditioning remain unchanged. We also fix the α = 1 .999 and 

βKL = 2 × 10 −3 for every forecasting dataset. We follow SF’s tube-based training construction for double pendulum and Rössler. We observe Lorenz dynamics are sensitive to injected state noise; hence we disable tube perturbations.

## H.2 World Models 

Baseline models. We train the SF and VSF predictors using the JEPA-based LeWM representation from the corresponding task-specific pretrained checkpoint (Maes et al., 2026). We freeze LeWM’s visual encoder, projection head, and action encoder, and compare against its pretrained autoregressive (AR) predictor. All three predictors operate in the same 192-dimensional latent space and use the same CEM planner, evaluation tasks, and planning budget. 

Method-specific settings. 

• AR predictor. We use the pretrained LeWM predictor, which autoregressively predicts the next JEPA latent conditioned on the action sequence. • SF predictor. We replace the AR predictor with a deterministic physical-time velocity model and use one velocity-field evaluation for each imagined transition. • VSF predictor. We condition the physical-time velocity on a prior latent sampled once and held fixed throughout each imagined rollout. This also requires one velocity-field evaluation per transition. 

Experimental details. LeWM encodes each 224 × 224 RGB frame with a ViT-Tiny (Dosovitskiy, 2020) backbone using 14 ×14 patches, 12 Transformer layers, three attention heads, and hidden dimension 192. Its final-layer [CLS] token is passed through LeWM’s BatchNorm MLP projector to obtain the 192-dimensional JEPA state used by every predictor. Each modeled transition spans five environment steps. We use a 90%/10% training/validation split. SF and VSF are trained with AdamW using learning rate 10 −4, zero weight decay, batch size 4,096, and exponential-moving-average decay 0.75 . We train VSF using Eq. (E.1) as the loss for 60 epochs on Push-T and Cube and 90 epochs on Two-Room and Reacher, and report results over three seeds. For planning, CEM maintains a Gaussian distribution over every action sequence. Each of 30 CEM updates evaluates 300 candidates and fits the mean and diagonal scale to the best 30, starting from unit scale. Following LeWM, we evaluate 50 goals per run, sample each goal 25 environment steps ahead, and allow a 50-step execution budget. Predictor RMSE is measured on CEM-selected action sequences. Latent-source and prior-alignment variants. Figure 12 separates three effects that are otherwise coupled in VSF: stochastic latent conditioning, future-conditioned variational training, and alignment of the training posterior with the inference prior. Here p(z) = N (0, Idz ), and qϕ := qϕ(z | us, xs, s, h)

is the future-conditioned training posterior. Table 6 defines the four variants explicitly. SF is the non-latent, MSE-trained reference. The three latent-conditioned variants use the same decoder,

Prior-latent SF is the direct non-variational control: it uses prior samples during both training and forecasting, with neither a posterior encoder nor a KL loss. In contrast, Posterior VSF (no KL) remains variational because it uses the future-conditioned posterior during training. Its prior samples at inference are therefore unaligned with the training latents, creating a train–test mismatch. 

Does latent conditioning help? Prior-latent SF versus SF measures the benefit of the latent-conditioned, multi-sample predictor without a learned posterior. Its improvement across all metrics shows that latent-conditioned prediction is already useful without variational training. 

Does variational training help? Full VSF has lower error than Prior-latent SF on all five metrics in Figure 12. Prior-latent SF samples training latents independently of the motion it must predict, making it harder to associate latent values with different possible futures. Full VSF instead infers latents from the observed local motion during training, helping different latent values represent different continuations. This is useful near a bifurcation, where nearly identical physical states can lead to different outcomes. 

Why prior alignment matters. Full VSF versus Posterior no-KL isolates the role of the KL term when training uses a future-conditioned posterior. Removing alignment degrades every metric, especially the Rössler distributional scores. This is consistent with train–test mismatch. Without KL regularization, posterior codes may occupy regions or relative masses that are not reproduced by the prior, so prior-sampled rollouts can miss or misweight learned continuations.
