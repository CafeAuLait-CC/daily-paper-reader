# ABSTRACT 

Flow-map distillation enables one- and few-step generation by learning finite-time transitions of a pretrained generative ODE. We investigate whether changing the teacher’s time parameterization can make these transitions easier to learn. Mo-tivated by the hypothesis that trajectory segments with large normal acceleration are harder to distill, we propose a geometry-aware time reparameterization that allocates more student time to these regions while preserving the teacher’s geo-metric paths and terminal distribution. We derive a shared clock that equalizes a population normal-acceleration statistic under suitable assumptions, and construct a practical approximation from robust, regularized estimates across teacher trajec-tories. We incorporate this clock into Lagrangian flow-map distillation, using the transformed time coordinate to condition the student. The clock is estimated once before distillation and requires neither teacher retraining nor additional student parameters or inference-time network evaluations. Experiments on synthetic data, CIFAR-10, and CelebA-64 show improved sample quality over identity-time dis-tillation at matched inference budgets, including improvements in one- and two-step image generation. The gains in one-step generation, where no intermediate sampling times can be adjusted, highlight the benefits of time reparameterization during distillation. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Diffusion and score-based generative models have emerged as a leading paradigm for learning com-plex data distributions (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021b), enabling high-fidelity image synthesis and flexible conditioning at scale (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Rombach et al., 2022). These models learn to reverse a gradual noising process, with generation described by reverse-time stochastic dynamics or an associated probability-flow ordinary differential equation (ODE). Their success, however, comes with substantial inference cost: producing a sample typ-ically requires many sequential neural-network evaluations. Reducing this cost while preserving sample quality remains a central challenge, motivating extensive work on deterministic sampling and specialized numerical solvers (Song et al., 2021a; Lu et al., 2022). Flow matching (FM) (Lipman et al., 2023) provides a flexible framework for learning continuous-time transport through a simulation-free regression objective. Together with closely related rectified-flow and stochastic-interpolant formulations (Liu et al., 2023; Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden, 2023), it learns a time-dependent velocity field that transports a simple source distribution to the data distri-bution, using analytically accessible conditional paths to construct training targets. This viewpoint accommodates diffusion probability paths as well as simpler alternatives, such as linear interpola-tion between noise and data, offering greater flexibility in the design of the generative dynamics. Importantly, however, straight conditional interpolation paths do not generally imply straight trajec-tories of the resulting marginal velocity field (Frans et al., 2025). Sampling therefore still requires numerical ODE integration, and using too few steps can introduce substantial discretization error. One- and few-step generative models address this limitation by learning finite-time transport directly, either through distillation from a pretrained model (Salimans & Ho, 2022) or through direct training from data. Consistency models (Song et al., 2023) learn to map points along a probability-flow 1arXiv:2610.02427v1 [cs.LG] 1 Oct 2026 trajectory to a common endpoint, while consistency trajectory models (Kim et al., 2024) and flow map matching (FMM) (Boffi et al., 2025b) learn two-time maps connecting intermediate states along the dynamics. Related formulations include Shortcut Models (Frans et al., 2025), which learn step-size-conditioned updates by enforcing consistency between one large step and two smaller steps, and MeanFlow (Geng et al., 2025), which learns average velocities through a differential identity relating them to instantaneous velocities. By learning finite-time updates, these approaches replace many evaluations of an instantaneous velocity field with one or a few evaluations of a learned map. Their effectiveness consequently depends on how accurately this map can be learned. We investigate whether changing a pretrained teacher’s time parameterization can make its flow map easier to learn. Motivated by the hypothesis that segments with large normal acceleration are harder to distill, we construct a shared clock that allocates more student time to these regions while preserv-ing the teacher’s geometric paths and terminal distribution. The clock is estimated once from robust, regularized normal-acceleration statistics. We then train the student against the reparameterized ve-locity ebτ (x) = ϕ′(τ )bϕ(τ )(x) using Lagrangian flow-map distillation. This requires neither teacher retraining nor changes to the student architecture or additional inference-time network evaluations. Our main contributions are: • A normal-acceleration-based clock. We derive a shared time transformation that equal-izes a population normal-acceleration statistic under suitable assumptions, and estimate it robustly from teacher trajectories. • Geometry-aware flow-map distillation. We incorporate this time coordinate into La-grangian flow-map distillation, training a single student directly against the reparameterized teacher dynamics. The time map is estimated once before distillation and requires neither teacher retraining nor changes to the student architecture, with no additional inference-time network evaluations. • Improved few-step generation. Through synthetic and image-generation experiments, we demonstrate improved sample quality over identity-time distillation at matched inference budgets. In particular, we observe meaningful improvements to one-step generation, where no intermediate sampling times can be adjusted, highlighting the role of time reparameter-ization in the distillation problem itself. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Flow-map learning. Progressive distillation (Salimans & Ho, 2022) compresses successive teacher updates, while consistency models and consistency trajectory models learn endpoint pre-dictions or transitions between intermediate noise levels (Song et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024). Flow map matching (Boffi et al., 2025b) provides a mathematical framework for these constructions, with objectives for both teacher distillation and direct training. Subsequent work develops self-distillation formulations (Boffi et al., 2025a) and practical continuous-time distillation objectives (Sabour et al., 2025). Shortcut Models and MeanFlow offer complementary formulations based on compositional consistency and average velocities (Frans et al., 2025; Geng et al., 2025). Recent extensions include parallel prediction of interval velocities (Shaul et al., 2026) and distribution-based distillation using average-velocity information (Zhao et al., 2026). Our work builds on Lagrangian flow-map distilla-tion and investigates the time coordinate in which the teacher’s finite-time transport is learned. 

Trajectory geometry and coupling. Several approaches improve generative dynamics by simpli-fying their trajectories. Rectified flow and reflow encourage straighter transport (Liu et al., 2023), while minibatch optimal transport modifies source–target couplings to obtain simpler dynamics (Tong et al., 2024). Lee et al. (2023) learn couplings that reduce trajectory curvature and also investigate the benefits of straighter teachers for distillation. Isokinetic Flow Matching introduces a lookahead velocity-consistency regularizer motivated by reducing material acceleration (Khan, 2026). These methods modify the learned velocity field or its training coupling. Our transformation instead retains the geometric trajectories of a pretrained teacher and adjusts their traversal speed before flow-map distillation. 

Sampling schedules and trajectory diagnostics. Sampling efficiency also depends on the place-ment of numerical integration steps. Align Your Steps optimizes schedules through a stochastic-calculus-based discrepancy criterion (Sabour et al., 2024), while Geometry-Inspired Time Schedul-2ing uses trajectory information and dynamic programming to select integration times (Chen et al., 2024). Entropic Time Schedulers allocate sampling points according to conditional-entropy progress (Stanˇ cevi´ c et al., 2025). Particularly close to our construction, Sharpen Your Flow estimates the full material-acceleration magnitude of a pretrained flow and derives a square-root sampling density implemented through inverse-CDF scheduling (Gupta et al., 2026). Our method uses the normal component of acceleration and applies the resulting clock to the teacher dynamics during student training. This distinction is particularly relevant to one-step flow-map generation, where no inter-mediate inference times can be adjusted. 

Adaptive allocation of training effort. Temporal difficulty has also motivated adaptive training procedures. Curvature-Adaptive Consistency Flow Matching uses consistency-error feedback and reinforcement learning to allocate training across temporal stages (Tian et al., 2026). Its boundary-focused allocation provides qualitative evidence of nonuniform distillation difficulty, although its diagnostic differs from normal acceleration. Complexity-Balanced Diffusion Splitting uses accel-eration or spatial regularity estimates to partition time among specialized networks (Issachar et al., 2026). Our approach trains a single student using a fixed time transformation estimated from the pretrained teacher. 

Time reparameterization and learnability. Time parameterization can affect learning as well as numerical integration. Tsimpos et al. (2025) optimize the schedule of a fixed transport interpolation to control the spatial Lipschitz constant of its velocity field. Chen et al. (2025) develop Lipschitz-guided interpolation schedules and transfer formulas for applying them to pretrained models. Other approaches prescribe conditional velocity profiles (Bondar, 2026) or construct an inverse-CDF inter-polation schedule from regression losses measured during a pilot training run (Tania & Khan, 2026). Time transformations also appear in Flow Map Language Models, where a decoding-error-based clock guides temporal sampling and flow-map learning (Lee et al., 2026). These works establish the relevance of temporal coordinates to generative learning. We investigate a shared clock derived from the normal acceleration of a frozen teacher, and train the student directly against the reparameterized teacher velocity in the Lagrangian distillation objective.

Practical construction. We estimate the time map once before distillation using M teacher tra-jectories evaluated on a fine grid tk = k/N , k = 0 , . . . , N , with N ≥ 100 . We estimate material acceleration by finite differences of the teacher velocities along each trajectory, then project onto the normal direction to obtain nk,m . At each time, we discard the largest 5% of these values before computing the hardness profile. This limits the influence of rare sharp turns, which can produce par-ticularly large normal accelerations (phenomenon which we mainly observed in 2d experiments). We integrate the resulting clock density numerically and fit a monotonicity-preserving PCHIP in-terpolant to the inverse pairs (τk, t k) (Fritsch & Butland, 1984). The resulting C1 map has an analytically evaluable derivative. A positive floor ξ ensures a strictly increasing cumulative clock; in our experiments, the estimated hardness already remained bounded away from zero. # 5 EXPERIMENTS 

We first examine the temporal distribution of normal acceleration and the effect of our clock on teacher sampling. We then evaluate whether training in the resulting time coordinate improves flow-map distillation on synthetic and image datasets. 5.1 TEMPORAL GEOMETRY AND TEACHER SAMPLING 

Normal-acceleration profiles. We evaluate normal-acceleration profiles along trajectories of the CIFAR-10 and CelebA64 teachers. Both exhibit a similar U-shaped temporal profile, with larger normal acceleration near the endpoints and smaller values in the middle. Figure 2 shows CelebA64 as a representative example; the CIFAR-10 profiles exhibit the same qualitative behavior, with dif-ferent magnitudes. This concentration motivates allocating more student time to the boundary re-gions. It is also qualitatively consistent with the U-shaped distillation difficulty reported by Tian et al. (2026), who learn an adaptive time-sampling curriculum using reinforcement learning. This agreement motivates our diagnostic, although it does not establish a direct correspondence between normal acceleration and distillation error. Figure 2 illustrates the resulting transformation for CelebA64. The estimated clock makes the normal-acceleration profile more uniform across time. Meanwhile, the original teacher’s velocity norm is approximately constant over most of the interval, whereas the warped velocity exhibits 7greater variation. This observation motivates the physical-time displacement scaling used for image distillation.

We proposed a geometry-aware time reparameterization that redistributes normal acceleration across time to improve flow-map distillation. The clock is estimated once, preserves teacher trajectories, and requires neither teacher retraining nor additional inference-time network evaluations. Experi-ments show improved few-step generation, in particular one-step gains that highlight the role of time coordinates in learning the map itself. Establishing a theoretical link between normal acceleration and distillation difficulty remains a direction for future work.
