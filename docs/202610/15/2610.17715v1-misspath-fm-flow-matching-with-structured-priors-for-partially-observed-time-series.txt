## Abstract 

Flow matching has emerged as a powerful alternative to diffusion models for generative modeling, but standard conditional flow matching is fundamentally mis-aligned with partially observed time series: starting from an uninformed Gaussian source forces the model to transport even already-observed entries from noise to the target, wasting capacity on trivially known regions. We propose MissPath-FM , a partial-observation-aware flow matching framework that redesigns the source prior and probability path for incomplete time series. MissPath-FM employs structured source priors (interpolation priors and Gaussian process posterior priors) to initial-ize the flow close to the observed signal, paired with a missingness-aware smooth path that allocates transport asymmetrically between observed and missing regions. We evaluate systematically across four datasets, four missingness mechanisms (MCAR, block, MNAR, irregular), two missing ratios, and three sequence lengths (T ∈ { 24 , 48 , 96 }), totaling 48 settings against eight baselines. MissPath-FM wins 29 of 32 settings at T =48 and 45 of 48 overall (93.8%). Our ablations re-veal a regime-dependent design hierarchy in which no single component suffices: under random missingness the source prior yields a large initial gain, but under structured block missingness it can fail entirely and the learned flow becomes indispensable; the missingness-aware path contributes a further 12% –26% across all regimes. Mechanistically, structured priors shorten observed-region transport by up to 15 ×, concentrating the velocity field on genuinely uncertain entries. ## 1 Introduction 

Real-world time series are rarely fully observed. In healthcare, laboratory tests are sparse and asynchronous; in energy systems, sensors fail or report irregularly; missing values often occur in structured blocks rather than isolated dropouts. Models must therefore operate under partial observability , making predictions from incomplete and irregular evidence [3, 19]. Prior work addresses missingness through deterministic imputation, recurrent architectures, and proba-bilistic generative models. Diffusion-based methods [23, 1, 4] and transport-based approaches [5, 18] have further advanced generative imputation. While effective, these methods typically treat missing-ness as an input-side conditioning mechanism, overlooking how the geometry of generative transport 

interacts with partial observability. Flow matching has emerged as a powerful alternative to diffusion models [13, 16], learning a continuous velocity field that transports a source distribution to the target via ODE integration [25]. Recent works apply flow matching to temporal data: TSFlow [12] integrates GP priors for forecasting, and TimeBridge [17] explores data-dependent priors for synthesis. Despite this progress, none of these methods explicitly addresses the interaction between the flow-matching transport and partial

We argue that the central bottleneck in applying flow matching to incomplete time series lies not in the velocity network architecture, but in the geometry of transport under partial observability .Two questions naturally arise: (1) what source distribution should the transport originate from when part of the sequence is already known? and (2) how should the probability path allocate transport effort between observed and missing regions? Addressing these questions yields a simple design principle: the source prior should respect the observed signal, and the transport path should concentrate generative effort on the uncertain entries. Motivated by this perspective, we propose MissPath-FM (Miss ingness-aware Path design for 

Flow Matching), a partial-observation-aware flow matching framework for incomplete time series. MissPath-FM combines structured source priors (from interpolation priors to Gaussian-process posterior priors) with a missingness-aware smooth path and context conditioning based on missingness masks and time gaps. Instead of regenerating the entire sequence from pure noise, MissPath-FM learns a velocity field that transports uncertainty from observed evidence into the missing regions. Empirically, our evaluation across 48 settings reveals that no single design choice is universally sufficient. Under random missingness (MCAR), the source prior yields a large initial gain. Under structured block missingness, however, the prior alone can degrade performance when contiguous gaps leave no flanking anchors; here the learned flow becomes indispensable. The missingness-aware path provides a further 12% –26% improvement across both regimes. Only the full pipeline— prior, flow, and path acting together—achieves robust performance across all missingness types. Mechanistically, structured priors shorten transport on observed regions by up to 15 ×, reallocating capacity toward genuinely uncertain entries. Our primary contributions are: • We introduce MissPath-FM , a flow matching framework for partial observability that combines structured source priors, mask/gap conditioning, and missingness-aware probability paths. • We establish a regime-dependent design hierarchy in which no single component suffices: the source prior yields a large initial gain under random missingness but can fail under block patterns; the learned flow is indispensable precisely where the prior struggles; and the missingness-aware path contributes a consistent 12% –26% across all regimes. Only their combination achieves robust performance. • We provide a transport-geometric explanation , showing how structured priors shorten observed-region trajectories and reallocate the velocity field’s capacity to genuinely uncertain entries. • We demonstrate state-of-the-art performance across 32 settings at T =48 (4 datasets, 4 missing-ness mechanisms, 2 ratios), winning 29 against eight baselines (45/48 including T ∈ { 24 , 96 }). 

## 2 Related Work 

Time-series modeling under missingness and irregular sampling. A large body of literature studies multivariate time series with missing values and irregular observations. Early deep learning approaches such as GRU-D and BRITS incorporate missingness masks and time intervals into recurrent architectures, showing that missingness patterns themselves carry informative signals [3, 2]. Beyond recurrence, mTAN introduces learned continuous-time embeddings with multi-head temporal attention for irregularly sampled data [20], Neural CDE treats the time series as a control path driving latent dynamics [11], and SAITS replaces recurrence with masked self-attention for efficient deterministic imputation [7]. While effective, these methods target point estimation or classification and do not model the full conditional distribution over missing entries. MissPath-FM addresses this limitation by formulating partial observation as a conditional transport problem, where the source prior, context encoding, and probability path are jointly designed around the missingness structure. 

Probabilistic and generative models for incomplete time series. Another line of work tackles missingness through probabilistic and generative modeling. GP-VAE enforces a Gaussian-process prior in the latent space for smooth temporal structure [9], while Latent ODEs provide continuous-time latent dynamics that naturally accommodate non-uniform observations [19]. SSSD replaces the transformer with a structured state-space model, excelling in blackout-missing sce-narios [1]; and PriSTI incorporates spatiotemporal dependencies as conditional priors [15]. Beyond standard diffusion, CSBI applies the Schrödinger bridge to time-series imputation via entropy-regularized optimal transport [4], and NewImp reinterprets diffusion-based imputation from a Wasser-stein gradient flow perspective [5]. Despite strong empirical results, all these methods rely on predefined noise-corruption schedules or iterative bridge procedures, treating missingness primarily as an input-side conditioning signal. In contrast, MissPath-FM learns a continuous transport map whose source distribution is dynamically adapted to observed evidence, bypassing iterative denoising entirely. 

Flow matching and transport-based generative modeling. Flow matching provides a simulation-free framework for training continuous normalizing flows by regressing velocity fields along pre-defined probability paths [13]. Rectified-flow formulations [16] and OT-CFM with minibatch cou-plings [25] further straighten trajectories for efficient few-step inference. These advances have catalyzed growing interest in transport-based models for temporal data: TFM extends flow matching to irregular clinical time series [29]; FM-TS adapts rectified flows for generation [10]; TSFlow integrates GP priors for probabilistic forecasting [12]; CLWF uses Lagrangian OT flows for im-putation [18]; PSW-I applies optimal transport with posterior sampling for imputation [26]; and TimeBridge explores data-dependent priors within a diffusion bridge [17]. Three concurrent works are especially relevant. CFMI [22] applies conditional flow matching for general-purpose imputation but retains a Gaussian source without structured priors. CGFM [27] shares our motivation of informed priors by using auxiliary predictions as the source, but targets fully observed forecasting without mask conditioning. Impute-MACFM [14] introduces a mask-aware CFM objective for tabular data that penalizes velocity on observed entries, addressing partial observability through loss design rather than prior/path shaping. MissPath-FM is complementary: rather than relying on loss design, we reshape the source distribution so the velocity field naturally concentrates on missing entries, and pair it with a missingness-aware path that allocates transport asymmetrically. 33 Method 

We present MissPath-FM, a generative framework for incomplete time series that reshapes the flow matching transport around partial observability (Figure 1). The method has three core components, all used in every reported result: (i) an observation-aware source prior (Section 3.2), (ii) a missingness-aware probability path (Section 3.3), and (iii) a context-conditioned velocity network (Section 3.4). Two further elements are not core: the GP posterior prior is an optional alternative to the default interpolation prior in (i) and appears only in ablations and analyses, and RandomRate is a training protocol rather than a part of the architecture (Section 4). 3.2 Observation-Aware Source Priors 

We replace the uninformed Gaussian z0 ∼ N (0 , I ) with source distributions z0 ∼ p0(· | c) condi-tioned on the observation context, guided by two principles: (i) consistency —observed entries in 

z0 should be close to their known values, driving Cobs → 0; and (ii) temporal coherence —missing entries should be initialized with temporally plausible values rather than independent noise, reducing 

Cmiss and easing the learning burden on the velocity network. We consider two instantiations of 

p0(· | c), used alternatively rather than in sequence. 

Option A: interpolation prior (default). For each variable d, we apply piecewise linear interpolation along the time axis: observed entries retain their values; missing entries are interpolated between flanking observations (with forward/backward fill at boundaries). Unlike the prior, the MA path leaves Cobs and Cmiss essentially unchanged; what it shortens is the part of the trajectory during which the network must track observed entries, so its benefit shows up in accuracy rather than in trajectory length (Appendix I.1).

5.4 Transport Geometry Analysis 

Figure 4 and Table 6 directly quantify the transport reallocation on Synthetic Block 50%. Under the Gaussian prior, all entries travel long distances from noise to target; under the interpolation prior, observed entries stay near targets ( ∼15 × shorter transport). The miss-to-obs ratio increases from 

1.1× to 19 .2×, confirming that the velocity field concentrates on genuinely uncertain entries. The GP prior additionally shortens missing-region transport by 39% , explaining its advantage under block and MNAR settings. Figure 5 visualizes this progression at the entry level: from uniform velocity under the Gaussian prior (a), to concentrated missing-entry velocity under interpolation (b), to further reduced velocity under the GP prior (c). The same effect is visible in the final samples: measured 

before the deterministic restoration step, observed-entry MAE is 0.023 –0.025 for MissPath-FM against 0.40 –0.77 for a Gaussian-source flow (Appendix K.2), so observed values are held in place by the transport itself rather than by the final projection. This perspective explains why many alternatives fail: once the prior removes the dominant inefficiency, regularizers, cost-aware paths, and larger backbones have little residual room to contribute (Appendix L, H). 

## 6 Conclusion 

We presented MissPath-FM , a flow matching framework that treats partial observability as a transport design problem rather than an input-side conditioning mechanism. By combining structured source priors, a missingness-aware probability path, and context-conditioned velocity estimation, MissPath-FM reshapes the generative transport to concentrate on genuinely uncertain entries. MissPath-FM wins 45 of 48 evaluation settings (93.8%) across four datasets, four missingness mechanisms, and three sequence lengths against eight baselines. 

Limitations and future work. Our largest tested setting is D=36 and T =192 ; behaviour at larger scale is not established. The advantage shrinks when observations are very sparse or unreliable: against SAITS, MissPath-FM falls behind on two of four rate-sweep settings at 90% missingness, and under observation noise beyond about 0.2 (ETTm1) to 0.3 (Electricity) z-scored units (Appendix K). The GP prior is per-variable and ignores cross-variable dependencies; a multi-output GP could exploit them, but its cost grows cubically in D, already the bottleneck at D=36 . Downstream decision tasks and continuous-time irregular settings remain open. ## C Baselines and Evaluation Metrics 

C.1 Baselines 

We compare against eight external methods spanning deterministic, generative, and transport-based families. For each baseline we specify the reproduction method: PyPOTS denotes evaluation via the PyPOTS library [6], Official denotes the authors’ released codebase, and Re-impl. denotes our faithful re-implementation following the published algorithm. • SAITS [7] introduces a joint-optimization training strategy with a diagonal-masked self-attention mechanism for time-series imputation. It learns to reconstruct missing values by attending to observed entries across all time steps simultaneously, producing deterministic point estimates. Evaluated via PyPOTS for both imputation and forecasting (impute-then-forecast pipeline with a shared downstream LSTM forecaster). • BRITS [2] is a bidirectional recurrent model that incorporates missingness masks and time-gap information directly into the hidden-state dynamics. Forward and backward RNNs produce complementary estimates that are combined via a consistency loss. Evaluated via PyPOTS for imputation and forecasting. • CSDI [23] applies conditional score-based diffusion with a Transformer backbone. It conditions on observed entries and iteratively denoises missing positions, producing calibrated uncertainty es-timates via multiple samples. Evaluated via PyPOTS for imputation, forecasting, and unconditional generation. • GP-VAE [9] is a variational autoencoder that enforces a Gaussian-process prior in the latent space to encourage temporally smooth representations. The GP structure provides principled uncertainty quantification over missing entries. Evaluated via PyPOTS for imputation, forecasting, and unconditional generation. • FlowTS [10] adapts rectified flows for time-series generation, learning a velocity field that transports Gaussian noise to the data distribution. The Official repository supports generation only; we 

Re-impl. its imputation pipeline (conditioning on observed entries during ODE integration) and forecasting pipeline (impute-then-forecast) following the paper’s description. • TSFlow [12] integrates Gaussian-process priors within conditional flow matching for probabilistic forecasting, using the GP posterior as the source distribution. The Official repository supports forecasting only; we Re-impl. its imputation pipeline by adapting the GP-conditioned flow to reconstruct missing entries. On Electricity ( D=20 ), TSFlow’s per-sample GP Cholesky loop has 

O(B × D × T 3) complexity, requiring ∼60 minutes per epoch. We implemented a batched variant that groups samples sharing identical mask patterns and amortizes the Cholesky factorization, yielding a ∼600 × speedup while preserving identical GP posteriors. All tuning configurations converged smoothly. 14 • PSW-I [26] formulates imputation as an optimal-transport problem and uses importance-weighted samples to minimize the Wasserstein distance between imputed and true distributions. Evaluated using the Official codebase, which supports imputation only. • CLWF [18] applies Lagrangian optimal-transport flows that learn a continuous-time transport map from a source distribution to the data manifold, with a Lagrangian cost function that encourages straight trajectories. No official code is available; we Re-impl. both imputation and forecasting pipelines following the published algorithm. 

Why baselines are rerun rather than quoted. No prior work reports results on this exact grid (four mechanisms, two rates, and three sequence lengths under a single protocol), and published imputation numbers differ in masking convention, normalization, and window length, so quoting them would not give a like-for-like comparison. We therefore rerun every baseline under identical splits, masks, and metrics. 

Fairness of comparison. Three baselines include re-implemented components: FlowTS (imputa-tion/forecasting), TSFlow (imputation), and CLWF (all tasks). To verify that these re-implementations are competitive rather than artificially weakened, and that the comparison is fair, we note three facts. First, the three settings where MissPath-FM loses are all to official implementations (SAITS and BRITS via PyPOTS), not to any re-implemented baseline. Second, our re-implemented TSFlow achieves second-best performance on 6 of 32 imputation settings (all on Synthetic), indicating that the re-implementation faithfully captures the method’s strengths. Third, retraining the two strongest baselines with our RandomRate augmentation does not improve them (Appendix K.3). All re-implementations follow published algorithmic descriptions.

Proof of Proposition 1. The decomposition follows from linearity of expectation, conditioning on m: E[∥x1 − z0∥2] = P

Prior bias–variance tradeoff. The interpolation and GP priors represent complementary points on a bias–variance spectrum. The interpolation prior has no fitted parameters and is robust to any temporal structure, but produces biased (flat) initializations in long contiguous gaps where no flanking observations exist. The GP prior can exploit temporal correlations when the kernel length scale 

ℓ matches the data’s characteristic frequency, reducing missing-entry transport Cmiss substantially (our trajectory analysis shows −39% on missing-entry arc length); however, mismatched ℓ degrades performance severely ( +41% at ℓ=8 vs the optimal ℓ=5 ; see Table 11). This sensitivity motivates our recommendation of interpolation as the default prior, with GP as an optional enhancement when domain knowledge about temporal structure is available. 

Forecasting mode. When used for forecasting with horizon h, the source prior is constructed exclusively from the history window. The interpolation operator I uses only observed entries within the history; the future segment receives the forward-fill value with σ2miss variance. For the GP prior, the conditioning set (to, yo) contains only history observations, and the posterior naturally extrapolates into the future with increasing uncertainty. ## F Uncertainty Calibration 

Our main evaluation uses MAE of the sample mean, which assesses point-estimate quality but not distributional calibration. To address this, we evaluate four complementary probabilistic metrics on 8 representative settings (4 datasets × {MCAR 30%, Block 30%}) against the three probabilistic baselines that produce sample ensembles: CSDI, TSFlow, and GP-VAE. 

CRPS (Continuous Ranked Probability Score) jointly measures calibration and sharpness. ACE 

(Average Calibration Error) computes the mean absolute gap between nominal and empirical coverage across 19 quantile levels (5%–95%); a perfectly calibrated model achieves ACE = 0 . IS 90 (Interval Score at 90%) combines interval width with a penalty for non-coverage. Energy Score is the multivariate generalization of CRPS, capturing cross-feature dependencies. All metrics are ↓ (lower is better). Table 9 shows that MissPath-FM’s advantage extends beyond point estimates to full distributional quality. Across 4 metrics × 8 settings = 32 comparisons, MissPath-FM wins 29 (90.6%), with a clean sweep on the multivariate Energy Score (8/8). Two of the three losses are to CSDI on Electricity Block 30% (CRPS and IS 90 ), the same regime where deterministic baselines also compete strongly (Table 2), suggesting that this regime benefits from CSDI’s iterative refinement; the third is ACE on ETTm1 Block 30%, where TSFlow is better calibrated. Notably, ACE values are consistently below 

0.10 for MissPath-FM (except Synth Block: 0.104 ), indicating well-calibrated uncertainty across quantile levels. GP-VAE achieves ACE = 0 .50 everywhere, confirming that its posterior collapses to a point estimate and provides no meaningful uncertainty.

## I Extended Analysis 

I.1 Transport Geometry 

Table 6 (main text) quantifies the arc-length analysis visualized in Figure 4. Arc lengths are computed by numerically integrating ∥vθ (xt, t )∥ along the ODE trajectory for each entry. Under the Gaussian 19 prior, observed and missing entries traverse nearly identical distances ( ℓobs = 0 .716 , ℓmiss = 0 .757 ,ratio 1.1×), confirming that the velocity field wastes half its capacity on already-known values. The interpolation prior reduces ℓobs by 15 × (from 0.716 to 0.047 ) while slightly increasing ℓmiss (from 

0.757 to 0.906 ), yielding a miss-to-obs ratio of 19 .2×. This confirms the core mechanism: the structured prior concentrates the velocity field on genuinely uncertain entries. The GP prior further reduces ℓmiss by 39% (from 0.906 to 0.557 ) compared to interpolation, because the GP posterior mean provides a better starting estimate for missing entries, reducing the distance the flow must traverse. Interestingly, the MA path has minimal effect on arc lengths ( 19 .2× vs 18 .9×), suggesting that its benefit operates through the temporal scheduling of transport rather than through reducing total transport distance. 

I.2 Gap-Bucketed Analysis 

Table 12 stratifies reconstruction error by gap length on Synthetic Block 50%, distinguishing medium gaps (5–12 contiguous missing steps) from long gaps ( > 12 steps). GP priors yield the largest gains on medium gaps ( −34 .8% over interpolation, from 0.456 to 0.297 ), where the GP posterior mean can leverage observations flanking the gap at moderate distance to produce an informative estimate. Long gaps remain challenging for all methods: even GP+MA achieves 0.607 , far from the medium-gap performance of 0.296 . This highlights a fundamental limitation: when no nearby observations exist within the kernel’s effective range, neither the prior nor the velocity network can fully resolve the uncertainty, and the model must rely on global distributional statistics rather than local temporal structure. ## L Negative Results 

Table 21 summarizes design alternatives that we explored but that failed to improve over our default configuration. We report these explicitly because they collectively reinforce the paper’s central thesis: when the source prior is well-designed, most “obvious” improvements to the velocity network, loss function, solver, or path become redundant. The failure of OT-like paths confirms that the interpolation prior already achieves near-optimal transport allocation, making explicit cost-aware couplings unnecessary. Gap-aware loss weighting likewise failed to help, indicating that once the prior anchors observed entries, reweighting the loss has little left to contribute; the only loss-side term we keep is the light observed-entry term of Eq. (9) (Appendix G).
