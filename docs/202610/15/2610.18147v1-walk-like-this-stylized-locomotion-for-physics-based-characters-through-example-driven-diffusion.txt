Abstract: We present a real-time framework for expressive legged locomotion that enables independent control over velocity and style. Unlike existing data-driven controllers, which typically require explicit style labels for training, our method efficiently leverages large-scale unstructured motion data and generalizes to unseen styles. We disentangle style from functional locomotion through a tai-lored sampling strategy used to train a style encoder whose output conditions an autoregressive diffusion model. During inference, the user provides a style refer-ence clip and a joystick velocity command. The diffusion model then generates a kinematic reference tracked by a reinforcement learning controller. To improve user controllability and stylization strength, we propose an attention biasing mech-anism and a velocity command correction strategy. We demonstrate our method on a physics-based humanoid character in simulation and on a physical robot, showcasing robust real-time control and continuous style modulation. The results can be best seen in our supplemental video 1.

Keywords: Motion Generation, Physics-Based Control, Stylized Locomotion 

# 1 Introduction 

A central challenge in physics-based humanoid control is to move beyond functional locomotion and to perform tasks in an expressive gait style . Although some stylized locomotion controllers exist, they are typically designed around fixed objectives or limited to imitating a narrow distribution of motions [1, 2, 3, 4, 5], offering little flexibility for stylistic variation and generalization after training. Similarly, while CAMDM [6] achieves real-time stylized character control, its conditioning on explicit style labels prevents generalization to new styles. Style transfer offers an alternative by mapping style onto target motions [7, 8, 9], but these methods are not designed for real-time robotic tracking or interactive control. On the other hand, motion generators [10, 11, 12, 13] can be grounded in physics but lack expressive control over style. Hence, a real-time framework that adapts to unseen styles on humanoid robots is currently missing.

To fill this gap, we propose a framework for the interactive control of humanoid robots that enables real-time user control via joystick commands and an example motion clip that contains the desired style characteristics. This gives users intuitive control over both what the robot does and how it performs these movements. To enable this, we build a stylized generative motion model based on autoregressive diffusion [6]. Crucially, we condition the model on a style embedding that is extracted from a style example through a transformer-based encoder. We jointly train the diffusion model and style encoder on unlabeled data, and use a tailored sampling strategy to disentangle style from functional locomotion. Our method both generalizes to new styles and gives users the flexibility to provide style examples of any duration. We highlight both aspects in our experiments by extracting a style directly from a recorded human video demonstration. To enhance style adherence and enable rapid style transitions, we introduce an inference-time technique that re-weights the attention scores within the diffusion model, outperforming standard classifier-free guidance (CFG) [14] both in efficiency and effectiveness. Furthermore, we add a ve-locity correction scheme that mitigates drift when following joystick commands. Finally, we bridge the gap between generated motion and hardware dynamics by pairing the diffusion model with a reinforcement learning (RL) tracking controller. This combines the expressive power of a motion generator with the robustness of an RL low-level controller, as we validate with closed-loop simula-tion and hardware deployment. Our core contributions: (i) A generative motion-control framework enabling user control over locomotion and style, trained from large-scale unstructured datasets and demonstrated on hardware. 

(ii) An autoregressive diffusion model and style encoder that disentangle style from functional locomotion through an effective sampling strategy during training. (iii) An inference-time atten-tion biasing technique to modulate stylization strength alongside a velocity correction scheme to mitigate drift. We validate each design choice through ablations. 

# 2 Related Work 

Our work builds upon advancements in kinematic motion synthesis and physics-based control. Tab. 1 contrasts our work with prior approaches. 

Stylized Motion Generation Stylized motion generation traditionally relies on style transfer from reference to content motion [20], with modern approaches utilizing network activation matching [7, 15, 16, 17] or diffusion models [9, 18, 19]. While they produce high-quality results, these methods are usually too slow for real-time use and depend heavily on the source content motion. Even when coupled with generative models to mitigate this dependency [9, 19], they still lack the performance and controllability required for interactive applications. In contrast, purely generative approaches are better suited for real-time interaction [6, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. Closest to our work is CAMDM [6], a state-of-the-art interactive generator that uses an autoregressive diffusion model conditioned on discrete labels. While CAMDM achieves real-time, high-quality synthesis, it is restricted to seen training styles, provides purely kinematic motion, and lacks explicit control over style intensity or transitions. We overcome these limitations by introducing a more general style conditioning mechanism, explicit control over stylization strength, and an RL tracker to enforce physicality. 2noise

Physics-Based Character Control Physics-based RL is well-established for robust humanoid lo-comotion [27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. Compared to command-based strategies [27, 28, 32], tracking-based approaches offer superior style control [31, 33, 34], scaling from single references [34] to large datasets via mixture-of-experts [35, 36] or implicit rewards [1, 2, 3]. However, frameworks like CALM [37] fail to explicitly disentangle style from skills, and character-specific gait-design tools [4, 5, 38, 39] lack generalization. To enforce physical feasibility, recent trends combine kinematic diffusion with RL through iterative training [13], joint state-action prediction [40], or closed-loop fine-tuning [11], though these are often limited to simulation. RobotMDM [10] tackles the sim-to-real gap by fine-tuning the diffusion model. We instead train a tracking policy from scratch on a large dataset of diffusion-generated motions.

# 4 Method 

We develop a real-time interactive controller for stylized locomotion of physics-based characters, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Our framework contains two models: a kinematic, autoregressive diffusion model (Sec. 4.1) and an RL-based tracking controller (Sec. 4.3). 3The diffusion model generates kinematic motions based on user velocity commands and a stylized motion clip. A style encoder learns to extract the style from the motion in a compressed latent space. Both the generator and the style encoder are trained jointly from an unstructured motion dataset. During inference, we propose two simple yet effective techniques to improve stylization and adherence to user commands (Sec. 4.2). First, we modify bias terms in the generator transformer’s attention layers. Second, we enable intuitive user control through a velocity correction module that mitigates global drift. To transfer the generated kinematic motions onto a physics-based character, we use an RL-based tracking controller. We train this controller on a large dataset generated through autoregressive rollouts of our diffusion model. 

4.1 Kinematic Diffusion Model 

We use an autoregressive diffusion model for predicting the character’s next F states Mfut , inspired by CAMDM [6] (see Sec. 3). We condition the model on the past P states Mpst and motion differ-ences Dpst , future root states Cfut and a style condition csyl :

Inputs are tokenized individually per timestep through linear maps before being fed to the transformer, except the diffusion step d, for which we use a learned lookup table. We use a motion representation similar to [6], where the state at each timestep consists of the root position, orientation, joint orientations as well as link positions and orientations (details can be found in Appendix A.1). Unlike CAMDM, the future root states Cfut contain desired root yaw angles and not full rotations so that the character’s roll and pitch are fully controlled through the imposed style. Furthermore, we add past motion differences Dpst as an additional condition, computed as position and rotation differences of consecutive frames in Mpst . While the information in Dpst is redundant, we found it to improve coherence during autoregressive rollouts, e.g., consistent swing directions of arms. 

Encoding Style We compute style embeddings csyl by compressing a stylized motion clip Msyl to a latent representation using our style transformer E, i.e., csyl = E(Msyl ). Contrary to discrete style labels [6], such inputs contain explicit information about the style and allow generalization outside the set of training styles [7]. The style transformer E is also a transformer-encoder, where all frames of the style motion are tokenized through a linear projection. The transformer architecture is well suited to represent the various timespans S of different styles. In addition, we enforce ∥csyl ∥ =1 through normalization which facilitates generalization [3, 44] and allows mixing styles through spherical linear interpolation. Inspired by the use of register tokens in vision transformers [45], we use an input-independent register token r in the inputs of the style transformer. It serves as a working space to capture relevant style information and we use its value after the final transformer layer as the output csyl . See Fig. 2 for a visualization of the style encoder in the full model architecture. 

Training Both M and E are trained jointly. We first sample a motion sequence from the training data and then sample P + F consecutive frames to get Mpst and Mfut . The future root states Cfut are directly read from the motion. The model is then trained to reconstruct Mfut from a noisy version of it. For the style encoder, a different window of length S ∈ [Smin , S max ] is sampled from the 

same motion sequence to yield Msyl . Crucially, this dual sampling allows the style transformer to disentangle the style of the sequence from its content. We define content as the ground-projected root trajectory, while style encompasses all residual motion dynamics. The loss function combines a reconstruction loss with velocity, joint consistency, and foot sliding losses, adopted from [6]. 

4.2 Diffusion Model Inference 

We deploy the diffusion model autoregressively, conditioning the prediction of the next F frames on its own past predictions, the style csyl , and the future root states Cfut . The autoregressive nature of the model, however, comes with a number of limitations, which we address through inference-time adjustments. 4Attention Bias Conditioning on past motion gives the model a strong signal for how future motion should be generated, often leading it to ignore style conditioning and get stuck in a single style. To address this, we propose biasing the attention mechanism during inference. Specifically, attention in the transformer layers is computed as attention (Q, K, V; B) = softmax

This forces the model to respect the style token, facilitating style transitions and improving stylization strength. 

Velocity Correction The user controls the character through joystick commands (vx,y , v ψ ) com-prising heading, lateral, and yaw velocities, which are integrated over timesteps t ∈ { 1, . . . , F } to form the trajectory conditioning signal Cfut for the diffusion model. During inference, the character can drift from the intended trajectory due to accumulation of prediction errors. Furthermore, the character is controlled through velocity commands expressed in the root heading frame. As the root naturally oscillates during movement, the generated trajectory depends heavily on where exactly in that cycle the diffusion model is triggered. We prevent excessive corrections by projecting the desired state into a neighborhood of the current state during each timestep. Specifically, the desired position is clipped to be at most 0.5 m away from the current position and the desired yaw is clipped into a 0.5 rad range around the current orientation. We refer to Appendix A.1 for further details. 

4.3 RL-Based Tracking Controller 

We use RL to train a policy that tracks the generated kinematic motions, largely following related work [33, 34]. The policy predicts actions given observations and goal states. We refer to Ap-pendix A.2 for details on the design of the action space, rewards, observations, and goal states. 

Training Data We avoid the computational overhead of querying the diffusion model during RL by generating a large motion dataset offline via autoregressive rollouts. To do so, we run the diffusion model conditioned on style clips randomly sampled from the diffusion model’s training data paired with randomly sampled joystick commands. This ensures that we cover the whole space of motions that can be generated by the diffusion model. 

Deployment We deploy the policy and diffusion model in an open loop: the diffusion model is conditioned on its own past predictions, rather than the real state of the character, which we found to improve stylization quality. # 5 Experiments and Results 

We first compare our method to CAMDM [6] and analyze our style encoder. We then evaluate the attention biasing and velocity correction. Finally, we ablate our RL tracking policy and transfer our method onto a real robot. We refer to the Appendix for implementation details. 

5.1 Diffusion Model Quantitative Evaluation We compare our diffusion model against CAMDM [6], the current state of the art in generative modeling of stylized motion and the only model directly applicable to our setting when evaluating seen styles (Tab. 1). Both models are trained on the 100STYLE dataset [23]. We report FID score [47], style accuracy, foot sliding, and positional and yaw errors with respect to the condition trajectory (see Appendix A.3). We summarize results in Tab. 2 and provide a qualitative comparison in Appendix A.3.1. Compared to CAMDM, our model generates motion that is closer to the real motion capture data, as indicated by the lower FID and higher style accuracy, while achieving similar results in terms of physicality and alignment to the trajectory conditions. Furthermore, we find that adding attention biasing at inference improves overall motion quality for both CAMDM and our model, which we discuss in more detail in Sec. 5.2. 

Style Encoder We highlight the interpolation and generalization capabilities of our style encoder. First, we encode styles from the 100STYLE dataset and spherically interpolate between the cor-responding style latents. Next, we evaluate the generalization of our method by capturing videos of a person performing a given style. We then extract 120 frames of motion features from that video using an open-source pose estimation model [48] and pass them as input to the style encoder. Notably, no further processing after pose estimation is needed before feeding the clips to the style encoder. As seen in Fig. 3 and our supplemental video, our model interpolates smoothly between styles and even generalizes to a highly dynamic style such as jumping. For this experiment, we train the model on both the 100STYLE [23] and AMASS [49] datasets to improve generalization capabilities. Furthermore, Tab. 2 shows quantitative evaluations on a set of 10 styles of 100STYLE not included during training. Importantly, CAMDM is not applicable here, as it is inherently limited to seen styles. On unseen styles, our model reproduces their distinctive features and reaches style accuracy comparable to that on seen styles, but it struggles with finer details, leading to a higher FID. Visualizations of the motions generated for the test split can be found in Appendix A.3.1. Fi-nally, we apply three additional probes to the style latents for further evidence of disentanglement between style and content (the ground-projected root trajectory). Style Clip Sampling Strategy We verify the importance of our style encoder sampling strategy by training a model where we always sample the same motion window for both style reference and past and future motion. Using the same motion window results in a sample diversity of 0.8 deg for joint angles across different generated motion windows, while using different motion windows (our approach) results in 5.5 deg . We refer to Appendix A.3.1 for qualitative results, which show that without our sampling scheme, the model collapses, causing all generated motion windows to repeat the reference motion. In contrast, with our sampling scheme, we can disentangle the style from the locomotion and generate diverse motion. 

5.2 Inference Techniques Attention Bias We compare our proposed attention bias method to CFG [14] on past motion where the predicted motion is a weighted average of a fully conditioned and a masked prediction (see Appendix A.3). Tab. 2 shows how attention biasing significantly improves quality and stylization of generated motions. As it is a pure inference adjustment, we can apply it to both models, Ours + Bias and CAMDM + Bias , though the relative improvement is smaller for CAMDM because its discrete style labels are less informative than our style encodings. Improved stylization comes at the cost of more foot sliding, as negative weighting of past motion makes it more challenging to create coherent foot swings. Compared to CFG, attention biasing performs better in all metrics. We conjecture that this performance gap arises because the bias modifies the pre-softmax weights, so attention stays a normalized distribution over the same tokens and thus closer to the training regime. In contrast, CFG interpolates two separate in-distribution predictions at every denoising step, and the result of this guidance step may no longer lie in the training distribution. 5.3 Physics-Based Tracking RL Tracking Policy We evaluate how the choice of training data affects the RL policy’s tracking of diffusion-generated motions. Tab. 3 shows that training a policy on the original, non-diffusion-generated data has a significantly higher failure rate (falls per minute) when tracking diffusion output than when training on generated data. This happens because the policy trained on the diffusion output learns to correct artifacts, such as foot sliding, yielding improved stability at the cost of higher tracking errors and worse motion quality. We further compare our open-loop approach, where the diffusion model is conditioned on its own past predictions, with a closed-loop approach, where the diffusion model is conditioned on states estimated from the robot. The open-loop approach leads to better stylization since, in closed loop, the past simulated states differ in distribution from the past motions the diffusion model was trained on. Closed-loop execution leads to lower tracking errors, because the tracking errors enter the diffusion conditioning, pulling the generated motion 8toward what the policy executes rather than toward the intended style. Finally, we apply perturbation forces of 600 N to the character root, resulting in lower motion quality, higher tracking errors, and significantly more failures for both open and closed loop. Open loop nevertheless remains more stable than closed loop. Recovery states can enter the diffusion conditioning in closed-loop mode, pushing the diffusion model out of distribution. In contrast, with an open-loop deployment the diffusion model’s generation stream stays uncorrupted and provides clean recovery targets for the policy. We found no degradation of motion quality over the whole time horizon of 20 minutes per rollout. The tracking evaluations discussed so far were performed for styles contained in the training set of both the diffusion model and RL policy. For styles that are generated by interpolating between style latents of training styles, as seen in Fig. 3, tracking performance stays consistent at an MPJAE of 7.9 deg and 0 falls. FID and style accuracy cannot be computed for such interpolated styles, as no ground-truth data exists. 

Transfer to Physical Robot We apply our method to a Unitree G1 humanoid robot ( 1.32 m , 35 kg ,

29 DoF). After retargeting the human data to the G1 with ReActor [51], we train our model and de-ploy it zero-shot on the robot. Qualitative results, provided in Fig. 4 and our supplemental video, show that a single controller can robustly track diverse motion styles using different joystick in-puts. The robustness of the open-loop mode carries over to hardware, including under disturbances. Throughout ∼4 h of deployments, the robot successfully executed various trajectories and velocity commands across different styles without any failures. The diffusion model runs in real time on an onboard NVIDIA Jetson Thor at ∼11 ms per inference step and ∼17 ms command-to-motion output latency, compared to 8 ms /12 ms for simulation on an NVIDIA RTX 5090. 

# 6 Limitations 

While our work presents a framework for general, stylized locomotion control, several limitations remain. The RL policy can correct artifacts of the diffusion model (foot sliding, hovering, ground penetration). However, there can be a mismatch between the kinematic motions and the commanded velocity, which manifests as the tracking policy taking extra in-between steps to maintain stability. Introducing implicit rewards to achieve natural gaits could present a solution to these issues [1, 2]. Our method works with styles that are agnostic to the direction of movement, such as flailing the arms or walking in a wide stance. For styles that are tightly coupled to the direction of movement, such as a moonwalk or cartwheels, our method requires matching velocity commands. Furthermore, styles that are largely described through a distinct root trajectory, such as rotating clockwise while walking, specify the same quantity as the joystick, creating an inherent conflict in the interface, which we resolve in favor of the user command. Finally, our current framework lets the model learn the style implicitly. A more explicit parameterization could allow users to partially stylize, for example, focusing only on the lower or upper body behavior of a style reference. 

# 7 Conclusion 

We presented a real-time system for expressive legged locomotion that enables simultaneous user control over velocity and style via an example-driven, autoregressive diffusion model. Through a style encoder that is trained with an effective sampling strategy on short, unstructured motion clips rather than fixed style labels, we enable physics-based characters to generalize to completely unseen styles, including those extracted from real-world videos. This flexible interface opens the door to various other style input modalities, such as artist-created animations. 9Acknowledgments

A.1 Kinematic Diffusion Model Motion Representation We represent the motion state, similarly to CAMDM [6], as 

m = ( x, r, J, X, R), (6) with the position x ∈ R3 and orientation r ∈ R6 of the character’s root, the orientations of all its 

NJ joints J ∈ RNJ ×6, and the positions X ∈ RNB ×3 and orientations R ∈ RNB ×6 of all its NB

links. We represent all rotations in 6D [52]. For joint orientations, we represent a child’s orientation in the kinematic chain by its orientation in the parent’s coordinate frame. To train a model that is agnostic to the character’s global state, we transform all quantities, except the joint orientations, in Mpst and Mfut , to the heading frame of the character, extracted from the current state mt. The origin of this frame is set to the character’s ground-projected root position, its x-axis points in the direction of the root’s yaw, and the z-axis is aligned with the global up vector.

Model and Training Parameters Both the diffusion model M

and style encoder E are realized as transformer-encoders [43] with the same model parameters which can be seen in the inset table. These parameters are used across all experiments for both the hu-manoid and robotic characters. We train all models with 8 diffusion steps with a cosine noise schedule. Training is performed for 100 epochs and the model weights are optimized using Adam [53] with default parameters except for the learning rate, which is set to 10 −4. During training we randomly mask past motion by setting it to 0 with probability 0.15 . For the humanoid character we represent motion at 30 frames per second and set the future frames F = 20 with past frames P = 10 . The robotic character uses 50 frames per second with F = 30 and P = 15 . To ensure responsiveness to velocity commands, we trigger early autoregression after 10 and 15 frames, respectively, if the velocity commands change. For both characters, we sample style clips of lengths 1 s to 5 s . An im-portant consideration is that the humanoid uses spherical joints while the robot uses revolute joints. For the diffusion model itself, we treat both joint types as if they were spherical joints, which allows the diffusion model to predict infeasible orientations for the revolute joints. We then post-process the diffusion model predictions by projecting the predicted orientations onto the set of feasible ori-entations using a swing-twist decomposition [54]. 

Motion Smoothing To alleviate discontinuities between consecutive diffusion predictions, we ap-ply a smoothing scheme to generate motion. Specifically, rather than generating a new motion every 

F frames, we trigger the diffusion model after only F − K frames, creating a K-frame overlap be-tween consecutive predictions, as visualized in Fig. A.1. Then, the overlapping frames are blended together, using linear and spherical interpolation for positions and rotations, respectively. In prac-tice, we set K = 3 .

A.2 Reinforcement Learning 

We use RL to train a policy π(at | ot, gt) to track the generated motion. The policy predicts joint setpoints at for proportional-derivative (PD) controllers based on observations ot and goal condi-tioning gt. The conditioning gt contains the character’s target state at timestep t as predicted by the diffusion model, converted into the character’s heading frame of step t − 1. Additionally, we explicitly concatenate the joystick command to the target state. Proprioceptive observations consist of joint positions and velocities, root angular velocity, projected gravity and a history of previous actions.

A.3 Evaluations Evaluation Setup We train the diffusion model for 100 epochs. When training on 100STYLE [23] this corresponds to roughly two days of training on an NVIDIA RTX 4090. With the inclusion of AMASS [49], training time increases to roughly five days. To ensure convergence, the RL policies are trained for five days on an NVIDIA RTX 4090. However, reasonable performance is already achieved after a few hours of training. For all experiments involving attention biasing we set wsyl =2.0, w pst = −1.0 and for CFG we set γCFG = 0 .6. To assess the diffusion model quality, we generate 20 minutes of motion for each of the 100 styles in 100STYLE. For the transitions, we generate 5 transition motions from each style to every other style in 100STYLE. Here, we first generate 5 s 

of motion in style i, then switch the style reference to style j and generate another 5 s of motion. Velocity commands are randomly sampled for both the diffusion model and transition evaluations. For the evaluations of the RL policies we generate and track 20 hours of motions with randomized commands.

Style accuracy is computed as the percentage of generated motions for which the style classifier predicts the style label that corresponds to the motion that was used as style refer-ence.
