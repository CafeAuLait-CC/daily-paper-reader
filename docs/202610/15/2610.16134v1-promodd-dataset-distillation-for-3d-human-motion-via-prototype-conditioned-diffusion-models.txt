# ProMoDD: Dataset Distillation for 3D Human Motion via Prototype-Conditioned Diffusion Models 

Yijin Wei, Yawen Zou, Wenqi Cai, Guang Li, Hangli Ge, Katsuya Hotta, Chunzhi Gu, and Chao Zhang 

Abstract —Dataset distillation (DD) aims to retain the training utility of a large dataset in a much smaller set of examples. For skeleton-based action recognition, this requires capturing discriminative motion variation without disrupting the geometry and temporal continuity of articulated bodies. Directly optimizing joint coordinates can violate these constraints, while plausible motion generation alone does not ensure useful training examples. We propose Prototype-Conditioned Motion Dataset Distillation (ProMoDD), which conditions a motion diffusion model on class-wise prototypes of temporally resolved recognition features. We further tailor diffusion training to motion data, enabling the generator to model both skeletal configurations and their temporal evolution. We then construct each compact training set through budget-aware hybrid selection: representative real se-quences cover densely populated regions of the pooled recognition space, while selected generated candidates complement them in less-populated regions. This couples prototype-guided generation with feature-space selection, balancing the reliability of observed motions with the variation of synthesis under a fixed per-class sequence budget. Across five difficulty-stratified subsets of the single-person classes in NTU RGB+D 60, ProMoDD achieves its largest gains under tight distillation budgets, reaching 40 .32% 

top-1 accuracy on Full49 with three sequences per class, 4.57 percentage points above class-wise k-means. These gains reflect the complete framework. The same distilled sets train ST-GCN, MS-G3D, and CTR-GCN without regeneration. 

Index Terms —3D human skeleton, dataset distillation, diffu-sion models, skeleton-based action recognition

Motion collections have grown in scale and diversity [3]– [6], from NTU RGB+D [7] and NTU RGB+D 120 [8] to broader corpora such as Motion-X [5]. Although these datasets serve different tasks, their growing scale raises the compu-tational cost of repeatedly training and evaluating motion models. Generative priors help image DD avoid unrestricted pixel optimization [13], [14], and motion diffusion offers a learned prior for skeleton sequences [15]. Unlike images, however, skeleton motion must preserve both anatomical connections and temporal continuity. We therefore condition diffusion on class-wise prototypes of temporally resolved recognition features, capturing within-class motion patterns beyond ac-tion labels alone. A plausible motion is not necessarily a useful training example. Real-only selection is limited to observed motions, whereas an all-synthetic set risks inaccurate or redundant candidates. We thus use budget-aware hybrid construction: representative real sequences cover densely pop-ulated regions of the pooled recognition space, while selected generated candidates fill less-populated regions. Because a prototype condition does not determine where a generated sequence lies in the pooled space, we assign candidates using the pooled recognition features of the generated sequences. Taken together, these requirements call for an integrated framework that connects prototype-guided generation to budget-aware sample selection. We instantiate this framework as Prototype-Conditioned Motion Dataset Distillation (Pro-MoDD). The method centers on a motion diffusion generator tailored to skeleton motion. Conditioned on class-wise proto-types of temporally resolved recognition features, it models skeletal configurations and their temporal evolution. We then combine generated candidates with representative real se-quences through budget-aware selection in pooled recognition-feature space. Because generator training is independent of student optimization, it can be reused across sequence budgets and architectures. To address the lack of an established benchmark for dataset distillation in human motion, we build an evaluation suite from five overlapping, difficulty-stratified subsets of the 49 single-person classes in NTU RGB+D 60. Under the cross-subject protocol [7], we evaluate ProMoDD across budgets against random selection, class-wise k-means, and coordinate-space DM. On Full49 at three sequences per class, ProMoDD achieves 40 .32% accuracy, 4.57 percentage points above k-means. The gap narrows at larger budgets. A same-layer control finds no reliable gain from time-varying conditioning, while the distilled sets train ST-GCN [16], MS-G3D [17], and CTR-GCN [18], without regeneration. Motion representations and generation conditions. 

Skeleton-based recognition models learn spatial relations between joints and their temporal evolution. ST-GCN [16] models the body as a spatiotemporal graph, while MS-G3D [17] and CTR-GCN [18] extend the representation with multi-scale dependencies and channel-wise topology refinement. Their intermediate maps retain temporal structure before global aggregation for classification. In motion generation, action-conditioned models use discrete labels to specify the intended activity [19]–[21]. MDM [15] and MotionDiffuse [22] extend conditional generation to natural-language descriptions and diffusion-based synthesis [23], [24]. These conditions specify action semantics, but a class label alone cannot distinguish the temporal patterns of individual executions. Our generator instead uses multiple recognition-feature prototypes within each class. The prototypes preserve temporal positions in the extracted representation rather than reducing each motion to one pooled vector. This distinction also separates the objective of our generator from conventional motion synthesis. The aim is not simply to follow a semantic prompt, but to produce sequences that retain information useful for training an action classifier. 

Dataset distillation and generative priors. Dataset distil-lation seeks a small training set that retains the utility of a larger one [25]. DC [10], DM [11], and MTT [12] respectively match training gradients, feature distributions, and optimiza-tion trajectories [26]. When adapted to skeleton coordinates, direct sample optimization can modify joint locations without preserving bone geometry or temporal consistency. Generative approaches introduce learned priors to constrain the space of synthetic data [27], [28]. GLaD optimizes data through a generative latent representation [13], while Minimax Diffusion and D 4M use diffusion-based generation for compact image training sets [14], [29], [30]. UniDD introduces task-guided diffusion for multiple visual tasks, and learnability-guided diffusion adapts generation to the state of the learner [31], [32]. ProtoBlend uses feature-space clusters to allocate prototypes in video DD [33]. D 4M applies diffusion to image latents and uses category prototypes to guide generation [14]. Our method instead uses an ordered sequence of recognition features to guide diffusion over skeleton coordinates. Ignoring temporal order loses information about how an action unfolds, so we preserve it and add losses on motion velocity and acceleration to encourage coherent transitions. 

Real-sample selection and hybrid construction. Real-data reduction offers a simpler alternative to synthesizing every training entry [34]. Coreset methods select representative recordings, and dataset quantization constructs compact sub-sets from real images [35], [36]. DREAM [37] uses repre-sentative samples as targets for more stable dataset match-ing, whereas RDED [38] emphasizes realism and diversity in compact data construction. Selection preserves recorded motion but is limited to existing executions. Generation can provide additional candidates, although they may be inaccurate or redundant. We use the two sources together by placing real sequences in densely supported pooled-feature regions and as-signing generated candidates to the remaining regions. Unlike DREAM, which uses representatives as matching targets, our retained real sequences are themselves part of the final student training set. A. Problem Formulation and Overview 

Let D = {(xi, y i)}Ni=1 contain C action classes, with each motion xi ∈ RF ×J×3. Given a budget of n sequences per class (SPC), we construct a class-balanced surrogate eD = SCc=1 eDc,where | eDc| = n. The goal is to retain recognition utility when a student is trained on this surrogate instead of the full dataset. ProMoDD comprises three stages, as illustrated in Fig. 2. A frozen recognizer provides temporally resolved features, which we cluster within each class to form generation prototypes. These prototypes condition a motion diffusion model to sam-ple skeleton sequences. We select real or generated motion sequences according to their pooled recognition features under the class-wise budget. The features guide selection, while the distilled set contains the corresponding motion sequences. The generator can be reused across budgets and student architectures. Solving this problem requires class-wise prototypes that capture within-class temporal variation, a motion diffusion model trained to generate coherent skeleton sequences under these conditions, and a budget-aware selector that chooses real or generated motions in a shared recognition-feature space. The following subsections detail these components in order: temporal prototype estimation (Sec. III-B), temporally conditioned skeleton diffusion (Sec. III-C), and hybrid set construction (Sec. III-D).

C. Temporally Conditioned Skeleton Diffusion 

We build on the x0-prediction formulation of MDM [15]. During training, forward noising converts a clean skeleton sequence x0 = xi into a noisy input xt at diffusion step t.In parallel, frozen gF extracts the temporal condition zi. The diffusion generator takes (xt, t, y i, zi) as input and predicts clean motion. During generation, the class prototype pc,k 

replaces zi, and sampling starts from Gaussian noise (Fig. 3). The generator operates on coordinates without a latent decoder and is trained independently of the downstream student. 

Temporal conditioning. As illustrated in Fig. 3, a temporal Transformer encodes the feature map used to condition the diffusion generator: it takes the sample feature zi during train-ing and the class prototype pc,k during generation. Positional encodings preserve the order of the L temporal positions, and the Transformer maps them to L condition tokens. A shared projection maps these tokens and their temporally pooled summary to the generator width dh. The pooled summary is combined with the action-label and diffusion-timestep embeddings to form a global condition token. The generator prepends this global token and the temporal tokens to the projected noisy motion frames, allowing self-attention to use both sequence-level and time-resolved information. The temporal Transformer and diffusion generator are optimized jointly, while the feature extractor gF remains frozen. 

Motion-aware generator training. The diffusion generator predicts clean motion, denoted by ˆx0. We use classifier-free guidance (CFG) [40]: during training, the action-label and tem-poral conditions are jointly dropped with probability pdrop , so the same generator learns both conditional and unconditional predictions. At sampling, these predictions are combined using guidance scale w. The unconditional prediction serves only as a reference for the conditional prediction. CFG uses their difference to strengthen the influence of the supplied class label and prototype, controlling condition adherence rather than removing conditioning. In addition to coordinate recon-struction, we match the first and second temporal differences of predicted and target motions.

D. Real and Synthetic Set Construction 

At SPC = n, one option is to use the generator to produce all n sequences per class. Generated candidates expand the pool beyond the selected real examples, but may be inaccurate or redundant, which can limit their training value. Selecting only real sequences avoids synthesis errors, but restricts the distilled set to motions observed in the training data. We therefore combine representative real sequences with selected generated candidates, balancing the reliability of real examples with the additional variation offered by synthesis under a fixed per-class budget. For each class, the generator produces n candidate motions. We set the real-sample fraction to ρ(n) = nn+n0 , allocate 

nreal = round( ρ(n)n) sequences to the real subset, and use the remaining ngen = n − nreal generated candidates. The pa-rameter n0 > 0 controls how the real-sample fraction increases with the budget, shifting the allocation from generation-dominant at small budgets toward real-sample selection as n

grows. Fig. 2 illustrates our density-aware candidate selection strat-egy. We retain real sequences as reliable representatives of densely populated regions, where observed motions provide strong support, and use selected generated candidates to com-plement less-populated regions with limited real coverage. This pairs the reliability of observed motions with the potential variation of synthesis. Since prototype conditioning does not determine where generated motions fall in pooled space, we use globally pooled features to compare both sources. For each real sequence, we extract the globally pooled classifier-input feature ri ∈ Rdf from gF, subtract the full real-subset mean, and L2-normalize it with a small stabilizer to obtain hi. Generated candidates use the same real-data mean and normalization to obtain ehc,k . These pooled features support selection, while temporally resolved features condition generation. In the absence of an established benchmark for human-motion dataset distillation, we construct an evaluation suite to support controlled comparisons across distillation budgets and action difficulties. We choose NTU RGB+D 60 because it is a widely used, large-scale skeleton-action dataset with broad action coverage and an official cross-subject (CS) pro-tocol, providing a reproducible split and a common reference point [7]. We focus on its 49 single-person classes. The CS training and test splits contain 32 ,772 and 13 ,459 sequences, respectively. 

A. Difficulty-Stratified Evaluation Subsets 

An aggregate Full49 score can hide how well distillation preserves accuracy on easily recognized versus challenging actions. We therefore use difficulty-stratified subsets as de-scriptive stress tests, while retaining Full49 as the primary evaluation. A reference CTR-GCN trained on Full49 ranks classes by recognition difficulty. The per-example score com-bines a deficit in ground-truth confidence, a margin-derived term, normalized predictive entropy, and a misclassification indicator, with weights 0.4, 0.3, 0.2, and 0.1. The margin-derived term is half the sum of the confidence deficit and the largest wrong-class probability. The misclassification indicator is one when the predicted class differs from the ground-truth label and zero otherwise. We compute class difficulty as the weighted sum Sclass (c) = 0 .7 ¯dc + 0 .3qc, where ¯dc is the mean per-example score for class c and qc = max c′̸ =c Mc,c ′ is its largest off-diagonal entry in the row-normalized confusion matrix M (true classes are rows and predicted classes are columns). Easy10 contains ranks 1 to 10 , Medium20 ranks 15 to 34 ,Hard20 ranks 30 to 49 , and Mixed30 ranks 1 to 10 , 20 to 29 ,and 40 to 49 . Full49 uses all classes. These subsets overlap and retain all available training sequences for their selected classes. The class ranking is obtained from predictions on the official CS test split. Thus, these subsets are descriptive difficulty-stratified evaluations rather than independent benchmarks. Test predictions determine subset membership only. They are not used for per-sequence selection or as generator-conditioning targets. The number of classes and train/test sequences in each subset is summarized in Tab. I. 

B. Skeleton Preprocessing 

Our generator is defined on a fixed 22-joint HumanML3D skeleton, so we map NTU’s 25-joint annotations to this layout 6

to provide a consistent input topology and joint ordering. We retain the first body track and perform the mapping through direct joint correspondences and interpolated spine and collar positions, without SMPL fitting. Sequences with fewer than eight valid frames are discarded. We interpolate isolated joint spikes that move more than 0.5 m from both adjacent frames, then apply an orthonormal DCT retaining its lowest KDCT =10 coefficients. Motions are pelvis-centered, aligned using the torso and hips, and scaled by mean pelvis-to-neck length. We resample valid frames to F = 60 for generation. 

C. Evaluation Protocol and Metrics 

Comparison protocol. We evaluate SPC values of 

1, 3, 5, 10 , 20 , 30 , 50 , and 100 . Each method uses the same number of training entries per class. Baselines are random real selection, class-wise pooled-feature k-means, and coordinate-space DM [11]. Random selection uniformly samples n

distinct real training sequences per class without replacement. For the class-wise k-means baseline, we fit n clusters to the normalized pooled features hi defined in Sec. III-D and select the real sequence nearest to each cluster center. Our DM implementation initializes n synthetic sequences per class from randomly sampled real examples. Their joint coordinates are optimized to match class-wise real-data feature means using a CTR-GCN randomly reinitialized each iteration, without explicit skeletal-geometry or temporal-consistency constraints. Student architecture, optimizer, augmentations, epochs, and the training-time matching (TTM) objective [14] are matched within comparisons. We evaluate each method using five seeds s ∈ { 0, . . . , 4} and report the mean and standard deviation of test-set top-1 accuracy, our primary comparison metric. For ProMoDD, each seed jointly controls diffusion sampling, pooled-space k-means initialization, and student training, including initialization, data order, and augmentation. The corresponding random operations are also seeded for the selection and DM baselines. 

Training and seed control. The feature extractor is trained once on Full49. Each subset-specific teacher and each genera-tor configuration are trained once and held fixed across the five evaluation seeds. All generator training runs use seed 10. Thus, the reported standard deviations reflect variability in compact-set construction and student training, but not variability from independently retrained generators. All compact sets receive the same teacher-assisted student training. 

Motion metrics. We also report bone-length variation (BLV) as 100 times the mean instability score in Eq. (1). Jerk is calculated from third-order frame differences and averaged over all joints, frames, and sequences. Normalized jerk (NJ) divides this value by the jerk of the complete real Full49 train-ing split. Both diagnostics use the stored 60 -frame, 22 -joint sequences before augmentation, without further smoothing. The differences are computed over the resampled frame index, so NJ is a discrete sequence statistic rather than physical jerk in SI units. BLV summarizes all 21 bones, unlike the single-shin plot in Fig. 1.

D. Implementation Details 

Models and features. Tab. II summarizes the settings. The Full49 feature extractor gF is trained once and reused across subsets for temporal conditioning and pooled-feature selection. The subset-specific teacher fT provides confidence scores for prototype filtering and soft targets for student training. Both use CTR-GCN [18]. No external pretraining is used, and ST-GCN and MS-G3D [16], [17] are also evaluated on the same distilled sets. For feature extraction, gF runs in evaluation mode, with EF extracted after residual addition and ReLU in the eighth residual block, whose module name is l8 . Strides in blocks 5 and 8 reduce the temporal dimension from 64 to 16. Student training. Following D4M [14], all methods use the same TTM objective and student settings. TTM combines label supervision with KL matching to the frozen teacher, which receives the same augmented or mixed inputs as the student. Generated samples use their conditioning-class labels. Augmentation uses rigid rotations and temporal and joint-dimension mixing [41]. The KL term uses temperature τ and is scaled by τ 2, while label supervision uses unit temperature. The coefficients α and 1 − α weight the respective terms. 

Generator optimization. The objective in Eq. (5) uses sta-bility weights normalized by their minibatch mean. Gradients are clipped to an ℓ2 norm of 1.0, and updates with non-finite gradients are skipped. Hardware. Experiments were run on a system with an Intel Core Ultra 9 CPU, 64.0 GiB of memory, and two RTX 5090 GPUs. V. E XPERIMENTS

Effect of action difficulty. Difficulty affects how much ac-curacy each subset retains under distillation and how much it benefits from synthesis. At SPC = 3 , ProMoDD exceeds class-wise k-means by 1.93 points on Easy10, 6.17 on Medium20, and 4.77 on Hard20. The gain is therefore larger on Medium20 and Hard20 than on Easy10, although it does not increase monotonically with difficulty. Hard20 also loses more accuracy under distillation: at SPC = 10 , it retains about 63% of its full-data reference accuracy, compared with about 93% for Easy10. One possible explanation is that a few real exam-ples can represent easily recognized actions more effectively, while generated candidates can complement underrepresented regions for some more challenging actions. This interpretation is consistent with our hybrid selection strategy, but the exper-iments do not establish difficulty as the cause of the gains. The subsets overlap and were ranked using full-data teacher predictions, so these comparisons are descriptive rather than controlled tests of difficulty alone. 

Direct coordinate optimization. Our coordinate-space adap-tation of DM performs poorly in the tested setup: on Full49, the frozen teacher recognizes fewer than 6% of the optimized sequences, compared with over 95% of the real initializations, and Fig. 1 shows distorted bone lengths. This result does not show that DM is unsuitable for skeleton-based dataset distillation. Rather, it highlights the difficulty of directly opti-mizing a sequence of joint coordinates. Unlike image pixels, joint positions are coupled by skeletal geometry and temporal continuity, so unconstrained updates can produce invalid or unrecognizable motions.

We introduced ProMoDD, an integrated skeleton-based dataset distillation framework that combines motion diffu-sion conditioned on temporally resolved class prototypes, motion-aware training, and budget-aware hybrid selection, and evaluated it using five difficulty-stratified subsets of NTU RGB+D 60. On Full49 at SPC = 3, ProMoDD achieves 

40 .32% top-1 accuracy, 4.57 percentage points above class-wise k-means, and the same distilled sets train ST-GCN, MS-G3D, and CTR-GCN without regeneration. Our ablations also show that motion quality and recognition accuracy do not always improve together: temporal losses reduce normalized jerk with little change in accuracy, while explicit bone-length matching lowers BLV but can reduce accuracy. As the number of sequences per class increases, the accuracy gap between ProMoDD and real-only selection narrows. At larger budgets, real-only selection reaches similar accuracy with lower con-struction cost. One possible reason is that our current rule increases both the number and share of real samples as the budget grows. These real examples then cover more of the feature space, leaving less new coverage for generated samples 10 

to add. Future work will adapt the mix of real and generated samples to each budget and select generated examples based on the additional coverage and training benefit they provide. REFERENCES [1] Z.
