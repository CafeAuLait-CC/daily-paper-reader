## Abstract 

We consider the problem of learning to generate data while satisfying a prescribed 

sample-wise constraint with a user-specified probability of constraint satisfaction. Starting with a population-level constrained negative log-likelihood minimization and using a flow-matching (FM) parameterization, under regularity assumptions and sufficient expressivity of the velocity field family, we derive guarantees on the probability of constraint satisfaction for the corresponding primal-dual optimiza-tion algorithm. We provide a practical implementation of this approach, called Primal-Dual Flow Matching (PDFM), which has direct control over constraint satisfaction rate and requires only binary membership oracle feedback for con-straint satisfaction, avoiding the need for differentiable distances, projections, or special structure of the constraint set, such as convexity. We show that PDFM improves constraint satisfaction over vanilla FM and achieves competitive perfor-mance against constraint-aware baselines while maintaining distributional fidelity. Our code is available at https://github.com/ZhengyanHuan/PDFM .

## 1 Introduction 

The utility of generative models for scientific applications highly depends on their ability to generate samples that obey scientific laws or satisfy an application-specific criterion, modeled via constraints that can be validated numerically. For example, flow- and diffusion-based generative models are increasingly used to emulate chaotic spatiotemporal systems [Lienen et al., 2024, Du et al., 2024, Price et al., 2025], where the chaotic dynamics result in high sensitivity to initial conditions and therefore unpredictable trajectories that can be interpreted and modeled probabilistically [Medio and Lines, 2001]. However, in these settings, distributional fidelity alone is rarely sufficient: a generated sample is useful only if it is also physically consistent , e.g., satisfying boundary conditions, respecting conservation laws, or following a governing PDE [Beucler et al., 2021, Utkarsh et al., 2025]. Another important example comes from molecular generation [Hoogeboom et al., 2022, Song et al., 2023], where one wants to generate new molecules such that they conform to structures with chemically useful properties [Bickerton et al., 2012, Giaginis et al., 2018] that are numerically quantifiable. 

Related work: For constrained generation, one may divide existing literature into (a) Type of constraints, e.g., distributional constraints [Khalafi et al., 2024] or sample-wise constraints [this work]. Among sample-wise constraints, one may have constraints with topological specification, such as convexity [Liu et al., 2023]; constraints with known boundaries [Lou and Ermon, 2023, Fishman et al., 2023]; constraints with differentiable distances to them [Feng et al., 2025, Utkarsh et al., 2025]; or constraints that are known only via a membership oracle [Huan et al., 2026]; and (b) Methods used – whether it is inference-time [Christopher et al., 2024] [Utkarsh et al., 2025] [Li et al., 2026] [Zhu et al., 2026] or training-time [this work], [Lou and Ermon, 2023, Fishman et al., 2023] [Xie et al., 2024] [Feng et al., 2025] [Giannone et al., 2023] [Huan et al., 2026]. A more detailed review

For sample-wise constrained generation when the constraints are accessible via a membership oracle, we make the following main contributions :1. We develop a theoretically grounded approach, with provable guarantees on the probability of constraint satisfaction. In particular, leveraging an FM parameterization of a constrained negative log-likelihood minimization (Sections 2, 3), in Theorem 3.5 we show that under mild assumptions the Total Variation (TV) norm between the optimal solution and the FM parameterized solution can be controlled in terms of approximation error between the flows due to parametric modeling, which in turn leads to overall control on constraint satisfaction. We then show that a theoretical 

Primal-Dual Flow Matching (PDFM) Alg. 1 achieves this bound. 2. We then propose a practical implementation of PDFM in Alg. 2, with theoretical guarantees discussed in Sec. 4.3. Key innovations in this algorithm are motivated by deterministic policy gradient methods, where we build differentiable surrogates for constraint satisfaction probability under the FM parameterization. This enables the method to work with only membership oracle access to the constraint set. We note that this type of access to the constraint is the most flexible when the constraints are complex. 3. We provide an extensive evaluation of PDFM and show that compared to existing methods, it achieves competitive constraint satisfaction rates and distribution fidelity. For scientific applica-tions, we consider the problem of learning to generate PDE-constrained spatiotemporal dynamics of chaotic systems and show that our method achieves superior performance while requiring only binary feedback. In a molecule generation task, PDFM improves molecule stability and distributional fidelity over FM over FM, without task-specific designs.

Despite this weaker requirement, PDFM achieves strong constraint satisfaction while maintaining competitive distributional fidelity.

## 6 Conclusion 

In this paper, we present PDFM, a primal-dual framework for constrained generation that requires only membership oracle feedback, provides direct control over the constraint satisfaction rate, and admits theoretical guarantees. We also propose a non-stochastic constraint-guided approach for updating the velocity field, enabling efficient use of oracle feedback during primal optimization. One of the limitations of PDFM arises from the need to obtain sufficient positive constraint satisfaction feedback during training to learn fϕ reliably. Further details are discussed in Appendix I.

Constrained generative modeling has been studied from several complementary perspectives, de-pending on whether the constraint is imposed at the distribution level or at the level of individual generated samples. We refer to these two settings as distributional constraints and sample-wise constraints , respectively. This paper investigates sample-wise constraints with the objective of generating samples within the constraint set C.A closely related work of constrained generative models in the domain of distributional constraints 

is Khalafi et al. [2024], which requires the generated distribution to remain close in KL divergence, to one or more reference distributions. While Khalafi et al. [2024] and this work both utilize primal-dual frameworks for constrained generative modeling, their scopes and theoretical guarantees differ substantially due to different constraint settings. Algorithmically, this work’s optimization strategy designed for sample-wise constraints in Sec. 4.2 deviates from the score/flow loss in classical generative models. For sample-wise constraints, existing approaches can be broadly divided into training-time and 

inference-time methods . Training-time methods incorporate constraint information during model training, for example, by modifying the objective, architecture, or learned dynamics. This typically requires additional training effort, but they can generally use standard/slightly-modified sampling procedures at inference time. In contrast, inference-time methods can often be applied to regularly trained generative models, but they must steer, project, or correct the sampling trajectory during generation, which usually increases inference-time computational cost. This paper is a training-time method , that informs the FM model of the constraints during training and uses standard Euler sampling with the learned velocity field. 

A.1 Training-Time Methods 

Among training-time methods assuming Q(C) = 1 , approaches that enforce strict feasibility of the generated samples, i.e., P (C) = 1 , generally require stronger structures and knowledge of the constraint set C. Reflection-based methods, such as the reflected diffusion model (RDM) [Lou and Ermon, 2023, Fishman et al., 2023] and reflected flow matching (RFM) [Xie et al., 2024] enforce constraint satisfaction via reflected generation trajectories within C, requiring convexity of C and explicit knowledge of the constraint boundary ∂C. Mirror-based approaches, such as mirror diffusion model (MDM) [Liu et al., 2023], enforce constraint satisfaction by performing DDPM training in the domain of an unconstrained mapping of C, and obtaining generated samples from the inverse mapping of the unconstrained domain. MDM requires convexity of C and an available closed-form bijective mapping between C and the unconstrained space. Convexity of C, boundary information, and tractable bijective maps are often unavailable for complex constraints in practical settings. To broaden applicability, several existing methods relax strict feasibility guarantees and instead aim to improve, but not necessarily ensure, constraint satisfaction. Neural approximate mirror map (NAMM) [Feng et al., 2025] aims to approximate the bijective projection in MDM via NNs, relaxing the requirement of convexity and a closed-form bijective map to a differentiable distance to C. Trajectory Alignment [Giannone et al., 2023] trains diffusion models to align their sampling paths with the intermediate iterates of a physics-based optimizer, improving constrained design generation but requiring access to optimizer-generated trajectories during training. Flow Matching with Random Exploration (FM-RE) [Huan et al., 2026] improves feasibility by adding an explicit constraint-aware term to the FM training objective and injecting Gaussian noise into the generation trajectories to enable likelihood-ratio gradient estimation, thereby requiring only membership oracle feedback for C. However, FM-RE’s P (C) is only implicitly controlled by the weight of the constraint-aware term, and FM-RE does not provide a theoretical guarantee for feasibility. PDFM improves upon FM-RE while retaining the same weak requirement of C, i.e., membership oracle access, and further provides theoretical guarantees and direct control over the constraint satisfaction rate P (C).

A.2 Inference-Time Methods 

Inference-time methods improve or enforce constraint satisfaction by modifying the sampling pro-cedure of a pretrained generative model, typically through guidance, projection, or trajectory cor-14 rection during generation. Projected diffusion model (PDM) [Christopher et al., 2024] enforces strict feasibility by repeatedly projecting stochastic diffusion updates onto C at each step, requiring an available projection function, and potentially causing deviations from the training distribution. Physics-constrained flow matching (PCFM) [Utkarsh et al., 2025] enforces constraints through forward-shooting and Gauss–Newton correction of predicted terminal states, which requires a dif-ferentiable constraint residual and access to its Jacobian. HardFlow [Li et al., 2026] enforces hard constraints by reformulating FM sampling as a trajectory-optimization problem, requiring an explicit constraint function and a tractable constrained-optimization solver during sampling. Doob-Oriented Inference-time Transformation (DOIT) [Zhu et al., 2026] improves constraint satisfaction by applying Doob’s h-transform to add a dynamic correction to the diffusion sampling process, which can handle non-differentiable reward feedback but requires Monte Carlo reward-query estimates during sampling. While inference-time methods avoid retraining, they typically require a nontrivial design of the guidance, correction, or optimization procedure and impose additional computational burden during sampling.

## H Experiment Details 

All experiments are run on an NVIDIA A100 GPU with 80 GB of memory. We implement standard FM using independent random coupling and linear interpolants, following Algorithm 2 (Simplified Conditional Flow Matching) in Tong et al. [2024]. The network architecture for each experiment is specified in the corresponding subsection. During FM training, the learning rate decays from 10 −4 to 10 −5 during training. During subsequent primal dual training, we use the settings specified below. 

H.1 Illustrative 2-D Constrained Generation ( Q(C) < b )Network architectures: The velocity field uθ (xt, t ) is parameterized by a fully connected network taking (xt, t ) ∈ R3 as input. It contains five hidden layers of width 64 with SELU activations and a two-dimensional linear output representing uθ (xt, t ) ∈ R2. The feasibility predictor fϕ(xt, t ) uses the same hidden architecture with a scalar output. A sigmoid function is applied to the output logit to obtain the predicted probability of terminal constraint satisfaction. 

Hyperparameters: learning rate for the velocity field η(θ) = 1 × 10 −5, learning rate for the classifier η(ϕ) = 1 × 10 −4, dual variable learning rate ηi = 500 . Large ηi is applied because a high constraint satisfaction target corresponds to a large optimal dual variable. A larger update step therefore accelerates convergence. Significance for chaotic PDE emulation. The Kuramoto–Sivashinsky setting is representative of generative emulation tasks for chaotic systems, where samples are drawn from the emergent chaotic attractor. High-quality emulation requires both local dynamical accuracy and statistical fidelity to that attractor. The two terms in the PDFM objective parallel this decomposition: the FM loss matches the attractor distribution, while the chance constraint enforces local consistency with the dynamics, providing a principled probabilistic alternative to the combination of next-step prediction error and statistical-fidelity losses common in deterministic emulators of chaotic systems [Jiang et al., 2023, Melo et al., 2026]. Existing works such as [Song et al., 2023] introduce molecule-specific architectural and training designs, including hybrid probability paths for coordinates x and atom features h, equivariant optimal transport for coordinates, Hungarian–Kabsch alignment, and information-aligned paths for atom features, achieving superior stability rates. Network architectures. Following the EGNN backbone of Satorras et al. [2021], each molecular state xt ∈ RN ×8 is represented as a graph with three coordinates and five atom features per node. For both FM and PDFM, the velocity network uses four time-conditioned EGNN blocks with hidden dimension 64 , where pairwise relative coordinates and distances are used to update node features and coordinates. A 64 -dimensional sinusoidal embedding of t conditions each block, and the network outputs uθ (xt, t ) ∈ RN ×8. The feasibility predictor uses a separate pairwise graph network with hidden dimension 128 , a 32 -dimensional time embedding, and two interaction blocks. Pairwise messages are constructed from node features, relative coordinates, and squared distances and are aggregated by averaging. The pooled graph representation and summary statistics of pairwise distances are passed through an MLP and a sigmoid function to produce fϕ(xt, t ) ∈ (0 , 1) .

I.2 Implementation Tips and Relationship with FM-RE 

From an implementation perspective, a single PDFM iteration with fixed λ′ = λ/w is closely related to FM-RE, if the dual variable in PDFM is interpreted as the weight of the constraint-aware term in FM-RE. Although the procedures for estimating ∇θ Puθ (C) differ, both methods share the same underlying idea: they provide feedback to uθ (xt, t ) that is explicitly or implicitly derived from the terminal binary feasibility feedback. Since FM-RE requires sampling complete trajectories to obtain terminal oracle feedback, a natural question is whether PDFM also requires complete trajectory sampling during its updates. Only partially. PDFM maintains both uθ and fϕ during the primal update. Training fϕ requires complete trajectory sampling, since the terminal oracle value y = 1C (x1) serves as the training label for the intermediate predictions fϕ(xt, t ). However, complete trajectory sampling is not strictly required to compute grad θ in (18), because the feedback is provided directly by the fixed estimator 

fϕ.One subtlety is that evaluating (18) still requires samples of the intermediate xi∆t. From an implemen-tation perspective, these states can be approximated using random couplings and linear interpolants, i.e., xi∆t = (1 − i∆t)x0 + i∆tx 1, provided that the probability path does not deviate significantly, as in the PDE-constrained and molecule generation tasks. Note that fϕ, which is fundamentally trained as a classifier, is generally cheaper to train than the more complex velocity field uθ and does not require highly accurate prediction to provide useful feedback. Since PDFM does not require complete trajectory sampling for the θ-update, it has a computational advantage over FM-RE. In single-iteration evaluations for i > 0, PDFM training is approximately 

2–3× faster than FM-RE; for example, the runtime is about 1.5 minutes vs. 4 minutes for the 8-D ℓ2

ball constraint, and 25 minutes vs. 70 minutes for Case 1 in Sec. 5.3. Users seeking faster training can repeat multiple θ-updates between the ϕ-updates, provided that these θ-updates do not substantially change the constraint satisfaction behavior. 

I.3 Limitations 

The limitations of PDFM are summarized as follows: 1. Since fϕ is fundamentally trained as a classifier, it requires both positive and negative feedback. Highly imbalanced feedback, especially when the feedback is overwhelmingly negative, meaning that most samples generated by the current uθ violate the constraint, can prevent fϕ from learning reliable decision boundaries and lead to inaccurate feedback. Therefore, at the start of iteration i = 1 in Alg. 2, PDFM requires the current velocity field to generate at least some feasible samples. 2. PDFM requires the feasible region to be sufficiently represented or reachable under the data and model distributions. This means one cannot generally expect PDFM to learn a near-optimal constrained distribution when Q deviates significantly from C.
