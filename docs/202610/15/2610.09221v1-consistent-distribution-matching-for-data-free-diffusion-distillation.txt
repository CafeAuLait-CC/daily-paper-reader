ABSTRACT
Flow and diffusion models suffer from slow inference due to computationally
expensive numerical integration. Distillation provides a promising way for a
student model to learn from a teacher’s dynamics, enabling one-step or few-step
generation. However, existing methods often depend on curated distillation datasets,
costly teacher rollouts, or auxiliary proxy networks, which complicate model
training and scaling. In this work, we propose Consistent Distribution Matching,
a simulation-free and data-free distillation method for accelerating diffusion and
flow models while preserving strong generative capacity. Our key insight is to
unify sample generation and score estimation with one student network. Thus,
our framework uses only two models, a frozen teacher and a trainable student,
and optimizes one objective. We prove that minimizing our objective indicates
Wasserstein convergence of the student flow-map pushforwards to the teacher
marginals. On ImageNet 256×256, our method attains an FID of 2.04 with a single
function evaluation (1-NFE) and a 4-NFE FID of 1.37 within 40 epochs of training,
surpassing the state-of-the-art distillation baselines without data. Our code code
and model are available at https://consistentdmd.github.io/.

INTRODUCTION
Diffusion and flow-based generative models (Albergo et al., 2023; Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al.,
2023; Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Song et al., 2021; Karras et al., 2022; Ho et al., 2020; Rombach
et al., 2022; Tong et al., 2025) have become a leading framework for high-fidelity image and video
synthesis. By learning a continuous transport from a simple prior distribution to a complex data
distribution, these models provide stable training and strong sample quality across a wide spectrum of
generative tasks (Fu et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023b; Li et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025;
Yan et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2025). Despite these advantages, their inference cost remains non-trivial.
The primary bottleneck lies in the iterative denoising procedure during inference. Generating a single
sample requires numerically solving the differential equations governing the generative dynamics,
which often involves hundreds of neural function evaluations.

To address this challenge, diffusion distillation (Geng et al., 2024; Song et al., 2023; Song & Dhariwal,
2023; Lu & Song, 2024; Meng et al., 2023; Frans et al., 2024; Sabour et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2025)
provides a natural route to accelerate the generation with a new model. Given a powerful teacher
diffuser, the student is trained to reproduce the teacher’s generative behavior while compressing the
inference process in one or few steps. Existing distillation methods have made substantial progress,
yet their training pipelines often introduce additional complexity. Some depend on heavy backward
simulation (Liu et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2026; Gu et al., 2023) of a teacher diffusion model, auxiliary
fake score networks (Yin et al., 2024b;a; Xie et al., 2024b; Zhou et al., 2024; 2025d; Luo et al., 2025;
Ge et al., 2026), adversarial objectives (Zhou et al., 2025d; Kim et al., 2023; Sauer et al., 2024; 2025),
or multi-stage training (Lee et al., 2024). Although these components enable high-quality generation
in few steps, they incur substantial memory overhead, impose an egregiously high optimization
burden, and ultimately hinder the scalability of the distillation procedure.
Beyond this procedural complexity, reliance on external distillation data (Yin et al., 2024b;a; Peng
et al., 2025; Song et al., 2023; Lu & Song, 2024; Meng et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024) introduces a
fundamental source of mismatch between data and teacher probability path. Data-based methods
construct intermediate states by noising real data samples, often drawn from a distillation dataset
that differs from the data used to train the teacher. Consequently, these states may deviate from those
actually visited by the teacher during generation, causing the student to be supervised on a surrogate
state distribution, instead of along the teacher’s probability path. This mismatch of the supervision
states (Tong et al., 2025) becomes pronounced when fine-tuning tilts the teacher’s distribution away
from the original data (Xie et al., 2024b; Chen et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025) or when the teacher
adopts classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2022) or autoguidance (Karras et al., 2024). In
these settings, noising samples from the original data distribution provides progressively less faithful
coverage of the states visited by the teacher. Moreover, when a teacher model is trained on proprietary
data (Rombach et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2025; Seedream et al., 2025; Labs, 2025), constructing such a
data-anchored supervision path may be infeasible. Direct supervision on teacher trajectories would
address the mismatch, but obtaining such states requires backward simulation, thereby reintroducing
the aforementioned computational burden. This trade-off motivates our central question: can we
distill a diffusion or flow teacher into a few-step student without external data, teacher rollouts, or
auxiliary fake score models?
We propose Consistent Distribution Matching Distillation (CDMD), a data-free and simulation-free
framework for distilling diffusion and flow models using only a frozen teacher and a trainable student.
Our framework aims to circumvent the distribution mismatch issues. Our central idea is to unify
2


sample generation and score estimation within a single flow map model, eliminating the need for a
separately trained proxy network. The student predicts average velocities over arbitrary denoising
time intervals, supporting generation in one or a few steps. We bootstrap intermediate training states
from student predictions and analytic forward noising, avoiding real data and teacher rollouts while
allowing the training distribution to evolve with the student.
Although these states do not necessarily coincide with those visited by the teacher during genera-
tion, our objective progressively aligns the corresponding student probability path with the teacher
probability path. This enables the teacher to provide corrective velocity supervision directly on states
relevant to the student’s generative dynamics. At these states, we regress the student velocity surrogate
toward the teacher’s instantaneous velocity through a single objective. Moreover, as the states become
noisier, they increasingly resemble the distributions encountered during teacher training, making
the teacher’s velocity supervision more reliable. This trend is consistently observed across existing
distribution matching baselines (Yin et al., 2024a;b). The quality of the supervision also improves as
the student distribution moves closers to the teacher distribution.
We further establish a theoretical connection between local transport consistency and distribution
matching. Under appropriate assumptions, minimizing population loss implies that the distributions
induced by the student flow map converge to the teacher marginals in squared Wasserstein-2 distance
averaged over time. Importantly, this result does not require the supervision states to be sampled
from the teacher’s probability path. Next, the minimizer of our objective becomes the teacher average
velocity. Overall, these results explain how local consistency can identify the teacher’s transport
behavior and achieve distributional alignment without auxiliary score networks.
Our contributions are threefold:
1. We introduce Consistent Distribution Matching Distillation, a data-free and simulation-free
distillation framework for diffusion and flow-based generative models. The method uses
only a frozen teacher and a trainable student, avoiding external distillation data, teacher
rollouts and auxiliary proxy networks.
2. We provide a distribution-matching analysis showing that minimizing the proposed CDMD
objective leads to narrowing the gap between the teacher distribution and the student
distribution under regularity assumptions. The analysis also identifies the teacher’s average
velocity field as the unique global minimizer for our objective.
3. We analyze the variance of the JVP correction in the data-free setting and validate the
analysis on synthetic datasets. These results clarify when the correction improves stability
and when student-teacher path mismatch can degrade training. On ImageNet256×256,
Consistent Distribution Matching Distillation achieves a 1-NFE FID of 2.04 and a 4-NFE
FID of 1.37, achieving state-of-the-art performance among data-free distillation methods.
In the text-to-image comparison when training data is inaccessible, our method leads most
preference and alignment metrics and FID.

Diffusion and Flow Matching.
Let p0 denote the data distribution and p1 a prior distribution.
For each x0 ∼p0 and x1 ∼p1, Flow Matching (FM) (Albergo et al., 2023; Lipman et al., 2023;

CONSISTENT DISTRIBUTION MATCHING DISTILLATION
This section develops Consistent Distribution Matching Distillation as a distillation method without
external data or teacher simulation, as illustrated in Fig. 3. We first motivate supervision on states
bootstrapped from the student model. Beyond the tradition KL divergence, we develop Consistent
Distribution Matching from a Fisher-divergence criterion using a velocity surrogate derived from the
student network. Finally, we relate the resulting consistency residual to distribution matching under
regularity assumptions.

3.1
MINIMAL DESIGN ON DATA-FREE DISTILLATION
Current distillation methods require either online training with external data or expensive offline
generation of teacher samples. In the former setting, training states are drawn from a forward FM
path ˜pt, while the teacher follows its own probability path pt. Since ˜pt̸ = pt in general, the student
is supervised on a mismatched state distribution. Training directly on pt avoids this mismatch, but
sampling intermediate teacher states requires simulating the teacher dynamics, introducing substantial
computational overhead.
Unifying proxy model and student generator. Motivated by SiD (Zhou et al., 2024), we therefore
adopt a data-free construction based on the student rollout. However, evaluating this objective
typically introduces an auxiliary model to estimate the student’s marginal velocity, which violates our
design principle of involving only the teacher and student. To eliminate this extra model, the student
must be capable of both generating clean samples and providing a surrogate for its own marginal
velocity within a unified framework.

Setup. On ImageNet 256×256, we distill three teachers: SiT-XL/2 (Ma et al., 2024), SiT-XL/2 trained
with VA-REPA (Yang et al., 2026), and LightningDiT-XL/2 with a VA-VAE autoencoder (Yao et al.,
2025). The student uses the teacher architecture, with extra embeddings for r and w (Appendix D).
We report FID-50K and IS. All baselines use the same training budget and the same evaluation
pipeline. For text-to-image, we follow the protocol of MFD (Zhao et al., 2026) with a SANA-1.6B
teacher (Xie et al., 2024a). Full hyperparameters are listed in Tab. 5.
Synthetic benchmark. We first use a 2D spiral, where both the target distribution and the ground-
truth average velocity are known. The exact velocity field serves as the teacher. This removes the
effect of a learned teacher checkpoint and of image metrics. We track two quantities during training
(Fig. 5). First, CDMD and FreeFlow reduce the endpoint W2 distance at the same rate. Endpoint
matching therefore cannot distinguish the two methods. Second, only CDMD keeps reducing the
teacher average-velocity MSE, while FreeFlow plateaus at a much higher value. This suggests that
matching the endpoint marginal does not imply learning the teacher transport map. The full metrics
in Tab. 6 show the same pattern. Marginal Wasserstein scores are tied, but CDMD has lower coupled
endpoint error and lower teacher average-velocity error. Appendix D provides the full setup. In Fig. 5
(c), we observe that JVP yields the most stable behavior when computed in the teacher direction,
even on student-bootstrapped states.
ImageNet 256×256. Tab. 1 groups data-free methods by teacher under matched training budgets.
CDMD achieves the best 1-NFE FID across all three teachers: 3.47 with SiT-XL/2, 2.93 with SiT-
XL/2+VA-REPA, and 2.04 with LightningDiT-XL/2. It also benefits more from stronger teachers:
replacing SiT-XL/2 with VA-REPA improves CDMD by 0.54 FID, compared with 0.41 for FreeFlow
and 0.07 for DMD2, while MeanFlow degrades. Without retraining, the same student further improves
with additional NFEs, reaching FIDs of 2.60 with VA-REPA and 1.62 with LightningDiT-XL/2 at
2 NFEs, and 1.37 at 4 NFEs, close to the teacher’s 1.35. We omit FreeFlow without DMD in the
LightningDiT block because it fails to converge within the training budget. Baselines marked with ⋆
are one-step methods that we adapt to the data-free setting. Specifically, we construct training states
by noising clean samples from the current student generator, corresponding to the s = 0 case of
CDMD. Notice that, these adaptations remain worse comparing to CDMD.

Inference-time scaling. FreeFlow (Tong et al., 2025) is anchored to the prior. Its student only takes
noise at t = 1, so it cannot be applied over sub-intervals. In contrast, CDMD learns uθ(xt, t, r) for
any interval, so extra steps are available at inference time. Fig. 6 (a) shows that the benefit depends on
guidance. With w = 1.5, FID keeps improving with more steps and approaches the dashed reference.
With w = 2.0, FID is best at very few steps and worsens as steps increase. This suggests that
strong guidance helps one-step generation but amplifies the student–teacher mismatch over multi-step
sampling. Fig. 11 in the appendix shows that the gain from extra steps also depends on the sampler.
Training-state construction. Alg. 1 samples m ∈[0, 1] to linearly interpolate the two states in
Eq. (6): larger m emphasizes one-step student samples, while smaller m emphasizes shared-noise
interval states. Tab. 2 shows that intermediate values work best, with the lowest FID at m = 0.5 and
highest IS at m = 0.875, while convergence speed remains similar. Fig. 6b shows a related trade-off
for the fraction of t = r pairs: 10% improves 1-NFE FID but degrades multi-step sampling, whereas
50% yields more stable few-step performance. This suggests that sufficient exposure to local intervals
is important for multi-step sampling.
Text-to-image generation. We distill SANA-1.6B (Xie et al., 2024a), a flow-matching text-to-
image model. The teacher and student are initialized from the same checkpoint. We follow the
protocol of MFD (Zhao et al., 2026), training and evaluating on LAION-Aesthetics 6.5+ (Schuhmann
et al., 2022). We report Aesthetic Score, PickScore (Kirstain et al., 2023), HPSv2 (Wu et al., 2023a),
ImageReward (Xu et al., 2023), CLIPScore (Hessel et al., 2021), and FID against COCO-5k 2017 (Lin
et al., 2014).
Tab. 3 gives three findings. First, among learned 4-NFE methods, CDMD has the best Aesthetic
Score, PickScore (PS), and ImageReward (IR). Its FID of 27.83 is also below MFD’s 28.77. Second,
CDMD beats the teacher run at 4 NFEs on all six metrics, and it beats the 20-NFE teacher on
Aesthetic Score, IR, and FID. Third, CDMD trails on HPSv2 and CLIPScore, where four and five of
the six baselines score higher, respectively. These results show that CDMD works in one large-scale
text-to-image setting. They do not establish statistical superiority. CDMD is also cheap to train.
It uses about 7 GPU hours, compared with 22 for Diff-Instruct, 25 for MFD, and 30 for DMD2
(Tab. 7). Only consistency distillation is cheaper (∼4 GPU hours), yet it scores lower on all five point
metrics. All costs are measured on one NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPU, the same setup as the baseline
measurements of MFD (Zhao et al., 2026).

RELATED WORK
Trajectory Distillation. Distillation accelerates diffusion sampling by training students to reproduce
the teacher’s dynamics in fewer steps. Knowledge distillation (Zheng et al., 2023) learns a direct
mapping from noise to data, while progressive distillation (Salimans & Ho, 2022; Meng et al., 2023)
repeatedly halves the number of sampling steps. Consistency models (Lu & Song, 2024; Song &
Dhariwal, 2023; Song et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2023a) map states along the same ODE trajectory to a
shared clean sample. Flow map distillation (Boffi et al., 2025; Geng et al., 2025a;b; Sabour et al.,
2025; Lee et al., 2026) extends this idea to transitions between arbitrary times along a trajectory.
BOOT (Gu et al., 2023), π-Flow (Chen et al., 2025), and rectified flow (Liu et al., 2023) explore
9


ways to reduce reliance on real training data. For methods that require teacher reference trajectories,
generating them during training or precomputing them can limit scalability as tasks become more
complex. Our method avoids numerically integrating the teacher during training. Compared with
FreeFlow (Tong et al., 2025), which anchors its flow map at noise, our model supports transitions
from arbitrary input states and times.
6
CONCLUSION
We presented CDMD, a data-free and simulation-free framework for distilling pretrained diffusion and
flow-based teachers into efficient one-step or few-step flow map students. Our method eliminates the
need for external distillation data, teacher rollouts, backward simulation, and auxiliary proxy networks
by training on a student-induced distribution with a single objective. Motivated by the velocity form
Theoretically, we showed that, under regularity assumptions, minimizing our CDMD objective leads
to convergence of student sampling distributions to teacher distributions in Wasserstein distance,
averaged over reference times, and every global minimizer recovers the teacher average velocity on
sampled states. Empirically, CDMD achieves state-of-the-art ImageNet256×256 generation quality
among data-free distillation methods and further benefits from inference-time scaling through its
parameterization. Our method also achieves the best overall performance in text-to-image generation,
leading on both preference and alignment metrics as well as image-quality metrics. These results
suggest that strong data-free distillation can be achieved with a simpler two-model training pipeline,
while preserving the flexibility of multi-step sampling. Future work includes improving the stability of
the JVP term, developing initialization strategies when no model weights are available, and extending
the framework to large-scale generative tasks where data scarcity problem dominates.

RELATIONSHIP WITH PRIOR METHODS
Tab. 4 compares representative distillation methods by the number of networks and optimizers they
need, and by whether they require real data, trajectory simulation, or an adversarial objective. DMD2,
SiD-LSG, SiDA, FreeFlow, and CTM train a third auxiliary network, either a fake-score model or a
discriminator, with a second optimizer. BOOT is data-free but simulates trajectories during training.
FACM and the flow map models (MF, CM, IMM) use only two networks and one optimizer, but they
construct noisy states from real data. CDMD is the only method in the table that is both data-free and
simulation-free while keeping only the teacher, the student, and a single optimizer. The subsections
below relate CDMD to FreeFlow, flow maps, and DMD in more detail.

supervision over arbitrary timestep pairs, but confines training to trajectories initialized at t = 1. As a
result, the student is optimized primarily as a one-step noise-to-data sampler, rather than as a general
flow map capable of modeling denoising transitions between arbitrary timesteps (t, r) with t > r.
Consequently, it reduces training complexity at the cost of limiting inference-time scaling and not
naturally preserving the multi-step scaling behavior of the original flow model.
In the notation of FreeFlow (Tong et al., 2025), Fθ denotes the forward-time velocity, whereas our u(·)
denotes the corresponding backward-time velocity. Therefore, the two are related by Fθ = −u(·).
Beyond this notational difference, FreeFlow relies on pseudo-gradients propagated through student-
generated trajectories, typically over intervals such as [0, 1] or [t, 1]. Consequently, its updates depend
on the quality of the intermediate student states ˆxt, which may introduce additional sensitivity during
training. In contrast, our method avoids this rollout dependence by providing supervision directly on
arbitrary local intervals [r, t]. This enables training across the denoising process without explicitly
simulating intermediate student trajectories.

IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
This section gives the training, architecture, and evaluation details for class-conditional ImageNet
(Appendix D.1), the analytic 2D benchmarks behind Fig. 5 (Appendix D.2), and the SANA-1.6B

D.1
CLASS-CONDITIONAL IMAGENET
Training-state construction.
Fig. 7(a) shows the two data-free constructions of ˆxt in Eq. (6). In the
upper construction (s = t), the student jumps from the prior sample x1 directly to each intermediate
time, e.g., ˆxt = fθ(x1, 1, t), so the training state lies on the student path. In the lower construction
(s = 0), the student first produces a clean sample ˆx0 = fθ(x1, 1, 0), which is then re-noised along
the conditional flow path with fresh Gaussian noise. Each construction costs one student evaluation
and no teacher rollout. Fig. 7(b) shows the supervision on such a state: the student average velocity
uθ(ˆxt, t, r) defines the jump ˆxt −fθ(ˆxt, t, r) over [r, t], and its corrected velocity is regressed onto
the teacher velocity v(ˆxt, t). In Alg. 1, z1 and z2 are the s = 0 and s = t states, respectively. They
are mixed with ratio m (Tab. 2) and detached before the teacher is queried.
Training.
The backbone architectures follow SiT, which is based on DiT (Peebles & Xie, 2023)
with adaLN-Zero (Peebles & Xie, 2023) for conditioning. To condition the student model on the
additional variables, we use a two-layer SiLU-activated MLP whose hidden dimension matches
the model’s hidden dimension. We add new embeddings for r and w, and adopt adaLN-Zero
for conditioning. The resulting conditioning signal is fed to each transformer block. No further
architectural changes are necessary for stable and effective training. Note that SiT teacher and
SiT+VA-REPA have different configurations on what the time in FM represents. SiT adopts the
convention when x1 ∼pdata, x0 ∼pprior which is the opposite of the notion we adopt in this paper.
Our convention on FM time aligns with SiT+VA-REPA (Yang et al., 2026) or SiT+REPA (Yu
et al., 2024) in their implementation. Our training algorithm strictly follows 1. In addition, we
use Flash-Attention to reduce GPU memory usage and accelerate training. Plus, we customize the
Flash-Attention kernel to support JVP computation, following sCM (Lu & Song, 2024).
All models are trained using the PyTorch framework. The detailed training hyperparameters are
provided in Tab. 5.
Architecture.
Inspired by DMF (Lee et al., 2026), we investigate delaying endpoint conditioning
to the later DiT blocks, as illustrated in Fig. 13. It is important to note that our architecture also
differs from DMF (Lee et al., 2026): rather than decoupling the t and r signals, we preserve the
original time-conditioning branch throughout the network and inject the additional endpoint signal
only into the later DiT blocks. We find this design to be more effective for data-free distillation.
Empirically, we find that decoupling strategy from DMF can accelerate FID convergence during early
training, but does not improve the final performance and can occasionally degrade it. We thereby

D.3
TEXT-TO-IMAGE DATASET
To adapt our method on text-to-image dataset at a larger scale, some aspects need to be altered. First,
we need to anchor on the teacher trajectory to prevent drifting effect. Similar to the baselines, we
use 4 step during our inference. To control the noise added during training, we adopt the TDM (Luo
et al., 2025) method in our data-free setting. Every time we construct the intermediate states we
start with the nearest earlier teacher checkpoint and we add noise according to the forward path. We
decrease the variance of our target which leads to a better final performance. Fig. 10 illustrates this
construction. The student generator first moves the prior sample backward along a coarse schedule,
x1 →ˆxs →ˆxr →ˆx0, and caches every intermediate state. A training state at time t is then obtained
by re-noising the nearest cached state that is cleaner than t along the forward conditional path: ˆx0
for t ∈[0, r), ˆxr for t ∈[r, s), and ˆxs for t ∈[s, 1]. Each re-noising step therefore covers a short
interval, so the training states stay close to the 4-step trajectory used at inference. In this case, we
observe more diversed background and better detailed being preserved from the teacher model. With
the same hardware and matching configuration, our CDMD requires approximately 7 GPU hours
and 18 GB of peak memory (Tab. 7). Its cost is higher than CD but lower than the reported costs
of Diff-Instruct, DMD2, and MFD. Note that our method performs significantly better than CD
according to Table 3.

ABLATION ON ENDPOINT CONDITIONING
Fig. 13 compares how three transformer designs receive time information. (a) The DiT teacher
predicts the instantaneous velocity and conditions every block on the embeddings of t and the class
label y through adaLN-Zero. (b) The MeanFlow transformer predicts the average velocity and adds
an embedding of the second time variable to the same signal, so every block sees both time variables.
(c) The CDMD transformer keeps the teacher conditioning for the all blocks and adds the endpoint
embedding, implemented as the gap t −r, only to the last L −D blocks and the final layer.
Tab. 8 varies D for the L = 28 student under a fixed budget of 25K iterations. At depth D, the first
D blocks see only the teacher conditioning, and D = 0 conditions every block on (t −r), as in
MeanFlow. Conditioning every block on the gap (D = 0) gives an FID of 17.60, far worse than any
delayed variant. Delaying the signal by five blocks already lowers FID to 2.80. All depths from 5 to
20 reach FID at most 2.80 and IS above 273, and D = 8 performs best with FID 2.21 and IS 284.46.
Delaying the signal too far (D = 25) raises FID to 3.84, since only three blocks remain to separate
different intervals that start from the same (xt, t).
We interpret this effect through teacher capacity. The first D blocks receive exactly the conditioning
of the teacher, so they can reproduce the teacher features for the instantaneous velocity at (xt, t),
which every interval [r, t] shares. The endpoint signal then only modulates the later blocks, which
turn these shared features into the average velocity of a specific interval. With D = 0, every block
must handle both time variables from the start, and the student spends capacity on the endpoint
before it reproduces the teacher. This matches our finding on the t and r schedule in Appendix F.2:
the conditioning depth and the fraction of t = r pairs both control how much of the student stays
dedicated to the teacher velocity, and preserving this teacher capacity is what keeps the distilled
student close to the teacher.

ABLATION ON INFERENCE-TIME SCALING
F.1
SAMPLER COMPARISON WITH THE SIT-XL/2+VA-REPA TEACHER
We further study inference-time scaling by comparing Euler, Heun, and DDIM samplers under
different classifier-free guidance scales. Figure 11 reports FID as a function of the number of
sampling steps for w = 1.5 and w = 2.0. In some literatures (Lee et al., 2026; Song et al., 2023),
DDIM method has also been referred as the Restart sampler.
The ablation suggests that increasing the inference-time sampling budget does not automatically
improve our distilled model. DDIM gives the strongest low-step performance in both guidance
settings and remains especially stable from one to four steps. Euler is competitive at one or two steps
for w = 1.5, but its FID increases as more steps are used and then plateaus. Heun is the least stable
in the low-step regime: it exhibits a sharp FID spike at two steps and only recovers after additional
steps. The effect is more pronounced under stronger guidance, where both Euler and Heun drift to
noticeably worse FID than DDIM as the sampling budget grows.
30


These results give useful guidance for scaling inference time in our model. The interval-conditioned
parameterization supports multi-step sampling, but the benefit depends strongly on the sampler and
guidance scale. In this setting, DDIM is the most reliable choice for converting extra inference steps
into stable sample quality, while higher-order or updates can amplify student-teacher mismatch at
small step counts. We attribute this behavior primarily to these two factors. First, our training schedule
assigns substantial probability mass to intervals with t̸ = r, which may make the learned update
less compatible with aggressive higher-order extrapolation at inference time. Second, the quality
of the teacher, particularly its performance without CFG, has a pronounced effect on the distilled
model. Empirically, we observe that differences in teacher performance without CFG translate into
differences in the final student performance. Note that this student is trained with t = r for only 10%
of the pairs (Tab. 5). Appendix F.2 tests the first factor with a student trained with t = r for 50% of
the pairs.
F.2
INFERENCE-TIME SCALING WITH THE LIGHTNINGDIT-XL/2 TEACHER
Fig. 12 repeats the inference-time scaling study for the student distilled from LightningDiT-XL/2.
We sample the same student with Euler and DDIM from 1 to 64 steps without retraining. Unlike
the SiT-XL/2+VA-REPA student in Fig. 11, the Euler FID decreases from 2.04 at 1 step to 1.62 at 2
steps and 1.37 at 4 steps (Tab. 1), and reaches its minimum at 8 steps. From 8 to 64 steps, the Euler
FID stays nearly flat and close to the teacher reference instead of drifting upward. The Euler IS rises
from about 294 at 1 step to about 320 at 16 steps and then saturates. Additional steps thus refine
the samples, as they do for the teacher FM model, and never cause the large degradation of the 10%
t = r student.
The main difference between the two students is the training schedule. The LightningDiT student
samples t = r for 50% of the pairs, whereas the SiT-XL/2+VA-REPA student in Fig. 11 uses 10%
(Tab. 5). On t = r pairs, the objective reduces to ∥uθ(ˆxt, t, t) −v(ˆxt, t)∥2, so the student directly
learns the teacher instantaneous velocity, which governs small sampling steps. This result matches
Fig. 6b in the main text, where raising the fraction of t = r pairs from 10% to 50% substantially
reduces the multi-step degradation (FID about 5.1 instead of 9.8 at 50 steps). Since the same behavior
appears with a different teacher, the stability is a property of the t and r schedule rather than of a
particular teacher: keeping t = r for 50% of the pairs is enough to preserve the multi-step behavior
of the teacher as the number of sampling steps increases, while still giving the best 1-NFE FID in
Tab. 1.
DDIM behaves differently. Its FID is close to Euler at 1 and 2 steps but rises steadily after 4 steps
and exceeds 5 at 64 steps, while its IS grows monotonically to above 420. Rising IS together with
rising FID indicates that DDIM increasingly trades sample diversity for class fidelity as the number
of steps grows. For this student, Euler is therefore the preferred sampler for inference-time scaling.

CDMD WITH GUIDANCE
Classifier-free guidance (CFG) (Ho & Salimans, 2022) is significant to class-conditional image gener-
ation because it facilitates conditional generation by extrapolating from unconditional to conditional
predictions. Since the most pre-trained diffusion baselines are evaluated with CFG, we formulate
our distillation objective to match the guided teacher dynamics rather than the unguided velocity
field alone. Let v(xt, t | c) and v(xt, t | ∅) denote the conditional and unconditional teacher
velocity fields, respectively. For a guidance scale w ≥1, the guided velocity field is defined as
vcfg(xt, t | c) = (1 −w) v(xt, t | ∅) + w v(xt, t | c).
In addition, we can reinforce this signal by incorporating student guidance with scale α ≥0 during
training to support 1-NFE sampling (Geng et al., 2025a). Compared with standard CFG sampling,
which evaluates both conditional and unconditional branches at each solver step and therefore doubles
the NFE count, this training-time guided target lets the distilled student absorb part of the conditional
guidance signal before inference. Specifically, we define the enhanced guided velocity field as
follows:

LIMITATIONS AND BROADER IMPACT
A key limitation of the current formulation is the absence of meaningful constraints on the total
derivative term d
dtu(xt, t, r) = v(xt, t)Dxtu+∂tu. To date, this component has not been rigorously
analyzed. In prior work, it is typically detached from the computational graph and excluded from
backpropagation, effectively treating it as a constant. However, empirical observations reveal that
its norm is large and fluctuates substantially throughout training, suggesting that this simplification
may be problematic. A plausible direction for regularization is to leverage a well-trained reference
velocity field v(xt, t), encouraging the derivative term to exhibit similar, more stable behavior. In
practice, not only is the dataset private, but the teacher model weights are also inaccessible. Hence,
we cannot initialize the student model with the teacher model weights, which is a common practice
in distillation work. This makes training more challenging and less stable. Moreover, we speculate
that for different tasks, the way we bootstrapped our xt is of great importance. This can substantially
influence the learning dynamics and overall effectiveness of distillation.
