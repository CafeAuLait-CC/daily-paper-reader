Abstract 

Flow Matching has recently become popular as a technique for generative modeling with applica-tions in many domains. In the standard Flow Matching formulation, we interpret the vector field as the solution of a least-squares regression problem, which is known to be sensitive to contamination. Therefore we leverage the viewpoint of robust statistics and replace the squared Euclidean distance with the Euclidean distance as a regression objective. This seemingly minor change strongly affects the properties of Flow Matching. We provide a probabilistic interpretation of the new regression tar-get as a conditional geometric median and use this characterization to study distributional robustness of the Euclidean Distance-based Flow Matching. We analyze the mathematical properties of this changed objective and the effect of a contaminated target distribution on the involved conditional velocity distributions. We discuss the challenge of directly characterizing the distribution that our method induces. In specific contamination settings we show theoretically that our method yields an improvement for severe contamination. Finally, we present numerical experiments conducted on the CIFAR-10 image dataset where a proportion of the samples has been contaminated. Our Euclidean distance-based Flow Matching shows a clear advantage in scenarios of severe contamination of the target distribution. 

# 1 Introduction 

Flow Matching (FM) (Lipman et al., 2023, 2024; Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden, 2023; Liu et al., 2023) is a framework for training a flow model (Chen et al., 2018; Grathwohl et al., 2019) to generate samples from a target distribution that is available only through data. Starting from a known source distribution from which we can sample, FM learns a time-dependent vector field that drives the generation process. New samples are generated by drawing from the source distribution and applying the flow induced by this learned time-dependent vector field. It is well known that under suitable regularity assumptions, the (population) optimal vector field for the standard formulation generates the prescribed target distribution exactly. Real-life datasets often do not only contain samples from the assumed true distribution, but they contain contaminated samples as well. A classical example is the Belgium telephone calls dataset (Rousseeuw and Leroy, 1987) that includes several wrongly recorded data points. Therefore we model the observed distribution as a mixture model of the clean and a contaminating distribution, rather than only the target distribution. Consequently, robustness is the desirable property that the learned distribution stays close to the clean distribution, even in the presence of contamination without manually filtering the samples. Flow Matching is a powerful technique to produce samples from the target, but suffers from replication of the exact target including the contamination. We present a remedy for this by a simple modification of the Flow Matching objective. Our approach replaces the squared Euclidean distance loss in the standard Flow Matching objective with the Euclidean distance loss. The latter penalizes large regression residuals linearly rather than quadratically, motivating its use to reduce sensitivity to contamination. At the population level, the modified field is a geometric median of the conditional velocity distribution, whereas

Finally, we evaluate both objectives on contaminated CIFAR-10 data, comparing their generation quality across different contamination models and proportions. 

# 1.1 Related Work 

Several works focus on improving Flow Matching by modifying the coupling between source and tar-get samples, for instance Pooladian et al. (2023) and Tong et al. (2024a). Repeated rectification also straightens trajectories (Liu et al., 2023). These approaches modify the coupling or transport path while retaining the standard squared regression objective. Song et al. (2025) consider a related alter-native objective in visuomotor learning. In their main method, they reformulate velocity prediction as terminal-sample prediction and train the resulting model using a componentwise absolute-deviation loss together with a two-step sampling procedure, although they also test their loss with conventional flow integration. They empirically demonstrate faster convergence on visuomotor-learning benchmarks. We use Euclidean-distance based loss, which is invariant under orthogonal changes of coordinates and yields a conditional geometric median rather than coordinatewise conditional medians. Moreover, our focus is distributional robustness. Robustness was recently studied in the closely related setting of diffusion models and score matching. Khrapov et al. (2024) replace the standard squared denoising objective with a scheduled pseudo-Huber loss with a time-varying threshold. They empirically demonstrate improved resistance to contaminated training data in both image and speech generation tasks. Similarly Li and Dong (2026) study replacing the mean squared error denoising loss with transformations induced by f-divergences. Their framework includes bounded-influence choices that downweight samples with large residuals and empirically im-prove generation under contamination. In the distinct setting of parametric score matching, Schwank et al. (2025) construct a robust estimator through geometric median of means aggregation and establish theoretical guarantees with particular attention to exponential-family models. Our approach differs in that the geometric median is not introduced as an external aggregation procedure but instead arises directly as the population-optimal regression target of our modified objective.

# 4 Robustness analysis 

In this section, we study how contamination of the target distribution affects the population vector fields of both the squared and the L2,1 method. The robustness of the geometric median has been assessed in various works (Small, 1990). It also appears in more recent works, as an aggregate estimator (Minsker, 2015; Minsker and Strawn, 2024). However, in our Flow Matching setting the geometric median is only applied on a conditional distribution at every time and space position to yield a vector field, whose integration in turn yields the generative model. Therefore the robustness properties are highly non-trivial. We separate our analysis into two levels. First, we study how target contamination affects the conditional velocity distributions and the corresponding population vector fields. Second, we investigate whether this robustness can translate into a generated distribution that is closer to the clean target. We consider a Huber contamination model (Huber, 1964), i.e. we assume that the true target distribution is P and a proportion of ϵ observations are instead replaced with a contaminating distribution Q:

P cont = (1 − ϵ)P + ϵQ, 0 ≤ ϵ < 1/2.

A crucial property is given by the geometric median’s so-called high finite-sample breakdown point. That is the smallest proportion of observations that must be replaced by arbitrary values to make an estimator arbitrarily large or otherwise uninformative. Its finite-sample replacement breakdown point is BGM = ⌊(N + 1) /2⌋/N (Lopuhaä and Rousseeuw, 1991). This means that if a proportion smaller than BGM of the N observations is replaced, then the displacement of the geometric median is bounded, regardless of the strength of the contamination. In contrast, replacing even a single observation by a point arbitrarily far from the rest of the data can cause an arbitrarily large displacement of the sample mean. This property motivates our use of the geometric median as a velocity estimator, as it remains stable under a substantial proportion of arbitrarily corrupted observations. However, this property does not mean that the geometric median yields better results in all contamination settings. In particular, the mean of a contaminated distribution may be closer to the clean mean than the geometric median.

We compare classical Flow Matching with our proposed formulation under clean and contaminated distributions. Specifically, we investigate how our L2,1-Flow Matching retains the performance of classical Flow Matching on clean data, whether it is more robust under contamination, and whether it depends on the proportion and type of contamination. The clean-target dataset we use is CIFAR-10 (Krizhevsky, 2009) and we consider several contamination mechanisms. Li and Dong (2026) perform similar studies on CIFAR-10 as we do and we adopt several contamination mechanisms used in their experiments. # 5.3 Discussion and Limitations 

The results on clean data suggest that in this particular model, the modified Flow Matching objective can preserve generation quality. Lemma 3.2 gives a sufficient condition for this to hold. However, we cannot verify its assumptions for the CIFAR-10 distribution, although the empirical observations are consistent with it. The pattern for the contaminated models suggests that the L2,1-Flow Matching method is effective for certain kinds of contamination. In particular, in the models under which the L2,1 method performs better, contamination consists of strong perturbations of pixels, individually or across the whole image. We suspect that these contamination models result in substantial deviations from the clean image dis-tribution, under which the L2,1 objective is beneficial. The standard method performs better for both Gaussian blur and Motion blur. A possible explanation is that the blurring we applied produces a con-tamination that is less separated from the original distribution. This hypothesis is partially supported by Theorem 4.3. We showed that under the stated assumptions, for bounded clean data with increasingly distant Dirac contamination, eventually the L2,1 method will yield a better result, in the Wasserstein metric. Moreover, this resembles the intuition that the geometric median is generally more useful for contamination that is strongly separated from the original data. Nevertheless, we should be careful with this intuition. The intuition regarding the geometric median applies only partially, because the velocity distributions Law( X1 − X0|Xt = x), which are the basis of the geometric median, have different mixture weights than the contaminated target distributions.

# 6 Conclusion 

In this work we proposed an adaptation of the Flow Matching method, obtained by replacing the re-gression objective with an L2,1 objective. We gave a characterization of the solution as a conditional geometric median and established conditions under which this changed objective yields the same result as the standard method. We then investigated the effect that contamination has on the vector fields and provided bounds on the influence of contamination. We have established improvement in the Wasserstein distance for our method in specific contamination settings under stated assumptions. Empirically, the method achieves comparable performance on clean CIFAR-10 and improves generation quality under several, though not all, contamination mechanisms. We do not establish general regularity or uniqueness results for trajectories induced by the L2,1 formulation. Moreover, pointwise robustness of the velocity field does not by itself guarantee that the generated distribution is closer to the clean target. Extend-ing our distributional guarantees beyond the specific contamination settings that we analyzed therefore remains an important direction for future research.

Our implementation is based on the CIFAR-10 code released in the TorchCFM repository accompanying Tong et al. (2024a,b). We use their CIFAR-10 I-CFM configuration and retain its U-Net architecture, data preprocessing, and principal training configuration. We modify the regression objective in the L2,1

experiments and introduce the contamination mechanisms described in Table 1. CIFAR-10 images are converted to tensors, and normalized channelwise with mean and standard deviation 0 .5, yielding images in [ −1, 1] 3×32 ×32 . No smoothing is applied to the Euclidean norm. Since the dimension-dependent normalization factors are constant they do not change the population minimizers, but they can affect gradient clipping and are therefore reported as used in training. For each of the contamination experiments, the contamination is generated online: every target image occurring in a minibatch is independently corrupted with probability 

ϵ. Whether an image gets corrupted and associated noise are generated using a separate seeded random-number generator, so that matching randomization is used across objectives. We use a U-Net with 128 base channels, channel multipliers [1 , 2, 2, 2], attention at resolution 16, dropout 0 .1, four attention heads and 64 channels per head. Models are trained for 400,000 optimizer updates using Adam with learning rate 2 · 10 −4, batch size 128, 5,000 linear warm-up steps, global gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, and EMA decay 0 .9999. For FID evaluation, we use the CIFAR-10 evaluation script provided in the TorchCFM repository ac-companying Tong et al. (2024a,b), with only the modifications required to load and evaluate our trained models.
