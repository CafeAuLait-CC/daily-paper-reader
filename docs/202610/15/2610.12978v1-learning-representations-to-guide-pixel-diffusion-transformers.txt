Abstract
Visual representations are largely developed for understanding, yet what makes them use-
ful for generation remains unclear. We study this question in pixel diffusion transformers
and uncover two key insights. First, better representation quality does not necessarily
translate into greater generative utility, as externally aligned semantics can be progres-
sively lost during pixel synthesis. Second, architectural separation with specialized pixel
decoders mainly redistributes semantic and spatial modeling, without necessarily yield-
ing representations better suited to generation. These insights suggest that a useful gen-
erative representation must be both properly formed and actively exploited. Motivated
by these insights, we introduce Representation Guidance (RG), which directly forms
and exploits internal representations while retaining a plain Transformer architecture. A
low-dimensional bottleneck forces generation-relevant information into a compact rep-
resentation, which is then explicitly used to guide decoder generation by modifying the
flow-matching target according to its induced prediction offset. RG learns clearer se-
mantic and spatial structure while scaling efficiently in both parameters and computation.
On ImageNet 256 × 256, RG-L/16 with only 459M parameters outperforms substantially
larger pixel diffusion transformers, while RG-XL/16 achieves an FID of 1.55. RG further
reaches an FID of 1.74 at 512 × 512 and extends to text-to-image generation, achieving
0.89 on GenEval and 87.1 on DPG-Bench.

1 Introduction
Representation learning has been a central theme in computer vision (Chen et al., 2020; He et al.,
2020; Caron et al., 2021; He et al., 2022). By transforming pixels into structured features, modern
vision models have learned representations that transfer effectively across recognition tasks such as
classification, detection, and segmentation (Oquab et al., 2023; Siméoni et al., 2025). Yet visual
representations have been developed predominantly for understanding. Whether the same notion of
a “good representation” applies to generation remains much less clear. Unlike recognition, which
can discard much of the low-level image content once semantics are extracted, generation must
organize global semantics and spatial structure while ultimately recovering every pixel. This raises
a fundamental question: what makes a representation useful for visual generation?
Recent generative models have increasingly borrowed representations from pretrained vision en-
coders. One line of work improves the latent space in which generation operates, either by aligning
autoencoder latents with pretrained features (Yao et al., 2025; Leng et al., 2025; Yue et al., 2026) or
by directly using such features as generative latents (Zheng et al., 2026a; Shi et al., 2026b; Singh
et al., 2026). Another line aligns intermediate generative features with pretrained representations (Yu
et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b; Singh et al., 2025). While these approaches show
that semantic representations can facilitate generative learning, they largely focus on what informa-
tion a representation contains. For generation, this is only part of the problem: a representation may
encode rich semantic and spatial structure, yet provide limited benefit if it is not properly formed for
synthesis or if the generative process does not actually exploit it.
We study this problem in pixel diffusion transformers (pDiTs) (Chen et al., 2025c; Yu et al., 2026),
where representation learning and image synthesis take place end-to-end from raw pixels rather than
through a representation space predefined by an external autoencoder (Rombach et al., 2022). More
importantly, JiT (Li & He, 2026) shows that competitive pixel generation can be achieved with a
plain Transformer, providing a simple setting for examining how internal representations emerge
and interact with pixel synthesis. Our analysis of this setting reveals two key insights.
• First, better representation quality does not necessarily translate into greater generative util-
ity. Aligning an intermediate JiT layer with DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2023) substantially improves
its semantic and spatial structure at the aligned layer. However, these gains rapidly diminish in
subsequent layers as the network approaches pixel prediction. The same mismatch appears in
generation: REPA (Yu et al., 2024) accelerates early training, but the baseline eventually over-
takes it, while moving the alignment deeper further degrades the final FID. These results show
that simply injecting stronger semantics is insufficient—a representation benefits generation only
when its information is preserved and remains functionally relevant to subsequent synthesis.
• Second, architectural separation does not necessarily improve representation for genera-
tion; it mainly redistributes semantic and spatial modeling across the network. A natural
response is to separate representation learning from pixel synthesis with an additional specialized

reshapes the encoder representation: its spatial modeling capacity determines what the encoder
must retain, producing different balances of global semantics and spatial structure without yield-

and computation overhead. Thus, they mainly shift the modeling burden between encoder and
decoder while departing from JiT’s simple, scalable Transformer design.
Together, these findings suggest that a useful generative representation must be both properly formed
and actively exploited. It should emerge through a compact interface that encourages the model to
organize information into a concise, generation-relevant representation, while remaining tightly cou-
pled to subsequent synthesis. Neither alone is sufficient: forcing the generator to rely on a poorly
formed representation can amplify unhelpful low-level information, whereas a well-structured rep-
resentation provides little benefit if it can be ignored.
Based on these insights, we introduce Representation Guidance (RG). RG preserves a plain Trans-
former architecture and operates on the representation interface rather than introducing a special-
ized pixel decoder. First, a low-dimensional bottleneck between the encoder and decoder forms
a compact implicit latent space, encouraging generation-relevant information to be organized into
a concise internal representation. Second, RG explicitly strengthens the generative utility of this
representation. We compare decoder predictions with and without the representation and use the
resulting representation-induced velocity offset to modify the flow-matching target (Lipman et al.,
2023; Liu et al., 2023; Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden, 2023). This increases the model’s dependence
on the representation, while gradients through it simultaneously shape the encoder toward informa-
tion useful for synthesis. The bottleneck and guidance thus play complementary roles: the former
governs how the representation is formed, while the latter ensures that generation actually uses
it. Pretrained representation alignment thus provides a semantic scaffold, while the representation
consumed by the decoder is ultimately learned end-to-end for generation. RG also shares a core
principle with self-distillation in self-supervised learning (Caron et al., 2021; Oquab et al., 2023):
the representation is bootstrapped from targets derived from the model’s own predictions. Whereas
self-distillation sharpens the teacher distribution to induce discriminative features, RG sharpens the
velocity target along the representation-induced offset, which compels the representation to encode
the semantic and spatial structure required for generation (Figure 6).
Figure 2 summarizes the consequences of this representation-centered design. Without introducing
specialized pixel modules, RG achieves a substantially stronger parameter–quality and compute–
quality trade-off, scales consistently with model size, and develops markedly clearer semantic and
spatial organization in its internal representations. On ImageNet (Deng et al., 2009) 256 × 256,
RG-XL/16 achieves an FID of 1.55, while RG-L/16 reaches 1.79 with only 459M parameters, out-
performing substantially larger pixel-space models. RG further achieves an FID of 1.74 at 512×512
and extends naturally to text-to-image generation, reaching 0.89 on GenEval (Ghosh et al., 2023)

Does better representation quality translate into better generation?
Pretrained representation
alignment provides a direct way to strengthen the semantic and spatial structure of an intermedi-
ate representation. Rather than asking whether alignment can improve the aligned layer, we ask
what happens afterwards: does this improvement survive subsequent pixel synthesis and benefit
generation? We apply REPA (Yu et al., 2024) at different layers of JiT-B/16 and track both the
representation across layers and generation quality during training.
• Experimental Results. Figure 4 reveals a clear mismatch. Aligning layer 4 substantially im-
proves its RMSC, LDS, and linear probing accuracy, indicating stronger spatial structure and
global semantics. However, these gains diminish immediately after the aligned layer as the net-
work progresses toward pixel prediction. The same mismatch appears in generation: REPA-4
accelerates early training, but the baseline overtakes it after roughly 160 epochs and achieves a
better final FID (4.56 vs. 4.80). Moreover, moving the alignment deeper further degrades genera-
tion, with FIDs of 5.38 and 8.33 for REPA-6 and REPA-8.
• Analysis. These results suggest that later layers progressively overwrite externally imposed se-
mantics as they specialize toward dense pixel interactions and high-frequency synthesis. This
mirrors lazy aggregation in ViTs (Shi et al., 2026a): models tend to follow the shortcut their su-
pervision permits. As per-pixel supervision is largely satisfied by local operations, pDiTs lack
incentive to form and exploit high-level representations. Alignment can therefore create a strong
intermediate representation without making it useful to the final generative prediction; imposing
it closer to the output can instead interfere more directly with pixel synthesis. Our first insight is
that better representation quality does not necessarily translate into greater generative util-
ity. A useful representation must remain functionally relevant throughout subsequent synthesis.
Does architectural separation yield a better representation for generation?
A natural response
to the above mismatch is to separate high-level representation learning from low-level synthesis with
an additional, separately designed pixel decoder. Recent pDiTs follow this route using pixel-wise
MLPs (Ma et al., 2026a), convolutions (Chen et al., 2026), or pixel-level attention (Yu et al., 2026).
But does such architectural separation actually produce a representation better suited to generation?
We compare these designs under the same training budget and x-prediction.

3.2 Representation Guidance
RG addresses the two requirements in Section 2 with two components. For the first, an encoder
maps the noisy image zt to a compact representation through a bottleneck, and a decoder uses this
representation as a condition to predict the clean image. For the second, RG guides training with the
model’s own learned representation. It shifts the training target along the velocity offset induced by
this representation. Figure 3(b) illustrates the architecture, and Figure 6 the training pipeline.
Decoupled architecture. Following DDT (Wang et al., 2025a), the model consists of an encoder Eθ
for representation learning and a decoder Dθ for pixel synthesis. All transformer blocks follow the
JiT architecture (Li & He, 2026). The decoder has its own patch embedding and a few transformer
blocks. It accesses the encoder only through the representation r, which serves as a token-wise
adaLN condition. Its x-prediction gives the velocity vθ(zt, t, r).
Implicit latent space. A linear bottleneck maps the encoder output tokens h ∈RN×D to a low-
dimensional latent and back:
u = h Wdown ∈RN×d,
r = u Wup ∈RN×D,
d ≪D.
(4)
We call the space of u an implicit latent space. It shares the compactness and spatial organization

generation, while diffusion remains in pixel space.

3.3 Training and Sampling
Guidance teacher. Following MG (Tang et al., 2025), we compute ∆vrep with the EMA network,
which provides a stable target. Its two predictions share zt, t, and c, and differ only in r. An online
teacher performs slightly better (Appendix A.5). Guidance interval and warm-up. RG is applied
for t ∈(tmin, 1] and is enabled after a plain flow matching warm-up. The interval excludes highly
noisy inputs, whose representations are unreliable (Section 4.4). RG is insensitive to the warm-up
length (Appendix A.5).Samples outside the interval or without the representation use the plain
target v. Overall objective and cost. We apply REPA (Yu et al., 2024) to an intermediate encoder
layer. The overall loss is L = LRG + λ LREPA. RG adds two gradient-free forward passes of the
EMA network during training and no cost at inference. Algorithm 1 in Appendix A.1 summarizes
one RG training step. For clarity, it omits the EMA teacher, the guidance interval, and REPA.

4.1 Implementation Details
Class-conditional image generation.
We follow the training setup of JiT (Li & He, 2026) on
ImageNet-1K (Deng et al., 2009) at 256×256 and 512×512. We use AdamW with β = (0.9, 0.95),
a constant learning rate of 2×10−4, a batch size of 1024, EMA with a decay of 0.9999, and bfloat16
mixed precision on 8 A100 GPUs. Timesteps follow a logit-normal distribution (Esser et al., 2024)
with logit(t) ∼N(−0.8, 0.82). Unless otherwise stated, RG uses s = 1.4 and tmin = 0.3 and
is enabled after 20 epochs. Following ADM (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021), we report FID, Inception
Score, precision, and recall on 50K samples. We sample with a 50-step Heun solver (Heun, 1900).
CFG is applied only within a timestep interval (Kynkäänniemi et al., 2024).
Text-to-image generation.
RG-T2I-L/16 replaces the JiT blocks of RG with the MM-JiT blocks
of MiniT2I (Wang et al., 2026b), which condition on text tokens from a frozen FLAN-T5-Large
encoder (Chung et al., 2022). Following MiniT2I, the model is pretrained on CC12M (Changpinyo
et al., 2021) with LLaVA-NeXT captions (Caption Emporium, 2024) and then fine-tuned on a 120K
mixture of BLIP3o-60K (Chen et al., 2025a), DALL-E 3 (OpenDatasets, 2023), and ShareGPT-4o-
Image (Chen et al., 2025b), both at 512 × 512. We evaluate on GenEval (Ghosh et al., 2023) and
DPG-Bench (Hu et al., 2024), which contain 553 and 1,065 prompts. We generate 4 images per
prompt with 100 sampling steps and a CFG scale of 5.0. To explore high-resolution generation, we
further fine-tune the trained 512 × 512 model at 1024 × 1024 on curated internal data.

Class-conditional image generation on ImageNet.
As shown in Table 2, RG-XL/16 achieves an

5 Related Work
Pixel-Space Generation. Most diffusion models generate images in a compressed latent space.
Pixel-space generation directly models RGB images, avoiding the reconstruction bottleneck and pre-
serving fine-grained details. Early work explored scalable pixel-space diffusion, flow-based genera-
tion, and autoregressive modeling (Jabri et al., 2022; Kingma & Gao, 2023; Hoogeboom et al., 2023;
Tschannen et al., 2024). Recent transformer-based methods improve generation through patch-level
modeling, pixel-level refinement, frequency decomposition, and encoder–decoder architectures (Li
& He, 2026; Yu et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026a; Chen et al., 2026). However, learning semantic rep-
resentations and synthesizing dense pixels from the same raw input often slows convergence. Our
method instead learns a compact internal representation to guide the generative objective during
training, without requiring an external encoder at sampling time.
Representation Learning for Generation. Pretrained visual encoders provide semantic represen-
tations that transfer well to recognition tasks, but may discard the fine-grained information needed
for image synthesis. Prior work uses these representations as diffusion latents (Yao et al., 2025;
Zheng et al., 2026a) or aligns intermediate diffusion features with frozen encoder features (Yu
et al., 2024; Shin et al., 2026). Dedicated encoder–decoder architectures also separate semantic
modeling from pixel synthesis (Wang et al., 2025a; Ma et al., 2026a; Chen et al., 2026). While
semantic representations can improve optimization, stronger alignment does not necessarily im-
prove generation, and external representations may be mismatched with pixel-level synthesis. Our
method learns representations end-to-end under the generative objective: a bottleneck encourages
compact features, while representation guidance strengthens their role in generation, preserving
global structure and fine-grained pixel information.
Guidance in Diffusion Models. Guidance is widely used to steer diffusion models toward con-
ditional distributions. Classifier guidance uses auxiliary classifier gradients, while classifier-free
guidance combines conditional and unconditional predictions during sampling (Dhariwal & Nichol,
2021; Ho & Salimans, 2022). Recent work leverages internal predictions: Internal Guidance in-
troduces auxiliary intermediate-layer supervision and extrapolates between intermediate and final
predictions during sampling (Zhou et al., 2026). RAEv2 shows that reparameterizing the REPA
prediction head enables self-guidance without a separately trained guidance model or an additional
classifier-free forward pass (Singh et al., 2026; Zu et al., 2026). While these methods primarily guide
sampling, our representation guidance modifies the flow-matching target during training using the
model’s own representation. Gradients propagate through this representation, directly shaping both
the learned vector field and the internal features used for generation. Appendix A.3 compares RG
with autoguidance (Karras et al., 2024) and Internal Guidance in pixel space.
6 Conclusion
We presented Representation Guidance (RG), which guides pixel diffusion transformers with their
own learned representations. RG combines a low-dimensional bottleneck with a representation-
guided flow-matching target to strengthen the generative utility of internal features while preserving
a plain Transformer architecture. It achieves FIDs of 1.55 and 1.74 on ImageNet 256 × 256 and
512 × 512, respectively, and extends to text-to-image generation. Our findings highlight that useful
generative representations must be both properly formed and actively exploited.
Limitations.
Our experiments focus on image generation, and extending RG to larger model
scales, video generation, and other modalities such as 3D and audio is left for future work.

A.3 Relation to Autoguidance and Internal Guidance
Autoguidance (Karras et al., 2024), Internal Guidance (IG) (Zhou et al., 2026), and RG all guide
a model with a weaker version of itself, extrapolating from a weak prediction toward the full one.
How the weak prediction is obtained determines what the guidance amplifies (Table 10, top).
Connections.
Autoguidance obtains the weak prediction from a separately trained model with
reduced capacity or training, and extrapolates at sampling time. IG attaches an auxiliary output
head to an intermediate block, supervises it with the flow matching loss, and extrapolates from this
shallow prediction toward the final one. Its training-time variant adds ω sg(vfinal −vinter) to the flow
matching target, the same form as Eq. (5). RG admits a similar view. Since r is computed from
zt, ∆vrep is the difference between the full model and its representation-free sub-network, i.e., the
decoder without access to the encoder.
Differences.
The weak prediction of autoguidance lacks capacity, and that of IG lacks depth. The
weak prediction of RG lacks only the information carried by r: both branches share the decoder,
the input, the time, and the condition. ∆vrep therefore isolates the contribution of a compact learned
representation, whereas the offsets of autoguidance and IG contain every difference between two
predictors. RG also differs in what training optimizes. In autoguidance and IG, the guidance direc-
tion is a by-product of separately supervised predictions. In RG, the gradient of LRG flows through
r into the encoder, so training shapes the representation that defines the guidance. RG needs neither
a second model nor an auxiliary head, and adds no inference cost.

Internal Guidance in pixel space.
IG was developed for latent diffusion transformers, SiT and
LightningDiT on VAE latents with v-prediction. We apply its training-time variant to JiT-B/16
following the IG paper: an auxiliary head after block 4, an auxiliary loss weight of 0.5, ω = 0.5
from epoch 20, EMA predictions, no guidance interval, and no REPA. We train for 200 epochs and
evaluate as in Section 4.4. IG raises the FID of JiT-B from 4.56 to 5.22 and lowers IS (Table 10,
bottom). Its optimal CFG scale drops from 3.3 to 2.4, so part of the guidance is absorbed into the
model, but this does not improve generation. Under the same comparison, RG reduces the FID from
3.79 to 3.28 and similarly lowers the optimal CFG scale from 3.95 to 2.60.
Why IG is less effective in pixel space.
Measured with the metrics of Section 2 (Figure 11), JiT-B
with IG meets neither requirement identified there.
The representation is not properly formed. The auxiliary head forces block 4 to predict pixels di-
rectly, which imposes pixel-level pressure on an intermediate layer. At this block, RMSC drops
from 0.46 to 0.28 and LDS from 0.11 to 0.04 relative to JiT-B, and linear probing accuracy falls
from 21.2% at layer 3 to 19.1%. Layers 5–8 remain less spatially structured than in JiT-B (RMSC
0.34–0.45 vs. 0.46–0.48), and the peak probing accuracy is unchanged (29.1% vs. 29.5%). The
auxiliary supervision thus pulls intermediate features toward pixel synthesis, the same tendency that
erodes externally aligned semantics in Section 2.
The guidance does not exploit a representation. In pixel space with x-prediction, the shallow pre-
diction is almost as accurate as the full one. At the end of training, the auxiliary head reaches a flow
matching loss of 0.0696, only 4% above the 0.0667 of the final output, and the RMS of vfinal −vinter
is 0.107. The remaining eight blocks add a small residual. We conjecture that this residual mainly
consists of fine pixel detail rather than semantics or layout, consistent with the high-frequency fea-
tures of pixel models (Figure 7). Amplifying it does not strengthen global structure. In latent space,
where the VAE removes most pixel detail, this gap more likely reflects semantic refinement.
How RG addresses both issues.
RG changes what defines the weak prediction and where the
representation is formed. First, r is formed at a compact bottleneck and is never asked to reproduce
pixels. The decoder handles pixel synthesis from zt through its own patch embedding. The RG-
B encoder therefore keeps high spatial diversity and locality (RMSC up to 0.91, LDS up to 0.66)

epochs. We report the top-1 accuracy on the ImageNet validation set.

Guidance teacher.
By default, RG computes ∆vrep with the EMA network (Section 3.3). We
also train RG-B with the online network as the teacher and keep the stop-gradient and all other
settings. Both teachers use two gradient-free forward passes, and the EMA weights are maintained
for sampling in either case, so the training cost is identical. As shown in Figure 13, the online teacher
lowers the FID at every CFG scale, from 3.28 to 3.18 at the respective optimum, and raises IS from
264.1 to 272.3. The gap grows with the CFG scale, from 0.02 at 2.0 to 0.12 at 2.8.
We attribute this gain to the learned condition of RG. MG guides with a fixed class label, so an
EMA teacher only smooths the prediction. In RG, the condition r is itself learned and changes
during training. With a decay of 0.9999, the EMA network averages over roughly 104 steps, or
about eight epochs, so its offset describes an earlier representation rather than the r that conditions
the student. The online teacher evaluates Eq. (5) exactly as written, with the same parameters for the
teacher and the student, and the target always reflects the current representation. The stop-gradient
treats this offset as a constant at each step, which suffices to keep training stable in our setting. Both
results come from single runs. The consistent gap across CFG scales suggests a systematic effect,
but all other results in this paper use the EMA teacher.
Warm-up length.
By default, RG is enabled after 20 epochs of plain flow matching. Enabling it
at epoch 0, 10, or 30 instead changes the FID by less than 0.01 (Figure 13, right), a far smaller effect
than that of the RG scale or interval (Figure 9). RG is thus insensitive to the warm-up length. It even
works when enabled from the first epoch, while the EMA teacher is still close to its initialization.
We conjecture that the representation carries little information early in training, so ∆vrep is small
and RG stays close to plain flow matching. The guidance then grows as the representation becomes
informative. The warm-up is therefore optional, and we keep 20 epochs as the default.

maintained until the decoder uses it, rather than on the peak alignment quality.
