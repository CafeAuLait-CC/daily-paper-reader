Abstract
Synthesizing believable 4D hand–object grasping sequences
is important for character animation, extended reality, and
embodied agents. Grasping requires a hand to approach,
contact, and lift or manipulate an object coherently. Existing
synthesis methods often model only the final static grasp or
rely on additional conditions, such as language, target grasps,
or object trajectories, to specify the interaction. We instead
study the underconstrained problem of generating a complete
grasping motion from a single initial hand–object state. Our
framework decouples hand-motion generation from object-
motion recovery. Given an object point cloud in the initial
wrist frame and the initial parameterized hand configuration,
conditional flow matching predicts a sequence-level contact
map and a hand pose at each normalized sequence time.
This time-indexed formulation avoids the error accumulation
of autoregressive rollout while representing the complete
motion. We then infer an attachment frame and recover rigid-
object motion through training-free, contact-guided weighted
registration. We evaluate on held-out DexYCB objects and
unseen object geometries reconstructed from in-the-wild
videos by Open-CHOIR. Compared with LatentHOI, our
method produces smoother hand and object motion in every
setting, matches or improves on its contact and penetration
metrics in the wild under a shared initialization, and is about
30× faster per generated frame. In a 15-participant study,
our motions were preferred in roughly two thirds of (forced-
choice pairwise) comparisons. Precise contact closure on
novel geometry, however, remains a key open challenge.
Code will be released.

Introduction
Grasping is a fundamental way in which humans interact
with the physical world. Reproducing it computationally
requires more than synthesizing a plausible static hand pose:
the hand must approach an object, establish contact, and
manipulate it through a temporally coherent motion. Such
dynamic grasping is valuable for character animation, virtual
and augmented reality, and embodied agents.
Acquiring training data for dynamic interactions, however,
remains challenging. High-quality datasets commonly rely
on calibrated multi-camera RGBD rigs or specialized motion-
capture systems [3, 8, 29]. Monocular reconstruction reduces
the (specialized) capture burden, but must resolve coordinated
hand and object motion despite severe mutual occlusion and
depth ambiguity [33]. Consequently, available datasets cover
only a limited range of objects and interaction trajectories.
Existing synthesis methods address this ambiguity either
by predicting a static grasp endpoint [10, 21, 36] or by
introducing additional conditions such as language, target
grasps, body motion, or object trajectories [2, 5, 6, 16].
Generating a complete dynamic grasp from only a single
initial hand–object state therefore remains underexplored.
We address this problem through a hand-centric formula-
tion. We learn how the hand evolves during a grasp while
treating the motion of a rigid object as a consequence of
contact established with the hand. This decoupling keeps
the learned component focused on articulated hand dynam-
ics and recovers object motion geometrically after grasp
formation. We train and evaluate on captured benchmark se-
quences, and use interactions reconstructed from in-the-wild
videos by Open-CHOIR [33] to test transfer to novel object
geometries. This separates reusable grasping dynamics from

Algorithmically, we decompose dynamic grasp generation
into two stages. First, given an initial object point cloud
expressed in the initial wrist frame and the corresponding
initial MANO [27] hand configuration, our conditional flow-
matching model [18] predicts how the hand evolves from
approach, through contact, to object manipulation. The
framework predicts a sequence-level contact map and a
MANO pose at each normalized sequence time, without
autoregressive rollout. Second, given the generated hand
trajectory and contact information, we infer an attachment
frame and the corresponding hand–object contacts, then
rigidly propagate the object through the remainder of the
sequence using these correspondences.
Thus, we learn
contact and articulated hand motion, then recover object
motion geometrically without another learned model.
We train on DexYCB [3] and evaluate unseen-object gen-
eralization on objects disjoint from training, using both
captured and LatentHOI-generated initial hand poses. We
further test cross-domain generalization using in-the-wild
sequences reconstructed by Open-CHOIR [33]. Our evalua-
tion shows that the generated hand trajectories transfer well
across this distribution shift, while revealing grasp closure
as the primary remaining challenge. In a 15-participant
study, our motions receive 66.25% preference for naturalness
and 64.58% for apparent grasp plausibility; generation takes
2.8 ms per frame, versus 85.8 ms for LatentHOI [16]. Further,
our training-free object-motion stage propagates unseen rigid
objects. In summary, our contributions are: (i) formulating
dynamic grasp generation from one initial hand–object state,
without observed interaction or text; (ii) modeling contact-
conditioned hand motion with conditional flow matching;
(iii) recovering rigid-object motion from inferred contact
without further training; and (iv) evaluating transfer to unseen
DexYCB objects and Open-CHOIR reconstructions, with
grasp closure identified as the main challenge.

Related Work
Full-body human–object interaction synthesis.
A sub-
stantial body of work models interactions at whole-body
and scene scales. InterDiff [34] predicts future human and
dynamic-object motion from observed interaction history
and applies a physics-informed correction during diffusion.
OMOMO [14] reverses this conditioning direction, generat-
ing full-body manipulation motion from a supplied object
trajectory and using predicted hand positions to promote
contact. Text-conditioned approaches instead generate the
human, object, and their contacts jointly: CG-HOI [7] uses
learned contact as guidance, while CHOIS [15] additionally
conditions on initial states and sparse object waypoints to
support long-horizon interactions in 3D scenes. Recently,
DecHOI [11] separates trajectory planning from detailed ac-
tion synthesis and supports responsive replanning in dynamic
environments. These methods coordinate locomotion, bal-
ance, and manipulation using past motion, language, object
trajectories, waypoints, or scene constraints. They prioritize
body-scale behavior over finger articulation. We instead
focus on grasp formation from a single initial hand–object
state.
Hand animation in computer graphics.
Early graphics
systems used rule-based grasp and hand control [26, 28].
Captured examples enabled shape- and task-aware grasp
selection [17], physics-based controllers [24, 40], and contact-
force-aware interaction transfer [12]. Optimization methods
used initial contact and kinematic goals [19], an initial
grasp and partial object trajectory [20], high-level object
goals [23], or captured body and object motion [35]. These
works establish contact and dynamics as central to hand
animation. We generate approach–contact–lift motion from
an initial hand–object state, without a prescribed goal or
object trajectory.
Static hand grasp synthesis.
Object-conditioned grasp
synthesis commonly predicts a plausible hand configura-
tion at a single instant.
The GRAB dataset introduced
GrabNet [29], which combines a conditional variational
model with refinement to synthesize MANO grasps for
unseen object shapes. GraspTTA [10] further couples hand-
pose generation with hand–object contact consistency and
adapts the model at test time to out-of-domain objects, while
ContactGen [21] generates an object-centric contact repre-
sentation before optimizing the hand pose. More recent
diffusion priors improve diversity and geometric generaliza-
tion: G-HOP [36] jointly models hand configurations and
category-level object shape, and S2HGrasp [32] operates on
partial, single-view scene point clouds. Related work on
robotic dexterous hands has emphasized physical feasibil-
ity at scale. DexGraspNet [31] introduces a differentiable
force-closure-based pipeline for synthesizing diverse static
grasps, including support for MANO hands, while DexGrasp
Anything injects physical constraints during both diffusion
training and sampling [41]. Concurrently, DCGrasp [1] uses
a distance-profile representation to support controllable grasp
generation. These methods generate grasp endpoints rather
than complete approach–contact–lift motions.
Dynamic hand–object motion synthesis.
Moving beyond
grasp endpoints, ManipNet [37] synthesizes finger motion
from object geometry and manipulation trajectories using
a hand–object spatial representation. D-Grasp [5] learns a
reinforcement-learning policy in simulation for approach,
grasp, and transport given a reference grasp and target object
pose.
GraspXL [38] scales simulation-based control to
diverse unseen objects and hand morphologies, with control
over grasp region, approach direction, wrist rotation, and hand
position. These methods pursue physically grounded motion

but rely on prescribed goals or a simulation environment
rather than an initial configuration alone.
Generative methods instead vary the conditioning signal.
Text2HOI [2] uses diffusion conditioned on language, object
geometry, and generated contact maps, while DiffH2O [6]
separates grasp acquisition from subsequent manipulation us-
ing text and optional target-grasp guidance. LatentHOI [16]
separates global motion from fine-grained grasping in a
latent representation to generalize to unseen objects; its
single-hand model and DexYCB evaluation make it our clos-
est experimental baseline (Section 5). HOIGPT [9] tokenizes
interaction sequences for generation or completion from
text, objects, and partial motion, while OpenHOI [39] com-
bines language reasoning with affordance-driven diffusion
for open-vocabulary manipulation. MEgoHand [42] uses
flow matching with egocentric RGB, language, and an initial
hand pose, whereas HO-Flow [4] uses latent flow matching to
generate joint hand–object motion from text and a canonical
object. Most of these methods generate hand and object
motion jointly and use semantic or trajectory-level condi-
tions to resolve the intended action. In contrast, our learned
components predict hand-side contact and articulated hand
motion from only a single initial hand–object configuration,
without text, target grasps, target object poses, or prescribed
trajectories; object motion is estimated subsequently.

4
Method
Dynamic grasping couples two challenges. Hand articula-
tion is high-dimensional and under-constrained, since many
grasps fit the same initial state. By contrast, under our rigid-
attachment assumption, object motion after grasp formation
follows the hand. Learning both jointly forces one network
to represent a stochastic articulation prior and a deterministic
rigid-body consequence. Instead, we decompose the task
into (i) a generative model that predicts hand-side quantities
and (ii) an optimization that recovers object motion geometri-
cally (Section 4.3). This confines learning to the ambiguous
hand motion while allowing the geometric stage to transfer
to unseen shapes.
Key to our approach, we make contact an explicit interme-
diate, rather than letting the pose network implicitly discover
where the hand should touch each object. Specifically, we
first predict a compact, object-grounded contact target—a
single sequence-level contact-distance map over the hand ver-
tices (Section 4.1)—and then generate poses conditioned on
it (Section 4.2). Both generators are trained with conditional
flow matching [18], and all quantities are expressed in the
canonical frame of the initial hand in the first frame. This
target lives in a hand-centric space largely invariant to object
identity, turning grasp this unseen geometry into the easier
reach this contact pattern task. We find this design choice
transfers more readily across object geometry (Section 5).

Evaluation
Datasets.
DexYCB [3] contains 1,000 grasping sequences
across 20 YCB object categories. Following LatentHOI [16],
we train on 799 sequences of 16 objects and evaluate on
200 sequences of four held-out objects: master chef can,
banana, mug, and foam brick. One sequence lacks com-
plete hand annotations and is excluded. Left-hand sequences
are mirrored to the right hand. For mesh reconstruction,
we use the annotated MANO shape coefficients. We call
this protocol unseen object. For in-the-wild unseen object
evaluation, we use Open-CHOIR [33] reconstructions with
novel object geometries, without fine-tuning. The 30 test se-
quences each show a right hand manipulating a reconstructed
object. The Open-CHOIR hand and object parameters are
converted to the DexYCB annotation format by folding the
release’s hand-only frame rotation into the MANO global
orientation and baking the per-axis object scale into the rest
mesh, and each sequence is then expressed in the canonical
frame of its initial hand, as for DexYCB. Since the release
provides no shape coefficients, the hand uses the MANO
mean shape. Reconstructions serve as pseudo-ground truth.
We retain the interval with both hand and object parame-
ters and evaluate generated motions at 65 uniformly spaced
normalized sequence times.

Comparison.
We train LatentHOI [16] on the same split
using its official single-hand implementation and pretrained
GraspVAE. It is a task-level, not input-matched, baseline: it
receives canonical object geometry and the prompt “pick up
⟨object⟩” (“pick up object” for Open-CHOIR), but no initial
hand pose or reference trajectory. Its default 1,000-step
DDPM sampler generates 96 frames.
Ours (GT init.) uses the ground-truth first-frame hand
pose; Ours (LatentHOI init.) uses LatentHOI’s generated first
frame. Both methods use the same geometric object propa-
gation and 65-frame evaluation grid, resampling LatentHOI
outputs at the corresponding normalized times. For each
sequence and method, we draw 10 samples and rank those
with finite HO-Dist and IV by rank(HO-Dist) + rank(IV).
We select the highest-ranked sample with a detected attach-
ment, or the highest-ranked valid sample if none has an
attachment. This preference tests geometric attachment, not
physical lifting (details in Section B). Both methods use all
200 DexYCB and 30 Open-CHOIR test sequences.
The DexGraspNet [31] baseline uses its MANO imple-
mentation to synthesize a static grasp and interpolates from
LatentHOI’s initial hand pose during approach. This tests
whether endpoint interpolation suffices for realistic grasping
motion. Since it does not generate post-grasp motion, we
include it in the qualitative comparison and user study, but
not the full-sequence metrics in Table 1.

Implementation details.
Architecture. We use 𝑁= 1,024
object points, 𝐾= 75 contact candidates, 𝐾𝑓= 245 dense
finger-region vertices, and 𝐾𝑎= 8 attachment points. The
feature width is 𝑑= 256, with 𝑀𝑜= 𝑀𝑐= 16 pooled object
and contact tokens, 𝑀𝜏= 8 time tokens, 𝐿𝑐= 4 contact-flow
blocks, 𝐿ℎ= 6 pose-flow blocks, and 𝐿IRLS = 3 robust-
registration passes. Both generators use 𝐻= 8 attention
heads, feed-forward width 1,024, and dropout 0.1. The
PointNet encoder Φ𝑐uses pointwise widths 64, 128, and
1,024, followed by a linear projection to 𝑑, and each time
MLP has one hidden layer of width 𝑑.
Training. Both flows are trained concurrently for 300
epochs using AdamW with a constant learning rate and
weight decay of 10−4 and batch size 64, using every observed
frame as a target and sampling the two flow times inde-
pendently from U(0, 1). We keep the checkpoint with the
lowest sampled generation error on a validation subset of 20
training sequences. Each epoch, every training sequence is
re-anchored at a sampled pre-contact frame, with probability
0.4 of retaining the original first frame and a quadratic bias
toward earlier frames otherwise. The input object cloud is
redrawn from a four-times-larger farthest-point-sampled pool
and perturbed with 1 mm Gaussian jitter. A single AdamW
optimizer minimizes Lℎat every mini-batch and adds L𝑐
once every 32 mini-batches. The active terms have equal
coefficients, and the three blocks of Equation (13) are equally
weighted. The pose branch and shared PointNet receive
gradients at every step; the contact branch receives gradients
only when L𝑐is included.
Inference and post-processing. We use 𝜖𝑝= 1 cm and
𝜖𝑟= 𝜋/36 rad (5◦) to select the training contact frame, and
𝜖pen = 0.08, 𝜖𝑐= 5 mm, 𝑛min = 8, 𝑘𝐷= 10, and 𝛿= 1 cm
for post-processing. Both flows use 32 forward-Euler steps.
We evaluate sequences of 𝑇= 65 frames, retaining the initial
frame at 𝑡0 = 0 and generating 64 frames at 𝑡𝑘= 𝑘/64 for
𝑘= 1, . . . , 64. One contact map is sampled per sequence,
and the same pose-noise sample is shared across all motion-
time queries.
Runtime.
The contact flow is integrated once per sequence
and the pose flow at all 64 requested times in parallel. Attach-
ment detection and object propagation follow hand generation.
We time 200 DexYCB sequences on an NVIDIA RTX 4070
Ti SUPER, each with 65 frames and 32 Euler steps per flow,
synchronizing the GPU around generation and excluding the
first sequence as warm-up. Contact and hand generation
take 0.18 s per 65-frame sequence, or 2.8 ms per generated
frame. We report this fixed-length generation time for both
DexYCB and Open-CHOIR in Table 1. Total time including
data loading is 0.50 s per sequence.

Conclusion
We presented a hand-centric framework that generates com-
plete grasping sequences from a single initial hand–object
configuration. Conditional flow matching predicts sequence-
level contact and time-indexed hand poses; contact-guided
registration then recovers rigid-object motion without further
learning. Evaluation on unseen DexYCB objects and in-the-
wild Open-CHOIR reconstructions demonstrates transferable
hand dynamics, while contact quality and attachment remain
challenging under geometric distribution shift. We reported
better quality as well as significant speedup over existing
alternatives. Physics-aware contact refinement, adaptive
correspondence updates, and richer object dynamics could
extend this approach beyond rigid pick-up motions.

B
Baseline adaptation and sample selection
LatentHOI [16] produces 96-frame motions conditioned on
canonical object geometry and a pick-up prompt. Its output
is resampled to the common 65-frame evaluation grid. Our
method is evaluated from either the observed initial hand
pose or a LatentHOI-generated initial pose, as described in
Section 5.
For each sequence, LatentHOI and our method each draw
ten samples and use the same selection procedure. Samples
with non-finite HO-Dist or IV are excluded. Among the
remaining samples, HO-Dist and IV are ranked separately in
ascending order, starting at one; exact ties receive their mean
rank. Ranks are computed over this entire valid population,
before applying the attachment preference. Samples are
ordered by the sum of the two ranks, then by HO-Dist, then
IV, with remaining ties retaining generation order.
We select the first sample in that order with at least one
attached frame. The attachment flag is set at the geometrically
detected trigger frame and remains set thereafter. This tests
geometric attachment rather than physical lifting.
If no
sample is attached, the top-ranked valid sample is retained;
if no valid sample exists, the sequence is excluded from the
selected-sample average. Dataset scores average the selected
sample’s metrics equally across sequences.
C
Additional implementation details
Pose offsets use the continuous 6D rotation representa-
tion [43] and are normalized separately for global orientation,
articulation, and translation. Each block has one scalar mean
and standard deviation, repeated across the parameters in
that block. The initial articulation uses a separate scalar
mean and standard deviation. Object XYZ coordinates use a
per-axis mean and one shared standard deviation; normals are
unchanged. All statistics are fitted on training data, including
sampled pre-contact anchors.
