Abstract 

Generative models offer a powerful route to scientific simulation and discovery by enabling the study of complex populations and rare phenomena. Flow matching can generate realistic samples, yet inaccurate occurrence frequencies can distort population statistics and compromise inferences about rare events. Here we developed generalized mass-preserving coupled flow matching (G-MPCF), which matches the probability masses of Gaussian source regions to those of weighted data regions. This construction encodes the desired regional frequencies in the correspondence between noise and observations, shaping the learned dynamics while preserving the prescribed target distribution. On a rare-mode benchmark, G-MPCF reduces relative score error by 95.0% and numerical integration error by 89.9% compared with independent flow matching. On CIFAR-10 with a 100:1 class-probability ratio, it reduces corrected class-mass error by 44.6% compared with minibatch optimal transport, while improving mean image fidelity with matched computational budgets. We envision that incorporating regional probability calibration into generative modelling will support more reliable scientific simulation and inference, enabling the study of systems in which rare events and their occurrence frequencies are central to understanding the underlying mechanisms.  

> Keywords: flow matching, generative models, probability calibration, optimal transport, rare events

Generative models are becoming powerful tools for exploring complex scientific systems, including populations in which rare states carry impor-tant mechanistic information. Their usefulness depends not only on generating plausible obser-vations but also on reproducing how frequently these states occur, as distorted probabilities can bias population statistics and the interpretation of rare phenomena. Diffusion and score-based models learn to reverse progressive data corrup-tion 1–4 , whereas continuous normalizing flows learn differential equations that transform tractable source distributions into complex target distribu-tions 5–7 . These approaches have enabled studies of 1arXiv:2610.16780v1 [eess.IV] 12 Oct 2026 molecular transition states, turbulent trajectories and high-resolution Earth-system fields 8–10 . Yet realistic generated structures do not necessarily imply accurate population frequencies, making the simultaneous recovery of structural fidelity and pre-scribed occurrence frequencies a central challenge for scientific generative modelling. Flow matching and stochastic interpolants learn generative velocity fields along probability paths between tractable sources and target dis-tributions 11–14 . The source–target coupling deter-mines which displacements supervise these fields and can alter the interpolation geometry with-out changing the prescribed endpoint marginals. Dynamic optimal transport provides a kinetic-energy formulation of transport 15,16 . Minibatch transport and multisample matching construct finite-sample assignments 12,17 , while rectified flow uses learned dynamics to generate revised train-ing pairs 18 . Optimal flow matching develops direct transport mappings 19 ; cluster-wise trans-port exploits local data structure 20 , and learned plans and model-aligned costs further adapt pair-ing to the regression problem 21,22 . Together, these approaches establish coupling design as an impor-tant factor in generative learning, while leaving open how its joint probability structure affects endpoint frequency accuracy under limited model capacity and computational budgets. For imbalanced generation, the desired distri-bution also specifies how often each part of the data should occur. Sample weights allow this pre-scribed population to differ from the empirical data frequencies. Their interaction with source– target assignment is particularly relevant when low-probability regions receive few draws within a training batch. Previous studies have investi-gated bias correction and distribution calibration in generative models 23,24 , while precision, recall, density and coverage characterize sample fidelity and support 25–27 . A region can contain convinc-ing and diverse generated samples while receiving an inaccurate fraction of the total probability. In flow matching, this frequency error can arise even when training pairs have the prescribed endpoint marginals. Couplings with identical source and tar-get laws induce different conditional velocity fields along the interpolation path. Their approximation by a finite network affects the distribution reached during generation 28,29 .Regional probability offers a direct way to organize this regression problem. Matching the probability of a source region to that of a tar-get region assigns a corresponding share of source draws to the target’s local structure. Condi-tional sampling within the target region then retains the relative weights of its observations. The remaining freedom in pairing can organize the supervising displacements within each region. Regions fitted in a continuous feature representa-tion can capture structure across semantic classes. Class-frequency error and within-class coverage then provide complementary measurements of the resulting feature-based coupling. This construction connects population frequencies to the geometry of training pairs and motivates examining their effects on field approximation, sample allocation and within-region coverage together. The learned field also determines the numeri-cal work required for generation. Implicit diffusion sampling and higher-order solvers reduce the cost of following learned dynamics 30–32 ; consistency models and mean flows learn mappings for few-step inference 33,34 . Coupling design acts earlier, through the interpolation states and velocities presented during training. Its endpoint effects therefore involve both approximation of the train-ing field and evolution along the learned trajectory. Joint measurements of field, rollout and integration errors can connect changes in the training pairs to generation under a limited evaluation budget. Here we introduce generalized mass-preserving coupled flow matching (G-MPCF), which con-structs training pairs by aligning Gaussian source regions with weighted data regions according to their probabilities. As illustrated in Fig. 1, tar-get observations are sampled conditionally within matched regions, while additional within-region rank matching refines pair geometry without altering target sampling frequencies. Rare-mode experiments show reductions of 95.0% in rela-tive score error and 89.9% in solver error. On CIFAR-10 with a 100:1 class-probability ratio, matched-computation comparisons yield 49.3% and 44.6% lower corrected class-mass errors than independent pairing and minibatch optimal trans-port, respectively, alongside improved mean fidelity. Component interventions identify regional prob-ability matching as the principal source of these gains, with mass-only coupling achieving compa-rable calibration and fidelity. We first evaluated a rare-mode target with an explicit coupling and reference field. Rare12 con-tains eight common modes of probability 0.115 and four outer modes of probability 0.02, with centred and whitened coordinates. We partition a two-dimensional standard Gaussian into alternat-ing sectors of probability 0.115 and 0.135, then split the larger sectors at r⋆ = p−2 log(0 .02 /0.135) = 1.95425 to assign outer probability 0.02 to each rare mode. We call this analytic construction MPCF and compare it with independent conditional flow matching (I-CFM). Independent interpolation paths cross the central region towards different targets. Relative velocity MSE fell by 66.7%, and Euler-to-reference MSE at 16 evaluations fell by 89.9%. Score error fell by 79.3% and velocity error by 46.1% in Fig. 2f. MPCF also improved the one-and two-evaluation endpoints. At 16 evaluations, independent pairing reached SWD 0.2543 and MPCF 0.2722, accompanied by Euler-to-reference MSE of 0.00489 and 0.01019. At a budget of 16 evaluations, MPCF reached mean rare12 SWD 0.1392, com-pared with 0.1456 for OT-CFM, 0.1522 for OFM and 0.2026 for I-CFM. Its mode-mass error was 0.0260, the lowest among the six methods in Fig. 2i,j. At one evaluation, OFM gave the low-est mean SWD on both targets; MPCF gave the lowest mode-mass error, reaching 0.0248 on rare12 and 0.0171 on gaussian8 in Fig. 2g,h.

## Regional probability assignment explains the image gain 

We evaluated regional calibration and rank order-ing through five coupling operations, with three independent training runs each. Mass-only cou-pling used quantile assignment and conditional target sampling; geometry-only coupling used global rank matching; and uniform-region pair-ing combined equal source-region probabilities, regional sorting and the weighted conditional tar-get law. All operations shared the model, training budget and inference setting. Because mass-only and independent pairing preserve the same target marginal, their comparison isolates the effect of regional source–target correspondence. Comparing G-MPCF with the mass-only variant isolates the contribution of rank matching within each region. Mass-only coupling reproduced the allocation and fidelity gains of G-MPCF in Fig. 4b. Mean MassTV was 0.05023 for mass-only coupling, 0.05044 for G-MPCF and 0.09561 for indepen-dent pairing; their weighted FIDs were 19.98, 20.16 and 22.48, respectively. Geometry-only matching yielded MassTV 0.10020 and FID 22.53, compared with 0.10097 and 21.42 for uniform-region pair-ing. Notably, mass-only coupling achieved slightly lower mean MassTV and FID than G-MPCF, indicating no additional aggregate benefit from within-region rank matching in this setting. Thus, calibrated regional sampling accounts for the princi-pal allocation and fidelity improvements, although differences between the two calibrated variants remain descriptive given the three independent runs. For the second most probable class, the predicted-to-target frequency ratio increased from 0.68 under independent pairing to 0.86 under mass-only coupling and 0.85 under G-MPCF. Despite similar frequency profiles, the two calibrated vari-ants exhibited differences in class-specific recall, as shown in Fig. 4c,d. Minority recall was 0.694 for mass-only coupling and 0.565 for G-MPCF, with corresponding minority precision of approx-imately 0.894 and 0.900, respectively, as detailed in Supplementary Table S9. # Discussion 

We developed G-MPCF, a coupling approach that incorporates regional probability calibration into flow matching. The method matches Gaussian source regions to weighted data regions accord-ing to their probability masses, preserving the prescribed target distribution while reorganizing the velocity supervision used for training. On the rare-mode benchmark, the analytic coupling reduces relative score error by 95.0% and numer-ical integration error by 89.9% compared with independent flow matching. On CIFAR-10 with a 100:1 class-probability ratio, G-MPCF reduces corrected class-mass error by 44.6% relative to minibatch optimal transport and improves mean image fidelity under matched computational bud-gets. G-MPCF uses the standard flow-matching objective, allowing regional calibration to be incor-porated into existing training pipelines without changing the generative architecture. Extending G-MPCF to scientific data will require representations that distinguish the states whose populations are relevant to the applica-tion. In the image experiments, pretr ained fea-tures define the regions and observation weights determine their probabilities. For molecular and materials systems, conformational coordinates or structural order parameters could provide a physi-cal basis for defining these regions. Relative state populations obtained from reference simulations or experiments could then inform the target prob-abilities. Learning representations that resolve transitions between these states, and adapting the 11 partition as additional observations become avail-able, would be useful for applying the method to more complex systems. Independent estimates of generated state populations would allow regional calibration to be evaluated together with structural fidelity and coverage. With regional probabilities specified, the remaining freedom in the coupling can be used to organize the geometry of training pairs. The image ablations support this separation, with calibrated sampling accounting for the principal allocation and fidelity gains and local pairing affecting cov-erage. Matching rules that incorporate physical symmetries or appropriate transport costs could further adapt the construction to scientific data. Conditional extensions could represent changes in state populations with temperature, pressure or external forcing, providing a route to generating ensembles across physical conditions. We anticipate that regional probability matching will be useful for scientific sampling tasks in which structural accuracy and relative populations jointly deter-mine the quantities of interest, including molecular ensembles, competing material states and rare configurations in complex dynamical systems.

FID non-inferiority uses margin max(1 , 0.05FID B ) against compara-tor B; MassTV and recall margins are 0.01 and 0.02. Non-inferiority tests add the correspond-ing margin to each paired benefit. Supplementary Table S4 reports the component tests and joint criteria. Balanced transfer uses three paired runs and descriptive summaries; the component, scal-ing and selection experiments use separate sets of three paired runs. 

## Ablations and model selection 

Five coupling operations isolate regional calibra-tion and rank ordering. Mass relaxation adds 

ρ = 0 .25 , 0.5, 0.75 between calibrated and uniform regional probabilities, sharing the endpoint models with the component experiment. Three runs at each of the eight distinct settings give 24 trained mod-els, with initializations independent of the primary comparison. Scaling varies training-data fraction, model width or computation around the full-data, width-96 reference. Eight distinct configurations each use three paired runs of I-CFM and G-MPCF, with 10,000 images per run at Euler NFE 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16. The settings and resource measurements are specified in Supplementary Tables S12–S13. Validation compares a standard Gaussian source with a fitted 20-component, rank-64 prob-abilistic principal-component mixture 52 . For the numerical endpoint Tθ,k (z) at NFE k, the action 

Aλ,k (z) = z+λ[Tθ,k (z)−z] retains fraction λ of the full displacement. Two sources, independent, OT-CFM and G-MPCF pairing, λ ∈ { 0.25 , 0.5, 0.75 , 1}

and k ∈ { 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 }, plus two source-retention controls, give 122 candidates. Using the three-run means, the rule admits actions within 0.01 of the 14 best source-retention MassTV, retains those within one standard error of the minimum validation KID and selects the smallest NFE with fixed tie breaks. Each candidate uses 10,000 images per run over three runs. The selected action is evaluated on test data, followed by the full action grid. Sup-plementary Section 7 specifies the candidates and selection rule. Mean-based summaries use sample standard deviations across training runs; validation KID uses the standard error of the mean.

## 4.3 Shared model and comparator implementations 

The image methods share an unconditional residual U-Net and optimizer; Supplementary Tables S6 and S7 give the training schedules and model specification. The flow loss averages squared velocity errors over batch samples, stratified times and image coordinates. Evaluation uses exponential-moving-average weights. Numerical trajectories use uniform time steps from t = 0 to t = 1 and evolve without clipping; metric images are clipped to [ −1, 1] and encoded by rounding 127 .5( x + 1) to unsigned integers. Method-specific update counts match the computation envelope within 1%; rectified flow uses 50,000 first-stage and 7,893 second-stage updates. OT-CFM uses the exact squared-Euclidean minibatch assignment, corresponding to the BatchOT instance of multisample flow matching 12,17 . Two-stage rectified flow charges the teacher-generated pairs and both training stages. DGSWP uses the official learned-plan module, reusing each learned pool for ten flow batches 21 .MAC adapts the official full endpoint-prediction cost and solves its assignment exactly 22 . This adaptation uses the full cost in place of the source implementation’s Sinkhorn warm-up and plan extraction. ## 4.4 Coupling implementation, numerical precision and cost 

The fixed feature extractor is an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 37,38 . Images are resized to 224 pixels using antialiased bilinear interpolation and normalized with the extractor’s ImageNet channel statistics. The 512-dimensional penultimate features are averaged over each image and its horizontal reflection, then standardized using target-weighted training means and standard deviations. Weighted k-means 39 with ten initializations fits 20 non-empty regions. A sign-anchored principal axis of their centroids determines the region order. The eight leading weighted principal-component directions of the standardized features define the target coordinates. 41 7 Validation-based source and action selection 

## 7.1 Candidate grid and decision rule 

The selector compares a standard Gaussian with a directly sampleable mixture of 20 probabilistic principal-component components 52 , fitted on the training split. Components follow the fitted target regions and have probabilities pj , rank 64 in 3,072-dimensional pixel space and covariance floor 10 −5. For G-MPCF with this source, the sampled component index supplies the source-region label for conditional target sampling; the within-region rank permutation then uses the source projections. Each source is crossed with independent, OT-CFM and G-MPCF coupling. Each of these six source–coupling combinations is trained three times independently, giving 18 fields. For each full numerical endpoint Tθ,k (z), define the residual action 

Aλ,k (z) = z + λ[Tθ,k (z) − z], λ ∈ { 0.25 , 0.5, 0.75 , 1}, k ∈ { 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 }. (S55) Adding source-retention actions A0(z) = z gives 2(1 + 3 · 4 · 5) = 122 candidates. The residual fraction scales the final displacement after integration at the specified NFE. Selection uses mean validation KID and corrected MassTV across three training runs. Actions qualify on mass when their mean MassTV is at most 0.01 above the best source-retention mean, 0.7929. This threshold of 0.8029 admits 76 of the 122 actions. Among them, the KID threshold is the minimum mean plus its standard error. The rule chooses the smallest NFE among qualifying actions, followed by fixed source, coupling and fraction tie breaks. The minimum validation KID is 0.0131 with standard error 1 .23 × 10 −4, giving a threshold of 0.0132. A single action qualifies, using a standard Gaussian source, G-MPCF, λ = 1 and NFE 16.

## 8.3 Source adequacy in feature space 

The source-adequacy experiment evaluates residual actions in 64-dimensional representations of Kuzushiji-MNIST (KMNIST) 53 , CIFAR-10 and UCI Human Activity Recognition (UCI HAR) 54 , with 24 independent training runs per dataset and 72 in total. KMNIST uses 50,000 training, 10,000 validation and 10,000 test observations; CIFAR-10 uses 45,000, 5,000 and 10,000. Subject-disjoint UCI HAR partitions contain 6,063, 1,289 and 2,947 observations. Principal components and a single global variance scale are fitted on the training partition, using flattened pixels for the image datasets and the 561 supplied sensor features for UCI HAR. A 32-component diagonal Gaussian mixture is fitted by hard k-means assignments, with component probabilities given by training frequencies and variance floor 10 −6. Source and target observations share the fitted component label during flow training. The velocity network has three width-256 hidden layers with SiLU activation and uses AdamW, learning rate 5 × 10 −4, cosine scheduling and batch size 128. Each method receives 1 .8 × 10 11 charged FLOPs, including source fitting and pairing. Euler trajectories span [0 .001 , 0.999] from a fresh source draw. Each run compares source retention with 20 residual actions crossing λ ∈ { 0.25 , 0.5, 0.75 , 1} and NFE 1, 2, 4, 8, 16. Validation uses up to 3,000 observations and 512 common projection directions, grouped into 16 blocks of 32. An action qualifies when its mean SWD improvement over source retention is at least 1% and its one-sided lower confidence bound is positive. The bound uses a t quantile with 15 degrees of freedom and Bonferroni level 0 .05 /20 on the paired block improvements. Among actions within 1% of the best qualifying mean SWD, selection favours the lowest NFE, then lower SWD and smaller λ; if none qualifies, it retains the source. Test SWD uses the root-mean-square projected discrepancy in Section 3.3, with up to 3,000 samples and 128 directions. The selected action and full flow share the fitted field. The comparators include Gaussian-source independent and minibatch-OT flows, mixture-source independent and component-matched flows, and a one-cycle cluster-transport adaptation 20 . The last method allocates half of the available flow computation to Gaussian pretraining, reverse-integrates 8,192 cluster-stratified training observations for eight steps, fits full-covariance component Gaussians with eigenvalue floor 10 −5, and fine-tunes with within-component minibatch OT. All stages enter the same computation budget. Mean selected SWDs are 0.12004, 0.05422 and 0.10859 for KMNIST, CIFAR-10 features and UCI HAR; source-retention values are 0.12177, 0.05420 and 0.10855. For each dataset, we divide mean full-minus-selected SWD by mean source-retention SWD, then average equally across the three datasets. This normalized improvement is 0.07633, with stratified bootstrap interval [0 .06744 , 0.08532]. Mean selected NFE is 3.556, a 77.8% reduction from 16 for full flow. Dataset-specific means are 6.833, 3.333 and 0.5. ## 8.4 Additional coupling comparisons 

The rare12 mechanism study uses five independent training runs per method to compare independent joint velocity/score training, exact sector coupling, a fitted Laguerre assignment and exact polar coupling. Sector coupling assigns angular widths 2 πw j to target modes; Laguerre cells assign z to the maximizer of z⊤cj + hj ,with offsets fitted to target probabilities. The exact polar construction is given in Section 3.1. All use 3,500 updates and batch size 512. The joint-output baseline has 9,348 parameters; the other three fields have 9,218. The 32-pair comparison matches the two-output network and parameter count. Nearest-mode distance averages each generated point’s distance to its closest target centre; accumulated turning angle measures changes in successive trajectory directions. A six-method comparison uses five independent training runs per method and target for I-CFM, OT-CFM, two-stage rectified flow, Optimal Flow Matching, MeanFlow and MPCF 19,34 . Each method receives 3,500 optimizer updates with batch size 512. I-CFM, OT-CFM, 2-RF and MPCF use the 9,218-parameter network in Section 3.3; MeanFlow adds interval conditioning, giving 9,282 parameters. OT assignments operate on subbatches of 64. Rectification allocates 1,750 updates to each stage and generates training pairs with a 64-evaluation teacher. OFM uses an 8,804-parameter convex-potential network with two width-88 hidden layers, including 200 identity-pretraining updates; its inverse solver uses at most five iterations and tolerance 0.01. Endpoint evaluation uses 3,000 samples and 128 common SWD projections. Vector-field methods also share the benchmark 's Euler interval and numerical reference; MeanFlow uses equal intervals on [0 , 1]. The six requested budgets are 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32; Optimal Flow Matching uses a direct map with one effective evaluation throughout.
