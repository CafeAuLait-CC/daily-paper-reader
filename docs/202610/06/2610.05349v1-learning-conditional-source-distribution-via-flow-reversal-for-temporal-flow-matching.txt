ABSTRACT
We introduce CNP-Flow, a flow matching framework for temporal generation that
learns conditional source distributions through flow reversal. Whereas standard
conditional flow matching (FM) incorporates conditioning through the vector field
and draws source samples from a standard Gaussian, CNP-Flow uses a conditional
noise predictor (CNP) to produce an isotropic Gaussian source for each temporal
condition. The CNP is supervised by source samples obtained through flow reversal,
which maps observed targets backward through a pretrained FM model. A three-
stage pipeline pretrains the FM model, trains the CNP, and fine-tunes the FM
model using the learned source distribution, while preserving the FM backbone
architecture. Across video prediction, video interpolation, and 7-DoF Franka
robot motion planning, CNP-Flow consistently improves generation quality. It
also matches baseline performance with fewer function evaluations. Project page:
https://embodiedai-ntu.github.io/cnpflow

Generative modeling is typically framed as drawing samples from a learned data distribution, where
each generation is independent of the others. However, applications such as video generation (Ho

a more challenging and constrained setup: they must generate a sequence of temporally correlated
samples conditioned on past or future data. This defines the task of temporal generation, which
imposes two requirements: the transitions between samples must follow those of the data distribution,
and each sample must remain structurally consistent with the conditional inputs (Lai et al., 2018;
Ge et al., 2023). Depending on the conditioning, temporal generation can be further divided into
extrapolation and interpolation. Extrapolation predicts future samples given the past (Voleti et al.,
2022; Davtyan et al., 2023), while interpolation generates intermediate trajectories given the start and
end of a sequence (Janner et al., 2022; Carvalho et al., 2023; Reda et al., 2022).
Flow Matching (FM) (Lipman et al., 2023) is an effective framework for generative modeling: a neural
network regresses a deterministic vector field that transports samples from a source distribution to the
target distribution. The source is typically chosen to be simple, such as a standard Gaussian. Thanks
to this general formulation, FM models extend naturally to temporal generation by treating temporal
conditions as additional inputs (Esser et al., 2024; Gkanatsios et al., 2025). While conceptually
simple, their performance depends heavily on the quantity and quality of the conditions injected
into the model (Peebles and Xie, 2023). Existing approaches, therefore, focus on improving the
conditioning mechanism through various inductive biases: randomly masking conditional inputs as a
form of regularization (Voleti et al., 2022; Davtyan et al., 2023) or biasing generated samples toward
conditional data via gradient methods (Ho et al., 2022). However, the former sacrifices fidelity, while
the latter cannot handle multiple conditions simultaneously.
These approaches all target the conditioning mechanism. A separate line of work, however, takes
a different angle: modulating the source distribution itself. Recent studies show that FM models
can be steered toward higher-quality outputs by selecting more suitable source samples (Pooladian
et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2024; Chiang et al., 2026). A common approach in image editing (Meng
et al., 2022; Song et al., 2021) replaces the standard Gaussian noise with a noised version of the
original image, thereby preserving its spatial structure. In text-to-video generation (Ge et al., 2023)
and video editing (Chang et al., 2024), drawing source samples in a directionally correlated manner
across frames improves temporal coherence over drawing them independently per frame. Pushing
this further, recent video prediction methods (Shrivastava and Shrivastava, 2024b;a) replace the
standard Gaussian source with the previous frame. In robotic manipulation, action generation is
largely determined by the source samples, so training a dedicated noise sampler enables adaptation
of pretrained robot policies (Wagenmaker et al., 2025). Together, these results suggest that source
samples themselves carry rich conditional information, which motivates us to ask: can a learned
source distribution also benefit temporal generation?
We introduce Conditional Noise Predictor for Flow Matching (CNP-Flow), a flow matching frame-
work for temporal generation. The core idea is to learn a noise predictor that takes the current
condition as input and outputs a source distribution suited to downstream FM models under that
condition. This formulation naturally encompasses both interpolation and extrapolation (see Fig. 1).
To supervise the noise predictor, we construct pseudo-ground-truth source distributions by retriev-
ing source samples for each condition through flow reversal of a pretrained FM model. A recent
study (Chiang et al., 2026) shows that pretrained FM models map similar target data to nearby source
samples through flow reversal. Motivated by this, we parameterize these distributions as a family of
isotropic Gaussians, with the conditional noise predictor (CNP) outputting their mean and standard
deviation. This sidesteps the exact search for optimal source distributions, which is intractable, while
still providing effective surrogates that substantially improve temporal generation in our experiments.
CNP-Flow consists of a CNP and an FM model, trained via a three-stage pipeline that bootstraps each
model from the other. First, we pretrain an FM model with a standard Gaussian source; flow reversal
through the pretrained model then retrieves surrogate source samples for each condition. Next, we
train the noise predictor by maximizing the likelihood of these source samples under each condition,
enabling it to predict source distributions at test time. Finally, we fine-tune the FM model with the
predicted distributions, refining its vector field. Fig. 2 illustrates this on a toy example: compared
to a vanilla FM, CNP-Flow draws temporally correlated source samples and produces temporally
coherent sequences.

RELATED WORK
Temporal conditions. Generating temporally coherent samples is a central challenge in video
prediction and motion planning. Early approaches based on recurrent networks (Graves, 2012;
Babaeizadeh et al., 2021) and autoregressive models (Vaswani et al., 2017; Le Moing et al., 2021;
Ma et al., 2025) struggle with long-term temporal consistency or computational cost. Recently,
diffusion (Ho et al., 2020; Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021; Song et al., 2021) and flow models (Lipman
et al., 2023), driven by their success in high-fidelity image synthesis, have been extended to video
generation, prediction (Ho et al., 2022; Voleti et al., 2022; Höppe et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023;
Davtyan et al., 2023) and motion planning (Janner et al., 2022; Carvalho et al., 2023; 2025), exploring
the strategies to inject temporal conditions into the model. More specifically, Voleti et al. (2022)
condition on all past and future frames but randomly mask them during training, enabling a single
model to handle prediction, generation, and interpolation. Davtyan et al. (2023) instead condition
on a reference frame and one past frame to reduce training cost. Janner et al. (2022) condition on
the first frame and last frame to generate a feasible trajectory. Alternatively, Ho et al. (2022) bias
the sampling trajectory toward conditioning frames via gradient-based reconstruction guidance, but
the per-condition gradient pass scales poorly when multiple temporal conditions must be satisfied at
once. Despite their differences, these methods all focus on how the condition enters the generation
process, while assuming the source distribution is a fixed standard Gaussian.
Optimized noise prior. A separate line of work shapes the source distribution. In image genera-
tion, noise priors have been optimized through reward functions (Eyring et al., 2024), structured
patterns (Samuel et al., 2024; Mao et al., 2024), pre-collected databases (Mao et al., 2023), or
condition-dependent shifts (Chen et al., 2025). For video synthesis, Ge et al. (2023) introduce tempo-
rally correlated noise across frames, while Luo et al. (2023) and Zhang et al. (2024) model residual
noise between consecutive frames. In video-to-video settings, Chang et al. (2024); Burgert et al.
(2025); Liu and Vahdat (2025) warp the noise to align with the target motion. For video prediction,
Shrivastava and Shrivastava (2024b) and Chen et al. (2024) bypass the standard Gaussian noise by
modeling frame transitions as continuous interpolation. Despite their variety, these methods each
impose a specific noise structure, such as temporal correlations, geometric warping, or frame-wise
residuals, that either degrades over long horizons or sacrifices multimodality (Kim et al., 2026; Lee
et al., 2025; Sadat et al., 2024). In robotic manipulation, Wagenmaker et al. (2025) train a dedicated
noise sampler to adapt a pretrained diffusion policy to new tasks, though their approach operates in
low-dimensional action spaces and its scalability to high-dimensional settings, such as video, remains
unclear. Chiang et al. (2026) show that pretrained flow-matching models map similar targets to nearby
source regions, but their focus is on image generation and optimal transport rather than temporal

PRELIMINARY
Flow matching (FM) is a generative framework that learns to transport samples from a source
distribution p0 to a target distribution p1. A neural network with parameters θ is trained to regress
a time-varying vector field vθ, which defines an ordinary differential equation (ODE): dxt =
vθ(xt, t) dt. Integrating this ODE from t = 0 to t = 1 transports a source sample x0 to a target
sample x1. FM extends naturally to conditional generation by conditioning both the vector field
vθ(xt, t | C) and the target distribution p1(· | C) on a conditioning input C.
A standard training procedure pairs a source sample x0 with a target sample x1, samples a timestep
t ∼U(0, 1), and constructs an intermediate sample by linear interpolation: xt = (1 −t)x0 + tx1.
The model is then optimized with the following objective (Lipman et al., 2023):

4
METHOD
We propose CNP-Flow, a temporal generation framework that augments FM with a learned conditional
source prior. Standard FM models draw source samples from a standard Gaussian and take the
condition as an additional input. However, they often fail to generate high-quality data that faithfully
follows the condition. CNP-Flow reinforces the conditioning mechanism by using a conditional
4


Preprint
noise predictor (CNP) that seeks a source distribution suitable for generating target data under each
condition. Our design is general in two ways: (1) the noise predictor handles different types of
conditioning, including both interpolation and extrapolation, allowing CNP-Flow to unify these tasks
in a single framework; and (2) CNP-Flow only modulates the source distribution supplied to the FM
model without changing its architecture, so it can be integrated into existing FM frameworks.
In the following, we describe the training and inference procedure of CNP-Flow in Sec. 4.1, and its
application to downstream temporal generation tasks in Sec. 4.2.
4.1
TRAINING AND INFERENCE
CNP-Flow is trained in three stages: we first pretrain the FM, then train the CNP from the pretrained
FM, and finally fine-tune the FM under the learned CNP. An overview of the staged training pipeline
is illustrated in Fig. 3. Generation itself is straightforward: CNP-Flow draws a source sample from
the predicted source distribution and transforms it into a target sample via the learned vector field.
Stage 1: Pretraining the FM model. We pretrain the FM model in the standard manner for
conditional generation, using a standard Gaussian N(0, I) as its source distribution. The model
takes the temporal condition CT as an additional input and outputs a deterministic vector field
vθ(xt, t | CT ). In temporal generation tasks, target samples under similar temporal conditions often
share similar structures and dynamics. Since the learned ODE flow is continuous, flow reversal
maps nearby targets to nearby regions in source space, yielding source samples that are clustered by
the temporal condition rather than spread as a standard Gaussian. This suggests a feasible recipe:
construct surrogate per-condition source distributions through flow reversal of the pretrained FM
model, and train a model to predict them.

through flow reversal of the pretrained FM model. For each temporal condition CT , the CNP

4.2
APPLICATION TO TEMPORAL GENERATION TASKS
We consider a series of temporal generation tasks that progressively generate sequential observations
given temporal conditions. This formulation covers video prediction, video interpolation, and motion
planning, which differ primarily in how the temporal condition is constructed.
Video Prediction. Given initial frames x0 and x1, the goal is to autoregressively predict future
frames xi for i = 2, . . . , N. At step i, the preceding frame xi−1 serves as the reference frame, while
a context frame xc is sampled from earlier observations with c ∼U({0, 1, . . . , i −2}), yielding
the temporal condition Ci
T = (xi−1, xc, i −c) that captures both recent dynamics and longer-range
temporal structure.
Video Interpolation. Given boundary frames x0 and xN, the goal is to progressively predict
intermediate frames xi for i = 1, . . . , N −1. At step i, the previously generated frame xi−1 serves
as the reference, while xN provides a fixed future context, giving Ci
T = (xi−1, xN, N −i), where
N −i denotes the remaining temporal distance to the endpoint.
Motion Planning. Given an initial state x0 and a target end-effector pose xN, the objective is to
generate a feasible trajectory that reaches the target pose from the initial state. The model generates
the entire trajectory jointly, conditioned on CT = (x0, xN).
Detailed sampling procedures and algorithms are provided in Appendix A.

source distribution should improve. The mechanism matches the video case: the learned source
places the integration start aligned with the manifold of valid outputs. The modality, dimension, and
failure mode (a robot collision rather than low SSIM) are different.
Inference efficiency. Because µϕ(Ci) is already aligned with the data manifold, the ODE no longer
needs to first traverse the gap from a standard Gaussian before refining—each step is local refinement,
so coarse solvers suffice. Fig. 4 substantiates this on the KTH prediction. Standard Gaussian source
baseline plateaus at PSNR ≈26.5 from NFE = 10 onward; CNP-Flow attains and surpasses that
plateau at NFE = 5 (PSNR 27.48, SSIM 0.86) and continues rising to PSNR ≈28.2 at NFE = 10.
CNP-Flow at NFE = 5 already exceeds the baseline at NFE = 50, an order-of-magnitude reduction
in solver budget; the final quality is superior, so the gain is both efficiency and endpoint improvement.

Limitations and future work. While CNP-Flow improves generation quality and sampling efficiency
across video prediction, video interpolation, and motion planning, one key limitation remains: the
CNP uses a fixed form of temporal condition per task, limiting its use of richer contexts such as
longer-horizon histories. A natural extension is to pair more expressive conditional source families
with a flexible context encoder that absorbs longer-range, task-dependent structure. More broadly,
treating the source distribution as a learnable conditioning channel is orthogonal to vector-field
conditioning and applicable to any conditional FM task.

CONCLUSION
We presented CNP-Flow, a framework for learning conditional source distributions through flow
reversal for temporal generation. By using a pretrained flow model to provide source supervision,
CNP-Flow learns a source distribution adapted to each temporal condition while preserving the FM
backbone architecture. Experiments across video prediction, video interpolation, and 7-DoF Franka
motion planning demonstrate improved generation quality, alongside gains in sampling efficiency.

EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
C.1
VIDEO GENERATION
Compute environment. Video prediction and interpolation experiments are conducted on the AMD
AI & HPC Clusters Taiwan, equipped with AMD Instinct MI300X GPUs with 192GB HBM3 memory
and AMD EPYC 9684X 96-Core processors. GPUs within each node are interconnected through
AMD Infinity Fabric (XGMI). Flow reversal and final evaluation are run on a single 8-GPU MI300X
node, while CNP training and FM fine-tuning use a single MI300X GPU. All AMD experiments use
ROCm 7.2.0. Flow matching pretraining and fine-tuning use BF16 mixed precision.
Training time. The wall-clock runtime varies with dataset size and video resolution. For KTH,
BAIR64, and CLEVRER, the additional offline cost of the CNP-Flow pipeline is approximately
5.5, 9.1, and 17 hours, respectively, relative to vanilla fixed-source FM. On CLEVRER, our largest-
resolution video benchmark, source retrieval via flow reversal takes approximately 3 hours on a single
8-GPU MI300X node, while CNP training and FM fine-tuning take approximately 14 hours on a
single MI300X GPU. Including final evaluation, each video dataset/task completes within 24 hours
on the specified hardware.
Computational overhead. To quantify the additional cost of CNP-Flow at generation time, we
measure inference latency, peak memory, and model FLOPs under same hardware, batch-size, and
ODE-solver settings. As summarized in Tab. 5, CNP-Flow increases inference latency by only about
3.4%. The CNP is evaluated once per generated frame and requires 0.297 GFLOPs; at NFE=10,
this corresponds to approximately 0.34% additional model FLOPs, with about 15 MB additional
peak memory. The 98.08M-parameter FM backbone is augmented with a 47.69M-parameter CNP
(50.84M on CLEVRER). Source samples are retrieved offline via flow reversal and cached, and flow
reversal is never invoked during generation.
Backbone and latent space. All video experiments are performed in the VQ-GAN latent space
released by RIVER (Davtyan et al., 2023). The latent resolution is 8×8 for BAIR64 and KTH, and
16×16 for CLEVRER at 128×128. For video prediction, we reuse the released RIVER prediction

MOTION PLANNING
Compute environment. Motion planning experiments are conducted on a workstation equipped
with NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada generation GPUs with 48GB memory. Each training, flow reversal,
and evaluation run uses a single GPU. We build on the public MPD implementation (Carvalho et al.,
2025) with PyTorch and CUDA. Training uses EMA weights and BF16 automatic mixed precision,
while flow reversal and evaluation are performed in FP32.
Training time. The complete CNP-Flow pipeline completes within 12 hours on this workstation:
fixed-source FM pretraining (∼4 hours), source retrieval and caching via flow reversal (∼1 hour,
run once), CNP training (∼2 hours), and FM fine-tuning (∼4 hours), with the remaining time spent
on final evaluation. The cost of source retrieval and caching is amortized across all subsequent CNP
epochs, and the overall pipeline takes roughly 3× the training time of a vanilla fixed-source FM.
Task and trajectory representation. We evaluate on the Panda Warehouse benchmark from
MPD (Carvalho et al., 2025), which considers 7-DoF Franka Panda joint-space planning with end-
effector pose goals. We use the same demonstration dataset, robot model, collision environment,
and evaluation protocol as MPD. Trajectories are parameterized as cubic B-splines with 22 desired
control points along the trajectory, together with 2 phantom control points used to enforce boundary
conditions, giving 24 learnable control points in total. The spline is evaluated at 128 time samples
over a 10-second horizon.
Backbone architecture. The diffusion baseline and the fixed-source flow matching backbone share
the same temporal U-Net and conditioning architecture. The trajectory backbone predicts 24 learnable
B-spline control points. The U-Net uses a base channel size of 32 with channel multipliers (1, 2, 4, 8).
The start state and goal pose are encoded by separate 2-layer MLPs, and both are projected into a
128-dimensional context embedding.
Motion planning CNP. The motion planning CNP is a residual MLP over vectorized B-spline
trajectory control points. It predicts the mean and log-variance of an isotropic Gaussian source
distribution over the learnable control-point noise. The architecture is summarized in Tab. 11.

MPD baseline uses its cost-guided DDIM sampler, which interleaves denoising with cost-gradient
updates: we follow the paper and use 15 DDIM steps, activate guidance during the final 20% of
the denoising process. Specifically, the last 3 denoising steps each perform nguide = 4 cost-gradient
updates, resulting in 12 gradient updates in total.
For CNP-Flow, we use a 15-step Euler ODE sampler followed by 12 post-hoc cost-gradient refinement
steps, using the same cost terms and weights as MPD. Thus, both methods use 15 generative steps
and 12 cost-gradient updates.
Specifically, after ODE sampling, we run 12 refinement steps with learning rate 0.01, maximum
perturbation 0.15, and value clipping at 1.0, using the same cost terms and weights as MPD. This
keeps the cost-refinement stage matched to the released MPD configuration, while replacing the
diffusion prior with our learned-source flow matching sampler. Flow reversal is used only to construct
CNP supervision and is not invoked during sampling or evaluation. We report the same evaluation
metrics as MPD: Success, Valid fraction, end-effector position/orientation error, and collision intensity.

LARGE-SCALE PHYSICAL WORLD-MODEL BENCHMARK
To evaluate whether the benefit of CNP-Flow extends beyond the low-resolution video benchmarks
and RIVER backbone used in the main experiments, we further evaluate on ACWM-Phys (Xue
et al., 2026), a recent action-conditioned physical world-model benchmark. We use ACWM-DiT-S, a
205M-parameter flow-matching DiT with a frozen Wan2.1 causal video VAE, operating on 240×240
videos.
ACWM-Phys covers four distinct physical regimes: rigid-body dynamics, deformable objects, particle-
based dynamics, and kinematic systems. We select one task from each regime and evaluate both
the in-distribution (IND) and out-of-distribution (OOD) splits. Specifically, we consider push_cube,
push_rope, pour_water, and robot_arm. We compare the standard Gaussian source against CNP-Flow
under the same flow-matching backbone and evaluation protocol. Results are averaged over three
independent seeds and reported using MSE, masked MSE (M-MSE), PSNR, and SSIM.
As shown in Tab. 13, CNP-Flow improves all four reported metrics across every physical regime
on both IND and OOD splits. The improvement varies with the underlying dynamics: the gains
are modest on push_rope, while substantially larger improvements are observed on pour_water and
robot_arm. In particular, on the OOD robot_arm split, CNP-Flow improves PSNR from 22.12 to
24.39 and SSIM from 0.9021 to 0.9511. These results show that the benefit of condition-dependent
source modeling is not specific to the RIVER backbone or 64×64 video generation, and transfers to
a substantially larger flow-matching DiT under distribution shift.

Analysis. This behavior can be understood from three observations: (1) As illustrated in Fig. 2,

which may lead to intersecting ODE trajectories across timesteps and accumulated deviation from

target samples back to nearby source samples, indicating that the pretrained flow preserves local
temporal correspondence in the source space. This motivates learning a conditional source prior
that preserves proximity among source samples under similar conditions, resulting in more coherent
ODE trajectories that better capture the temporal structure of the data. (3) As shown in Fig. 6(b),
CNP-Flow produces a more concentrated source distribution with reduced variance compared to FM,
which contributes to improved stability and accuracy in temporal generation.
