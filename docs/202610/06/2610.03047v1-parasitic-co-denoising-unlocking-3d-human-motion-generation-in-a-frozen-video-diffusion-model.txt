# Parasitic Co-Denoising: Unlocking 3D Human Motion Generation in a Frozen Video Diffusion Model 

# Yunjiao Zhou, Junlang Qian, Lihua Xie, Jianfei Yang 

Nanyang Technological University, Singapore 

Abstract 

Despite never being supervised on explicit 3D motion, large-scale text-to-video diffusion models synthesize realistic hu-man motion in their generated videos. We ask whether this implicit knowledge can be turned into explicit 3D motion generation, without training a separate motion model. Prob-ing a frozen Wan2.1 reveals that a recoverable motion signal is present in its intermediate states across the entire denoising schedule, not confined to the clean output. Motivated by this, we introduce parasitic co-denoising, a paradigm in which mo-tion is decoded from the host model along its denoising sched-ule rather than produced by an independent generator. We instantiate it as the Parasitic Motion Decoder (PMD), an effi-cient flow-matching decoder that shares the host’s noise sched-ule and reads its intermediate features through a σ-adaptive multi-layer fusion, leaving the host unmodified. Drawing its coverage from the host rather than from motion data, PMD leads dedicated motion generators on text-motion alignment at a small fraction of their trainable parameters, while produc-ing paired video and motion in a single pass that motion-only baselines cannot match. 

# Introduction 

Human motion generation (Plappert, Mandery, and Asfour 2016; Yu, Watanabe, and Fujiwara 2026) drives applications such as animation (Mourot et al. 2022), virtual reality (Bur-dea and Coiffet 2003), and embodied AI (Duan et al. 2022). Recent text-to-motion models advance rapidly (Weng et al. 2026; Zeng et al. 2025), yet the scarcity of motion-capture data leaves them unable to cover the long tail of human be-havior. Large-scale video diffusion models (Blattmann et al. 2023), by contrast, train on internet-scale data and render di-verse humans in natural, varied motion. Such motion, learned without any explicit 3D supervision, shows that these mod-els already internalize how humans move. This paper asks whether this implicit knowledge can become explicit 3D mo-tion generation, without training a separate motion model. Recent efforts leverage video models for 3D motion gen-eration (Yang et al. 2025; Lin et al. 2025), jointly training a separate motion branch under the guidance of video features. For this branch to inherit the video model’s generalization, it must be trained on 3D motion at a comparable scale, which drives these methods to contribute large paired video-motion datasets. This effort rebuilds in a separate pipeline a capacity the video model may already possess, and incurs the addi-tional cost of training and storing a second generative model. This suggests a more direct alternative: if motion is already encoded in the video model’s generative process, can it be decoded from it, rather than relearned in a separate model? To answer this, we first ask where motion should be de-coded from. Reading from the model’s clean output trivially recovers motion but reduces the problem to pose estima-tion (Zhou et al. 2023), ignoring the generative process that produced it. The question we pursue is whether motion is already present in the intermediate denoising states them-selves. At each noise level σ, we probe a frozen Wan2.1 video model (Wan et al. 2025) for the alignment between its intermediate state and the corresponding state of HY-Motion (Team 2025), a state-of-the-art open-source text-to-motion flow-matching model (Figure 1). The two are remarkably aligned. Across the entire schedule, the video model’s intermediate states retain 54–81% of the dedicated model’s motion-discriminative accuracy, holding 81% even at σ = 0 .9, where the input is nearly pure noise. Motion information inside the video model is therefore not localized at its clean endpoint. It is present throughout the denoising trajectory, exposing a signal that a motion generator can ride on rather than relearn. The signal exposed above suggests a separate motion gen-erator is unnecessary, and motion can instead be decoded from the video model along its denoising schedule, pro-duced together with the video. We call this strategy parasitic co-denoising . By reusing the host’s features at each step, the motion decoder attains the video model’s broad coverage at little extra cost. This requires more than reading off features at a single point. Such a readout accesses only one slice of the host’s trajectory and discards the rest, contradicting the very finding that motion is present at every noise level. To exploit that presence, the motion generator must itself unfold along the same trajectory, sharing the host’s noise schedule and advancing one step at a time alongside it. The motion decoder therefore advances on the same schedule as the host and conditions each step on the host’s matched-σ features, so that video generation and motion decoding proceed in step. We realize this design as the Parasitic Motion Decoder 

(PMD), a parameter-efficient decoder attached to a frozen Wan2.1 video DiT. PMD is itself a flow-matching decoder sharing the host’s noise schedule. The main contributions are summarized as follows: 

• We empirically show that a frozen text-to-video diffusion model already carries a recoverable 3D human motion signal, decodable from its intermediate states throughout the entire denoising schedule. 

• We introduce parasitic co-denoising , a paradigm in which a secondary modality is decoded from a host model along its own denoising schedule rather than produced by an in-dependent generator, applicable to hosts whose denoising process exposes a usable signal for that modality. 

• PMD leads dedicated motion generators on text-motion alignment while producing paired video and motion in a single pass, at a small fraction of trainable parameters. 

# Related Work 

Text-to-motion generation. Methods for text-conditioned 3D human motion generation can be broadly grouped by representation. Early approaches encode motion in a varia-tional latent (Petrovich, Black, and Varol 2022; Guo et al. 2022b). Diffusion-based methods including MDM (Tevet et al. 2022) and MotionDiffuse (Zhang et al. 2024) denoise raw kinematic sequences, with later work adding physical plausibility (Yuan et al. 2023) and fine-grained control (Xie et al. 2024). Token-based methods such as T2M-GPT (Zhang et al. 2023a) and MoMask (Guo et al. 2024) learn discrete motion tokens with autoregressive or masked transformers, a recipe later scaled with language-model backbones (Jiang et al. 2023). FineMoGen (Zhang et al. 2023b) introduces fine-grained spatio-temporal control, MotionLCM (Dai et al. 2024) accelerates inference via consistency distillation, and MotionCraft (Bian et al. 2025) scales motion training data. 

Reusing pretrained generative models. A growing line of work reuses features or weights of large-scale pretrained generative models for downstream tasks (Yang et al. 2024; Kong et al. 2024). Diffusion features have been shown to sup-port semantic segmentation (Baranchuk et al. 2021), seman-tic correspondence (Tang et al. 2023), monocular depth (Ke et al. 2024), self-supervised representation learning (Xiang et al. 2023), and self-conditioning (Feng, Zhang, and Zhao 2024). In the motion domain, ViMoGen (Lin et al. 2025) uses video features as auxiliary supervision when training a 

1.2B text-to-motion DiT (with a distilled 600 M variant for video-free inference); EchoMotion (Yang et al. 2025) jointly fine-tunes video and motion branches of a dual-modality DiT. MoAlign (Bhowmik et al. 2025) aligns video diffu-sion features with motion-aware subspaces. Most existing approaches either read out features at the clean endpoint of the host’s generation or distill its weights into a separate model. PMD participates in the denoising process itself, de-coding motion from intermediate states at every σ.

# Method 

Motivated by our finding that large-scale video diffusion models implicitly carry a recoverable human motion signal along their denoising trajectories, we tackle text-to-motion generation by attaching a parasitic decoder to a frozen video model. Wan2.1, like other modern video diffusion models, operates as a latent diffusion model (Rombach et al. 2022): a VAE (Kingma and Welling 2013) first encodes the video into a compact latent, and a DiT denoises this latent through a flow-matching schedule indexed by an interpolation coeffi-cient σ ∈ [0 , 1] . Given a text prompt c, the DiT advances the video latent from σ = 1 (Gaussian noise) to σ = 0 (clean video latent), which the VAE then decodes into a video v.Our decoder runs in the same latent paradigm: it advances Figure 2: The structure of Parasitic Motion Decoder (PMD). a motion latent in lockstep with Wan2.1 along the identi-cal schedule, producing a 3D human motion m alongside the video. We instantiate such a decoder in two stages (Fig-ure 2). Stage 1 aligns a motion latent space with video latent space, giving the two streams a shared geometry. Stage 2 trains the parasitic motion decoder (PMD), a flow-matching decoder that shares Wan2.1’s schedule and, at each step, reads features from selected DiT layers, fuses them through a σ-adaptive mechanism, and predicts a motion velocity.

With motion and video sharing the latent space RT ×dz from Stage 1, we now build the parasitic motion decoder (PMD) that denoises a motion latent in lockstep with the video DiT (Figure 2b). Ablation 

Paradigm-critical components. The clearest test of the paradigm is to remove the host signal: PMD collapses to a text-only generator of the same size (Table 3) and both semantic metrics fall sharply. The capability is the host’s, not the decoder’s. The other two components make it reachable. Removing the text condition keeps coarse semantics from the host but loses fine prompt control, and removing Stage 1 alignment is most damaging, leaving host features with no usable motion space and collapsing training. 

Fusion strategy. We next examine how the host signal should be read. A single final-layer hook lags the full model by a wide margin, so the signal is not concentrated at the clean output but spread across the host’s depth. A static uniform mixture narrows the gap yet still trails, since a fixed blend cannot track how layer relevance shifts along the schedule. Figure 4 exposes that shift: the mid-late layer L29 dominates throughout, while early- and late-layer contributions swap as denoising proceeds. Even hooking L29 alone, the strongest layer, falls short of the fusion, and it in turn beats a final-layer hook, so both which layer and how many matter. The motion signal lives in the shifting interplay across layers, and only 

σ-adaptive fusion tracks it. 

Hooked layers. The same picture holds along depth. Shal-low hooks underperform a spread across the host’s full depth, and three full-depth layers already recover most of the gain while shallow ones cannot, so what matters is spanning the trajectory, not the layer count. Motion is present throughout the host’s process, along both the σ axis and the depth of its stack, and PMD works by reading it wherever it lives.

40 ± 12 mm across 50 videos). The host shapes the motion without fixing it, and PMD generates from its own noise rather than reading a predetermined answer off the host. 

# Conclusion 

We presented parasitic co-denoising, a paradigm for generat-ing 3D human motion by decoding it from a frozen video dif-fusion model along its denoising schedule rather than training an independent motion generator. Our instantiation, the Par-asitic Motion Decoder, attaches a flow-matching decoder to a frozen video host through σ-adaptive multi-layer fusion. At a small fraction of the trainable parameters of dedicated motion generators, it leads them on text-motion alignment. We hope this paradigm extends to other modalities whose generation is implicit in larger generative processes. References
