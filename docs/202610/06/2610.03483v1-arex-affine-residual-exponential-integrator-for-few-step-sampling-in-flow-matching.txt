Abstract.
We introduce AREX, a training-free sampler for pretrained flow matching
models that uses the target mean and covariance to capture an analytically tractable
part of the sampling dynamics. We show that the velocity field of the moment-matched
Gaussian target is the L2-optimal affine approximation to the marginal velocity field.
This motivates decomposition of the learned dynamics into an affine component over the
whole sampling path, determined by the first two target moments, and a neural residual
term. AREX keeps the affine component and integrates it using an explicit matrix-valued
propagator. In turn, we only require to integrate over the residual term. This differs from
scalar exponential integrators, which analytically handle only isotropic linear dynamics.
Across image and text-to-image generation tasks, AREX consistently improves sample
fidelity in the few-step sampling regime without retraining the underlying model.

1 Introduction
Flow matching (FM) (Lipman et al., 2022) and related variants are ODE-based generative modeling
frameworks based on dynamical measure transport. Given a prescribed probability path from a
simple reference distribution to the data distribution, FM learns the associated velocity field with
a neural network and generates samples by integrating the resulting ODE. The computational
bottleneck is sampling: resolving the learned flow accurately can require many neural network
evaluations, and efficient few-step generators can reduce the number of function evaluations (NFEs).

There are two main lines of work. On the training side, Mean Flow (Geng et al., 2026a,b) proposes
to learn the average velocity, while Align Your Flow (Sabour et al., 2026) and pi-Flow (Chen et al.,
2026) distill a few-step generator from a teacher. On the sampling side, training-free samplers
such as DDIM (Song et al., 2020), DPM-Solver (Lu et al., 2022), DPM-Solver++ (Lu et al., 2025),
DPM-Solver-v3 (Zheng et al., 2023), DEIS (Zhang and Chen, 2022), UniPC (Zhao et al., 2023),
CAB (Roy and Nair, 2026), A-FloPS (Jin et al., 2026), and STORK (Tan et al., 2026) can be
deployed to any pretrained model without further training. Several of them have a similar structure.
DDIM, the DPM-Solver family, DEIS, UniPC, A-FloPS and CAB split the generative dynamics
into a linear part, which can be integrated exactly, and a residual part, which can be integrated by
a quadrature. The linear part is typically a scalar multiple of the identity. In A-FloPS the scalar
is fixed by the noise schedule first and then refit to the trajectory at every step. However, these
methods ignore the underlying data geometry and the fact that the distribution a sampler moves
towards can be strongly anisotropic. Since the scalar linear part could not help in capturing the
anisotropy, the neural network is exposed to the larger challenge of dealing with both the anisotropy
and the residual part.
In this work, we focus on the sampling side and ask:
How can we leverage both the analytical structure of the flow dynamics and the geometry of
the target data to design a better training-free sampler in the low-NFE regime?
To tackle this question we use the insight that the first two moments of the target distribution
induce a nontrivial part of the flow dynamics. The target covariance describes how the data vary
along different directions. Replacing the target by a Gaussian with the same mean and covariance
preserves this directional information while making the corresponding velocity field affine. Along
each eigenvector of the covariance, the dynamics then expand or contract at a rate determined
by the corresponding eigenvalue. For a general target, this affine field is the L2-optimal affine
approximation to the population velocity field at every time. It also admits a closed-form solution.
Since the same target covariance determines the principal directions throughout the trajectory, the
dynamics decouple along these directions and can be solved independently.
This gives us a principled affine component that does not need to be approximated numerically. We
introduce Affine–Residual Exponential Integrator (AREX), which decomposes the learned velocity
field into this moment-matched affine component and a residual term. AREX solves the affine
part analytically and applies numerical quadrature only to the residual, so the direction-dependent
dynamics captured by the target covariance do not have to be recovered using network evaluations.
We use an Adams–Bashforth (AB) scheme that reuses residual evaluations from previous steps
while requiring only one new network evaluation per step. Gaussian teleportation (Wang and
Vastola, 2024) similarly exploits a Gaussian approximation, but only over the initial part of the
trajectory before switching to a conventional solver; AREX retains the affine component over the
full trajectory. We further extend this construction to conditional generation with AREX-C, using
the within-condition covariance for the affine dynamics and estimating the condition-specific mean
using the first network evaluation. Figure 2 illustrates the resulting few-step behavior.
Our main contributions are as follows.
• Method. We introduce AREX, a training-free exponential integrator that uses the target mean
and covariance to separate a moment-matched affine component from the learned velocity field.
AREX solves this affine component analytically and applies Adams–Bashforth quadrature only
to the remaining residual, requiring one new network evaluation per step. We further extend the
construction to conditional generation with AREX-C using a shared within-condition covariance
and a condition-specific mean estimated using the first network evaluation.
• Theory. We provide theoretical results and organize them under three principles to justify
AREX. We show that the moment-matched affine field is L2-optimal (Proposition 1), connect
the residual term to non-Gaussian score correction (Proposition 2), derive a closed-form formula

2 AREX: Affine–Residual Exponential Integrator for Sampling
In flow matching, we generate a sample by integrating the learned velocity field vθ:
˙xt = vθ(t, xt),
x0 ∼N(0, Id).
(1)
When only a few network evaluations are possible, the numerical solver must approximate the ODE
trajectory using a coarse time grid. In this work, our aim is to address this problem, introducing a
method to make it easier to integrate, without changing or retraining vθ. To do so, we borrow a
standard idea behind exponential integrators: split the dynamics into a part that can be solved
exactly and a residual that must be approximated numerically (Hochbruck and Ostermann, 2010).
The choice of this split determines how much work is left to the numerical solver.
We advocate using the first two moments of the target distribution to identify an affine component
of the dynamics that has an exact solution. To see why these quantities are useful, recall that
the source distribution is isotropic, while the target generally is not. Its covariance describes the
directions in which the data vary and the scale of that variation. Along the flow matching path,
these directions must therefore expand or contract by different amounts as the source is transported
toward the target. This direction-dependent motion is already present before accounting for any
finer non-Gaussian structure of the data. For a Gaussian target, the mean and covariance completely
determine the flow, and the resulting velocity field is affine. More importantly, this affine flow
can be integrated in closed form. This suggests using the Gaussian with the same mean and
covariance as the target to capture the part of the learned dynamics that is explained by its global
geometry. AREX leverages this idea by integrating the corresponding affine flow exactly and
applying numerical quadrature only to the residual between the affine and learned velocity fields.

2.1 Target moments describe direction-dependent dynamics
We now make this intuition precise for the linear flow matching path used throughout this work.
Let p0 := N(0, Id) and p1 denote the source and target distribution. Given X0 ∼p0, X1 ∼p1, with
X0 and X1 independent, we connect the two distributions through the linear interpolation
Xt = (1 −t)X0 + tX1.
(2)
We denote the target mean by m1 = E[X1] and its covariance by Σ1 = Cov(X1). The covariance
describes how the target varies across directions. To characterize this directional variation, we
compute its eigendecomposition Σ1 = QΛQ⊤, with eigenvalues Λ = diag(λ1, . . . , λd). The columns
of Q give the principal directions of the target, while λj gives its variance along direction j. When
these variances differ, the target is anisotropic, with different scales along different directions. This
direction-dependent structure also appears along the flow path. In direction j, the variance of Xt is
σj(t)2 = (1 −t)2 + t2λj.
(3)
Thus, different directions expand or contract at different rates as the source is transported toward
the target. A scalar linear term cannot capture this behavior because it applies the same scaling in
every direction. This motivates choosing an affine component that captures this direction-dependent
structure before applying numerical approximation.

2.3 Integrating the residual

The first term is the exact affine solution derived above. The only quantity that remains to be

residual.

4 Empirical Results
In this section, we provide empirical results to evaluate the effectiveness of AREX(-C) on CIFAR-10,
ImageNet-256, and MJHQ-30K. All experiments are run on an NVIDIA GH200 GPU (120GB). We
further provide the details, wall clock analysis, ablation study and additional results in App. E.

For CIFAR-10, we use the I-CFM model from Tong et al. (2023) as backbone for the unconditional
model in pixel space, and evaluate FID using 50K generated samples (Krizhevsky et al., 2009).

5 Conclusion
We introduced AREX and AREX-C, training-free samplers for flow matching and justify the design
through three principles: (i) use the data statistics to define the affine backbone, (ii) integrate
the backbone dynamics exactly, and (iii) use the NFE budget only for numerically resolving the
residual. This gives a complementary perspective on few-step generation. Instead of requiring the
flow to be straight or simple, AREX exploits structured dynamics that can be solved analytically
and spend the NFE budget only on the residual. Across CIFAR-10, ImageNet-256, and MJHQ-30K,
this training-free strategy consistently improves generation fidelity in the low-NFE regime. More
broadly, our results suggest that effective few-step sampling can be achieved by principled separation
of the tractable structure from genuinely difficult residual dynamics at inference time.

A Related Work
Efficient few-step sampling has been studied extensively for diffusion and flow-based generative
models, through both improved numerical integration at inference time and training procedures
that simplify the learned dynamics.
Schedule-aware solvers and residual quadrature.
DDIM (Song et al., 2020), DPM-Solver, DPM-
Solver++ (Lu et al., 2025), DEIS (Zhang and Chen, 2022), and UniPC (Zhao et al., 2023) exploit
analytical structure induced by the diffusion schedule and improve the approximation of the
remaining network-dependent dynamics. CAB (Roy and Nair, 2026) similarly rectifies the dynamics
and improves their multistep quadrature without additional NFEs. These methods primarily exploit
scalar, schedule-induced structure. AREX instead introduces a data-dependent anisotropic affine
backbone determined by the target covariance and applies Adams–Bashforth quadrature only to its
residual. Under the linear flow matching interpolant, the covariance-free first-order specialization
reduces to the Euler/DDIM-type update.
Adaptive linear decompositions.
DPM-Solver-v3 (Zheng et al., 2023) and A-FloPS (Jin et al., 2026)
also seek more favorable linear–nonlinear decompositions. DPM-Solver-v3 adapts coordinatewise
linear coefficients using empirical statistics of the pretrained network, while A-FloPS fits a scalar
linear component locally from the evolving trajectory. AREX differs in both source and structure:
its backbone is the L2-optimal affine approximation to the population velocity field and is generally
matrix-valued, with eigendirections determined by the target covariance. Thus these methods
extract exploitable structure from the network or numerical trajectory, whereas AREX derives it
from the second-order geometry of the target distribution.
Gaussian analytical structure and complementary solvers.
The closest conceptual connection is
Gaussian teleportation (Wang and Vastola, 2024), which exploits the observation that diffusion
scores at sufficiently high noise are well approximated by a moment-matched Gaussian score and
uses its analytical dynamics to skip an initial portion of sampling. AREX instead retains the
moment-matched affine dynamics throughout the entire trajectory and numerically integrates
only the non-Gaussian residual; AREX-C extends this construction to conditional generation.
Orthogonally, STORK (Tan et al., 2026) improves coarse-step stability through stabilized Runge–
Kutta schemes and virtual stages. This targets numerical stability rather than the identification of
an analytically solvable anisotropic component, and is therefore complementary to AREX.
Finally, AREX provides a complementary perspective to training-side approaches that promote
straighter flows for few-step generation. Existing methods often seek straighter learned trajectories,
motivated by the reduced discretization error of first-order solvers (Tong et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2022).
AREX instead takes a training-free view: not all trajectory curvature needs to be removed. Its
affine backbone can generate substantial acceleration while remaining exactly solvable. Accordingly,
the leading local error of AREX is governed by the material derivative of the residual rather than
by the acceleration of the full trajectory. AREX therefore replaces global flow straightness with the
weaker requirement that the unresolved residual dynamics be temporally simple.

D Additional Discussions
In this section, we provide additional discussions on AREX.
D.1 Why Not Other Couplings?
While the affine-residual construction extends to general endpoint coupling with finite second
moments through the endpoint cross-covariance, the independent-coupling setting used here is
particularly simple: the cross-covariance vanishes, and with an isotropic source covariance the
time-dependent affine coefficients are functions of the target covariance alone, so they share a
fixed eigenbasis and admit a closed-form propagator. For a general coupling, the endpoint cross-
covariance must also be estimated, and the resulting time-dependent affine operators need not
commute, so such a simultaneous diagonalization is generally not possible. As a special case, if the
moment-matched Gaussian endpoints are coupled by OT with quadratic cost, the resulting path
is the Bures-Wasserstein geodesic (Peyré, 2025). Note also that the cross-covariance of the OT
coupling is generally unavailable without first solving or approximating the OT problem.

E Experimental Details and Additional Results
E.1 Experimental Settings
In this section, we provide details on the settings of the experiments in Sec. 4. All samplers are
applied to the same pretrained checkpoints; no model is trained or fine-tuned. On each benchmark,
every sampler uses the same noise seeds and the same FID code.
Benchmarks.
We use three benchmarks. They differ in conditioning and in the space where
the flow is integrated, so that the same sampler is tested in three cases: the unconditional task,
the class-conditional task and the text conditional task. On CIFAR-10 Krizhevsky et al. (2009),
we use the unconditional I-CFM checkpoints released with TorchCFM Tong et al. (2023). On
ImageNet-256 Russakovsky et al. (2015), we use SiT-XL/2 Ma et al. (2024) in the SD-VAE space
with classifier free guidance at its published scale w = 1.5. On MJHQ-30K Li et al. (2024), we use
SANA-0.6B at 512px Xie et al. (2024) with classifier free guidance w = 4.5, following the protocol
of Tan et al. (2026). The settings are shown in Table 5.
Evaluation.
FID is computed against the training set with pytorch-fid on CIFAR-10, with the
ADM reference batch and evaluator in ImageNet-256, and with the reference statistics of the SANA
toolkit in MJHQ-30K. We also report sFID, Inception score, precision, and recall in Table 18. One
NFE is one evaluation of the velocity field that the sampler integrates. With classifier-free guidance,
this is one evaluation of the guided field, computed as one batched forward pass that returns the
conditional and the unconditional prediction; the two branches are not counted separately. The
same convention applies to every sampler and to the cost tables of Appendix E.3.

released implementation, or the implementation in diffusers, with the hyperparameters that its
paper reports for the backbone at hand, or the defaults of the code when the paper reports none.
These settings are never searched. The second level is a small set of discrete options that each
method exposes, such as the solver order. These options are selected per NFE on the held-out split
described below, and they are the only quantities that are tuned. Table 6 lists the implementations
and the options, and Table 7 lists the selected values. For DPM-Solver-v3, the empirical model
statistics are estimated on the same checkpoint with exact Jacobian-vector products, using the
authors’ settings: N = 1200 timesteps and K = 1024 samples on CIFAR-10, and N = 250 and
K = 1024 on SiT-XL/2 and SANA. The time grids differ by method. On CIFAR-10 and SiT-XL/2,
Euler, DPM-Solver++, UniPC, CAB, STORK and AREX run on the uniform grid in t from 10−3
to 1. A-FloPS applies its own time shift to this grid, which is the identity on CIFAR-10 (shift
1) and moves the interior nodes on SiT-XL/2 (shift 3). DPM-Solver-v3 constructs its own grid
between t = 10−3 and t = 1 −10−3, uniform in t or in log-SNR according to the option selected
per NFE. On SANA, DPM-Solver++, UniPC, CAB and AREX-C run on the factory grid of the
released pipeline, STORK and A-FloPS use the grids constructed by their own schedulers, and
DPM-Solver-v3 uses its own grid, uniform in t. Thus, equal NFE means an equal number of
evaluations of the same field, not identical grids.
Two cases deserve a comment. On SANA, DPM-Solver++ and UniPC are the factory schedulers of
the released pipeline and run at solver order 2 at every NFE. On CIFAR-10 and SiT-XL/2, their
order is selected per NFE, and the selected order is 1 at NFE 2, 2 at NFE 4, and 3 from NFE 8 on.
Thus the label 2M applies to the SANA rows only. STORK-4 on SANA is run in the configuration
of its paper for this backbone, namely s = 9 with the first-order Taylor expansion on the scheduler’s
own grid, at every NFE. Its paper reports this configuration from NFE 7 on. The entries at NFE
4–6 in Table 3 are the same configuration applied below that range, with no adaptation. On
CIFAR-10 and SiT-XL/2, (s, Taylor order) is selected per NFE from {5, 9, 14} × {1, 2}.

E.2 Moment and Profile Estimation Details
Estimating the moments.
For AREX on CIFAR-10, moments m1 and Σ1 are estimated on pixel
level from training set with flips, 100k images in total. For AREX-C, µ(c) is the sample mean of
the condition c. And ¯S is the average over the conditions of the unbiased per-condition covariance,
so that Σ1 = ¯S + Cov(µ(c)). On SiT-XL/2, we estimated the moments of 2562334 latent elements
encoded by VAE from all 1281167 ImageNet training images and their flips. On SANA we could
not gain access to the training set, so we first do a self sampling for 2000 template prompts and
25 samples for each prompt. Self sampling uses the checkpoints own pipeline and precision with
factory grid, 20 sampling steps and w = 4.5.
Eigendecomposition.
The covariance is decomposed with a symmetric eigen solver from torch on
GPU (torch.linalg.eigh). On one GH200, forming and decomposing it takes 0.1s at d = 3072
for CIFAR-10, 0.13s at d = 4096 for SiT-XL/2 and 0.7s at d = 8192 for SANA, scales as d3. Due to
time and computation resource constraint we have not tested AREX in backbones with larger d.
For d ≳105, the dense decomposition is not eligible any more. In future work we plan to apply
top-k rank estimator by randomized PCA, which can reduce the computational cost to O(dNk).

The residual profile estimation.
The residual profile ϕ is measured once per checkpoint. We
integrate the model with Euler on the uniform grid of the sampler with 50 steps and record the
residual at every step in the eigenbasis of the covariance used by the sampler. The profile is the
root-mean-square of these residuals over the trajectories. On CIFAR-10 we use 2048 trajectories. On
the guided models, the trajectories follow the guided field, and the affine center of each trajectory is
determined from its first network evaluation, as in AREX-C. On SiT-XL/2 we use 2048 trajectories
whose classes are spread over all 1000 classes, and on SANA we use 1024 of the synthetic template
prompts. In both cases the trajectories are pooled into a single profile that is shared across
conditions. Thus, the residual quadrature weights are precomputed once and reused for every
condition.
E.3 Wall-Clock Cost
NFE is the cost axis of every table above, and the question we will discuss in this part is what the
sampler adds per step beyond the network evaluation, and what it costs once per checkpoint. All
timings are on GH200 at the batch size and precision each benchmark script uses, and take the
best of 20 repetitions after warm-up.
Inside the evaluation step, an AREX steps including one network evaluation, and adds two changes
of basis (x →U⊤x and back), the propagator vG and kernel weighted residual. All of the matrix
vector products have O(d2) complexity and the arithmetic has O(d) complexity. And the weights
are read from a table computed once pre grid. We present the overhead per sample against the
evaluation comparing to DPM-Solver++ in Table
10. Here DPM-Solver++ stands for every
baseline, as none of them changes basis, and what they do per step are beyond the network call.
UniPC and CAB use multi-step combinations, STORK uses virtual stages, A-FloPS uses least
square fits, DPM-Solver-V3 uses coefficient interpolation. AREX’s overhead is about 0.3 −1.5% of
a network call.
Beyond the network evaluation step, AREX need a offline evaluation to get a moments and the

at NFE 8, and at NFE 32 all residual integrators except IF-RK4 lie within 0.35 FID of each other.
Second, the exponential form is ahead of the integrating-factor form at every NFE up to 16 for
both stage counts, by a wide margin for k = 4. At NFE 32, IF-RK2 is slightly ahead of ETD-RK2.
Thus, at the smallest budgets the multistep rule is the better use of the evaluations.
