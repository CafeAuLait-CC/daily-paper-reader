## Abstract 

Generative modeling of graph-structured data is crucial for tasks ranging from drug discovery to social network simulation. Among these models, denoising diffusion models have achieved great success in graph generation by learning to progressively reverse a process that adds noise to the original graph. However, the standard noise-prediction approach of diffusion models is suboptimal for graph data. The goal for a graph generative model is to learn the clean graphs’ topological properties, such as connectivity and degree distribution. Because a diffusion model that predicts noise does not explicitly learn these topological properties, it is challenging for the model to output graphs with the desired structural statistics. To address this challenge, we introduce Direct Graph Flow Matching (DiGFM) , a novel graph transformer model guided by two goals: predict clean graphs and improve sampling efficiency. Distinct from the prevailing diffusion approach, DiGFM employs a continuous flow-matching paradigm and integrates direct graph prediction. Specifically, DiGFM maps the prior noise distribution to the clean graph distribution via a multi-step process: the model repeatedly predicts the underlying clean graph, and a transformation is employed to convert the model output to the velocity vector that points in the direction toward the clean graph distribution. This design enables DiGFM to generate high-quality samples using only 2.5% to 15.6% of the steps required by diffusion-based models, which leads to a 5.3 × to 257 × speedup in wall-clock inference time. Experiments demonstrate that DiGFM outperforms or matches prior state-of-the-art models across general graph benchmarks and molecular datasets, generating graphs with strong adherence to ground-truth structural statistics at significantly faster inference speeds. 

## 1 Introduction 

Graph generative models have advanced rapidly, with diffusion models achieving great success across diverse domains such as molecular generation [1], protein design [2], neural architecture search [3], social network analysis [4], and combinatorial optimization [5]. These models generate graphs by learning to reverse a forward process that progressively corrupts the original data with noise [6, 7]. Typically, this reversal is accomplished by having the network predict either the added noise or the original clean graph. The prevailing approach is noise prediction [6], but it is suboptimal for graph data for two reasons. First, the primary goal of a graph generative model is to learn graph topological properties, such as connectivity and degree distribution. Diffusion models that predict noise do not explicitly reconstruct these properties, making it challenging for them to output graphs with faithful structural statistics. Instead, a diffusion model that directly predicts the original clean graph can generate more accurately. Second, noise prediction suffers from a curse of dimensionality rooted in the manifold hypothesis [8, 9]. This hypothesis states that real-world data lies roughly on a low-dimensional manifold. Since clean graphs possess specific topological structures, they approximately lie on a low-dimensional manifold. In contrast, noise (e.g., Gaussian noise in diffusion) is off-manifold and distributed across the entire high-dimensional space. Because our model directly predicts clean graphs (low-

dimensional) rather than noise (high-dimensional), it learns the topological properties of the graph more easily and achieves significantly greater sampling efficiency. While the approach of predicting clean graphs has been explored by GruM [10], which predicts a weighted mean of clean graph structures, its underlying diffusion mixture approach presents limitations. GruM relies on Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes [11], which induce curved, stochastic trajectories from noise to data. As a result, it requires a large number of sampling steps (e.g., 1,000) during inference to accurately approximate these curved trajectories and maintain generation quality. To overcome these limitations, we introduce Direct Graph Flow Matching (DiGFM) , a novel generative model that directly predicts clean graphs and leverages the recent flow matching paradigm [12] to significantly improve sampling efficiency. Unlike prior work, DiGFM decouples the model’s output space (clean graph) from the loss space (velocity in flow matching). Specifically, given a noisy graph, DiGFM directly outputs a clean graph prediction; this prediction is transformed into a velocity vector, which an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) solver uses to take a step toward the next, less noisy version of the graph. Our experiments demonstrate that DiGFM outperforms or matches existing generative models across diverse datasets while requiring only a fraction of their sampling steps. On general graph benchmarks (Community [13], Ego [14], Stochastic Block Model [15], and Enzymes [16]), DiGFM outperforms prior models on the majority of the metrics, which cover both classical structural metrics (degree, clustering, orbit MMDs) and neural-based metrics (FID and RBF MMD). Notably, DiGFM achieves lower values for the average maximum mean discrepancy and neural-based metrics using only 15 to 20 sampling steps—just 2.5% to 15.6% of the steps required by previous state-of-the-art models. On molecular generation, DiGFM achieves a validity of 99.82% on QM9 [17] and 98.73% on ZINC250k [18], which indicates that almost all of the generated molecules are chemically valid without post hoc correction. Furthermore, DiGFM naturally supports conditional generation, and it achieves state-of-the-art performance on generating QM9 molecules conditioned on the dipole moment and on the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO). Our main contributions can be summarized as follows: • We introduce DiGFM, a generative model that directly predicts clean graphs in order to capture topological properties better than standard noise-prediction models. • We develop a framework that integrates direct graph prediction with the flow matching paradigm. • Our model outperforms or matches prior state-of-the-art models on general graph benchmarks and molecular datasets while achieving a 5.3 × to 257 × wall-clock inference speedup by using fewer sampling steps. ## 2 Related Work 

Recent advancements in graph generative modeling are predominantly driven by diffusion and flow-based frameworks. These models can be categorized into continuous and discrete approaches. Continuous models operate on node and edge attribute matrices in continuous space (i.e., Rn×dx and 

Rn×n×de ), aiming to denoise initial Gaussian matrices into the target graph’s attributes. In contrast, discrete models are designed specifically for graphs with categorical node and edge attributes. They aim to denoise the prior probability distributions to recover the actual probability distributions of discrete node and edge types. 

Continuous Graph Diffusion/Flow Models. Table 1 summarizes the prediction spaces of the continuous models. Graph models based on the standard Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) framework [6] predict the added noise at each timestep. Similarly, score-based models [7] predict the score, which is proportional to the negative of the noise. Models based on diffusion bridges [19] and flow matching [12, 20, 21] predict the drift or velocity, which mathematically represents the difference between the clean graph and noise. Continuous graph models operate in either the original graph space or in a compressed latent space via an autoencoder. While latent space models [22–24] offer a unique paradigm, their reliance on autoencoders can introduce reconstruction artifacts. Specifically, the autoencoder may be unable to distinguish between certain types of vertices [24], and strict structural properties (e.g., planarity) can be violated through a single edge reconstruction error. Since our primary objective is to generate 2topological structures with high fidelity, DiGFM operates directly in the graph space to bypass potential autoencoder bottlenecks. Among continuous graph models, only GruM [10] and our DiGFM directly predict the clean graph rather than noise, velocity, or related quantities. This direct prediction encourages both models to capture target structural statistics more accurately. However, GruM relies on a diffusion mixture framework [19], which induces highly curved sampling trajectories. By leveraging the flow matching framework, inspired by recent success in direct pixel-space image generation [9], DiGFM is able to generate high-quality graphs with significantly fewer sampling steps than GruM.

Discrete Graph Diffusion/Flow Models. Table 2 summarizes the prediction spaces of discrete models, which are an important parallel line of research to continuous models. Notably, these models predict the clean graph, since they output categorical probability distributions for every node and edge type and are trained to match the clean graph’s node and edge categories via cross-entropy loss. While the discrete approach is prevalent among graph diffusion/flow models, we choose a continuous framework for DiGFM due to three primary advantages. First, the continuous approach naturally allows our model to generate graphs with both continuous and discrete attributes; in contrast, discrete approaches require added architectural components, e.g. [29], to generate both continuous and categorical features. Second, operating in continuous space allows DiGFM to straightforwardly adopt advancements in image-domain diffusion/flow models, such as integrating classifier-free guidance [30] for conditional molecular generation (e.g., generating QM9 molecules with specific dipole moments). Third, in continuous space, we can leverage numerical ODE solvers [31] with large step sizes to approximate the trajectory from noise to the clean graph. In practice, this leads to greater sampling efficiency compared to the standard Markov chains [32] used in discrete flow models.

## 3 Direct Graph Flow Matching 

In this section, we present our Direct Graph Flow Matching (DiGFM) model. We first formulate the graph generation problem, then introduce our novel integration of direct graph prediction into the flow matching framework, and finally explain the graph-specific architectural designs required to make this approach work well. 

3.1 Problem Formulation 

The objective of graph generation is to learn a generative model that maps a prior noise distribution 

pnoise to a target data distribution pdata of realistic graphs. Once trained on a given dataset, this model can be efficiently sampled to generate diverse, novel graphs that share the structural and topological properties of the original graphs. Consider an n-vertex graph G, which can be undirected or directed. We represent G by its node attribute matrix X ∈ Rn×dx and its edge attribute matrix E ∈ Rn×n×de , where dx and de are the dimensionalities of the node and edge attributes, respectively. These attributes can be either continuous or discrete. Continuous attributes, such as edge weights, are represented by their direct values within the attribute matrices. Discrete attributes are encoded using a one-hot representation 3within these matrices. Thus, the learning objective is to approximate a continuous vector field that transports initial Gaussian noise matrices (X0, E0) into realistic clean attribute matrices (X1, E1).

3.2 Direct Graph Generation via Flow Matching 

To address the problem of graph generation, we integrate direct, clean graph prediction into the flow matching framework. 

Flow Matching Framework. Let x = ( X, E) denote the clean graph sampled from pdata , where 

X and E are the node and edge attribute matrices, respectively. Similarly, let ϵ = ( ϵX , ϵE ) denote Gaussian noise sampled from pnoise with the same tensor shapes. We define a forward process that constructs a noisy sample zt = ( Xt, Et) at any time step t ∈ [0 , 1] via a linear interpolation: 

zt = tx + (1 − t)ϵ.

Here, all scalar operations apply component-wise, meaning Xt = tX + (1 − t)ϵX and Et =

tE + (1 − t)ϵE . Under this formulation, zt represents pure noise at time t = 0 and the clean graph at time t = 1 . The flow velocity v = ( vX , vE ) is the time-derivative of zt: specifically, 

v = ddt zt = x − ϵ. Flow-based methods [12, 20, 21] train a neural network vθ (zt, t ) to predict this velocity. Since zt is a tuple, the network takes both Xt and Et as input and outputs predicted velocities for both nodes and edges. The model is trained by minimizing the loss function: 

L = Et, x,ϵ ∥vθ (zt, t ) − (x − ϵ)∥2. (1) where the squared difference decomposes into the sum of the squared differences over the node and edge components. Once the model has been trained, samples can be generated by solving the ODE  

> ddt

zt = vθ (zt, t ), starting from noise at t = 0 and ending at a generated graph at t = 1 . This can be done by numerical methods such as the Euler or Heun ODE solvers. 

Adapting to Direct Clean Graph Prediction. While predicting velocity or noise is standard practice for flow matching and diffusion models (which were initially developed for image generation), graph topologies are very sensitive to noise and intermediate continuous states. As noted earlier, by predicting the clean graph instead, the model is forced to explicitly reconstruct topological properties. Further, it avoids the inefficiency of predicting high-dimensional, off-manifold targets like noise ϵ

or velocity v, since velocity v = x − ϵ inherits the high dimensionality of noise. Thus, we train DiGFM to directly generate the clean graph x.To do so, we decouple the loss space ( v-space) from the model’s output space ( x-space). Given the constraints zt = tx + (1 − t)ϵ and v = x − ϵ, we can solve for the velocity in terms of the clean graph x and the current noisy state zt, yielding v = 11−t (x − zt). This allows us to define the transformation: 

vθ (zt, t ) := 11−t (xθ (zt, t ) − zt). (2) Thus, DiGFM can directly predict the clean graph xθ ; this predicted clean graph is transformed into velocity vθ via equation (2); finally, the model is optimized using the flow matching objective (1). Substituting equation (2) into (1) reveals an alternative perspective on DiGFM’s loss function: 

L = Et, x,ϵ 1(1 −t)2 ∥xθ (zt, t ) − x∥2.

This expression can be interpreted as a reweighted form of L2 loss measuring the deviation between the model’s predicted clean graph and the ground-truth clean graph. The factor 1(1 −t)2 assigns smaller weights near t = 0 (where inferring the clean graph from pure noise is extremely difficult) and larger weights as t → 1 (where the network must perfect the final graph topology). 

3.3 Graph-Tailored Network Architecture and Inductive Biases 

DiGFM uses a graph transformer [25, 35] as its backbone model. Specifically, the model takes the noisy graph Gt = ( Xt, Et) and time step t as input, and it outputs the predicted clean graph G1.To adapt this architecture for direct flow-based graph prediction, we introduce several crucial graph-specific inductive biases (details in Appendix A): • Output Bounding: Since the model’s output xθ (zt, t ) should be a clean graph, we apply a sigmoid activation to the predicted edge matrix directly after the final transformer block. This bounds the model predictions to the interval [0 , 1] , which helps prevent out-of-distribution values before converting to velocity via equation (2). 4• Higher-Order Structural Features: To help the model reconstruct the overall graph topology, we concatenate higher-order powers of the edge matrix (e.g., Et and E2 

> t

) during the forward pass, which provides information about multi-hop vertex neighborhoods. • Laplacian Spectral Node Features: For graph datasets without intrinsic node attributes, we define the clean target node attribute matrix X ∈ Rn×k as the top k Laplacian eigenvectors computed from the ground-truth clean graph. DiGFM jointly predicts these spectral node features X and the clean edge matrix E, which provides additional structural supervision when generating the target graph topology. 

3.4 Training and Sampling Pipeline Training Algorithm. The pseudocode of the training algorithm is in Algorithm 1. For undirected graph generation, we sample the noise for the adjacency matrix to be symmetric with diagonal entries equal to zero. This ensures that the initial noise graph G0 is undirected and has no self-loops. Also, to prevent division by zero in line 7 of the algorithm, we cap the flow matching time step t to be at most 

(Kmax − 1) /K max , where Kmax denotes the maximum intended number of sampling steps at inference. With our default Kmax = 100 , t is clipped at 0.99. This numerical bounding does not affect the final generated graphs, because during inference, our K-step ODE solver only evaluates the velocity field at time steps t ∈ { 0, 1/K, . . . , (K − 1) /K }. Thus, it never queries t > (Kmax − 1) /K max , so the sampling trajectory and output graph are unaffected (see Appendix C for empirical validation). To stabilize training, we apply an adaptive Exponential Moving Average (EMA) [31] to the model weights. We scale the EMA decay rate based on the number of processed graphs; this allows the model weights to update rapidly early in training and change more slowly as training progresses. More details about the EMA and Algorithm 1 are in Appendix A. 

Sampling Algorithm. To generate new graphs using DiGFM, we sample initial noise (X0, E0) and solve the ODE ddt (Xt, Et) = vθ (( Xt, Et), t ) from t = 0 to t = 1 . At each integration step, we predict xθ, convert it to vθ via equation (2), and step forward using an Euler solver (Algorithm 2). Since DiGFM operates in continuous time, the number of sampling steps K can be flexibly adjusted. Finally, continuous representations of discrete categories are mapped back to categorical labels via thresholding (e.g., quantize the values to 1 if > 0.5, else 0, for a binary adjacency matrix).

Conditional Generation. The preceding discussion is for unconditional graph generation. DiGFM also supports conditional generation, where the generated graph is conditioned on a specific numerical property or a categorical label y. We adopt the classifier-free guidance (CFG) method for conditioning [30]. During training, we drop the label y for a percentage of the data (e.g., 10%), replacing it with a null token ∅ to train an unconditional baseline xθ (Gt, t, ∅) alongside the conditional model 

xθ (Gt, t, y ). During sampling, we apply Algorithm 2, except the velocity prediction (line 7) becomes a linear combination of the conditional and unconditional velocities: 

vpred ← vuncond + s · (vcond − vuncond ),

where s is the CFG scale. Setting s = 1 yields pure conditional generation, while increasing to s > 1

provides a stronger conditioning guidance, which improves the generation quality. 54 Experiments 

In this section, we conduct experiments to evaluate DiGFM, demonstrating that it outperforms or matches existing models on generating graphs with the target structural statistics while operating sig-nificantly faster. We first assess generation performance across general graph benchmarks, molecule datasets, and conditional molecule generation (Section 4.2). Next, we analyze DiGFM’s sampling efficiency (Section 4.3). Finally, Section 4.4 presents an ablation study of core components of DiGFM, highlighting the effectiveness of the proposed clean graph prediction approach. 

4.1 Experimental Setup Datasets. We evaluate DiGFM on diverse benchmark datasets for both general graph and molecular generation. For general graph generation, where the focus is on evaluating topology without node or edge attributes, we use four datasets: Community [13], Ego [14, 27], SBM [15], and Enzymes [16]. These datasets contain two-community graphs, ego networks from Citeseer, stochastic block model graphs, and protein tertiary structure graphs, respectively. For molecular graph generation, which requires modeling both structure and node/edge attributes, we evaluate on the QM9 [17] and ZINC250k [18] datasets. More details about the datasets are in Appendix B. 

Evaluation Metrics. For general graphs, we evaluate structural fidelity by measuring the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) of four graph statistics between generated graphs and ground-truth graphs in the test set: degree (Deg.), clustering coefficient (Clus.), number of orbits with 4 nodes (Orbit), and the eigenvalues of the graph Laplacian (Spec.) [10]. For the SBM dataset, which is the only one with a standard definition of validity, we also report the percentage of valid, unique, and novel graphs (V.U.N.). For the other datasets, we compute the Fr´ echet Inception Distance (FID) and radial basis function MMD (RBF MMD) to measure the overall generation diversity and fidelity [36]. For molecular graphs, we use four common metrics: the percentage of generated molecules that are valid without post hoc correction (Valid %), Fr´ echet ChemNet Distance (FCD) [37], neighborhood subgraph pairwise distance kernel (NSPDK) [38], and scaffold similarity (Scaf.) [39]. In all tables, we include a “Training set” reference row. This records the distance between the training and test sets measured by each metric, so it serves as a baseline of the natural statistical distance between the training and test splits drawn from the same graph distribution. An optimal model is expected to achieve metrics close to this “Training set” baseline. 

DiGFM Setup. The hyperparameters of DiGFM for each dataset are given in Appendix A. 

4.2 Graph Generation Performance General Graph Generation. DiGFM outperforms baselines on the majority of metrics, across all four datasets (Tables 3-4). DiGFM’s low MMD metrics indicate that its generated graphs closely match the structural statistics of the ground-truth graph distribution. Further, the generated graphs are diverse (i.e., the model does not generate the same or similar graphs every time), as shown by the high V.U.N. and low FID and RBF MMD scores. For each metric, results are reported as the mean ±

standard deviation across five sampling runs. We visualize a single graph generated by DiGFM for each dataset in Figure 1, and additional generations are in Appendix F. Crucially, DiGFM achieves these state-of-the-art generation results using only a fraction of the sampling steps required by baselines. While the majority of baselines require 500 or 1000 steps, DiGFM generates using just 50 steps on the Community, Ego, and Enzymes datasets, and only 40 steps on the SBM dataset. We explore this acceleration further in Section 4.3, demonstrating that DiGFM maintains state-of-the-art performance on Community and Enzymes with as few as 15 steps.

4.3 Improved Sampling Efficiency 

We demonstrate that DiGFM is highly efficient at inference time: it outperforms previous models on both classical MMD and global graph metrics while using only 2.5% to 15.6% of their sampling steps. As Figure 2(a) shows, DiGFM rapidly converges to the ground-truth graph topology, requiring only 15 sampling steps to outperform baselines. ## 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we present Direct Graph Flow Matching (DiGFM), a novel generative model designed to overcome the limitations of standard noise-prediction methods in diffusion and flow models. By leveraging a continuous flow-matching paradigm integrated with direct graph prediction, DiGFM accurately learns the clean graph’s topological properties. A key aspect of our approach is the separate formulation of the model output space (clean graph) and the loss space (velocity), with a transformation between them. Extensive experiments demonstrate that DiGFM outperforms or matches state-of-the-art baselines across general graph benchmarks and molecular datasets, while achieving a 5.3 × to 257 × speedup in inference time. 9References

We provide additional details about DiGFM and its training that do not appear in the main text. 

Stabilizing Training via EMA. To stabilize training, we employ an adaptive Exponential Moving Average (EMA) [31] on the model weights. Specifically, at step s in the training loop, the EMA decay rate is β = 0 .5bsz /halflife , where bsz is the batch size. Here, halflife = min( tgt halflife , 0.05 ·n graphs ),where tgt halflife is the specified target halflife and n graphs = bsz · s is the number of graphs that the model has processed by step s in the training loop. With this approach, the EMA model weights update rapidly early in training and change more slowly later in training. 

Output Bounding. Since DiGFM directly predicts the clean graph rather than unbounded, off-manifold Gaussian noise, we can explicitly constrain the model’s output to match the valid domain of the target data. We apply these bounding operations after the final transformer block: • Edge Attributes ( E): We apply a sigmoid activation, Eout = sigmoid (Epred ), to bound predicted edge values to the interval [0 , 1] .• Node Attributes ( X): For categorical node attributes, we apply a node-wise softmax activation, 

Xout = softmax (Xpred ), so that the output is a valid probability distribution. For continuous attributes where the ground-truth node attributes come from Laplacian eigenvectors, we nor-malize each predicted node attribute vector to an L2 norm of 1, which ensures that it matches the norm of the ground-truth eigenvectors. 

Laplacian Spectral Node Features. For graphs without given node attributes, we set the target node attribute matrix X to consist of the eigenvectors of the combinatorial Laplacian L = D − A, where A

is the adjacency matrix and D is the diagonal degree matrix. Specifically, we extract and L2-normalize the k eigenvectors corresponding to the smallest non-zero eigenvalues. Additionally, to account for the sign ambiguity in standard eigensolvers, we apply a random sign-flipping augmentation 13 during training. At each training step and for each graph, we independently sample a sign vector 

σ ∈ {− 1, 1}k and multiply it with the precomputed eigenvectors to yield the input X. This ensures the model learns representations that are invariant to arbitrary eigenvector signs. 

Additional Discussion on x-prediction outperforming v-prediction. Although v and x are mathematically related by the simple linear transformation v(zt, t ) = 11−t (x(zt, t ) − zt) in equation (2), zt in this equation is a high-dimensional noisy quantity, which is difficult for the model to represent internally. Predicting v is significantly harder than predicting x because it requires the model to internally process zt:• With x-prediction, the high-dimensional quantity zt in the transformation equation is supplied 

outside the network. The network only needs to learn to represent the low-dimensional clean graph x.• With v-prediction, the network must learn the dependence on the high-dimensional, noisy quantity zt internally .This difference becomes important when the dimension of the noisy graph zt approaches or exceeds the model’s representational capacity. If the network contains any structural bottlenecks (such as narrower linear layers), some of the high-dimensional noise information required for zt would be discarded, which degrades the performance of v-prediction. In contrast, with x-prediction, the target output is a lower-dimensional clean graph, so a finite-capacity network can learn it more easily.

Dataset Details. We provide more details about the six datasets used. The Community dataset consists of 500 two-community graphs [13]. The Ego dataset consists of 757 3-hop ego networks extracted from the Citeseer network [14, 27]. The SBM dataset consists of 200 graphs from 14 the stochastic block model [15]. In both QM9 and ZINC250k, there are three edge types: single, double, or triple bonds. 

Evaluation Details for General Graph Datasets. To evaluate the MMD, FID, and RBF MMD metrics for Community and Ego datasets, we follow the protocol from [43, 36]. We generate 128 graphs from the model and compare against the test set, which contains 102 graphs for Community and 152 graphs for Ego. For FID and RBF MMD, the feature extractor used is a random Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN) with 3 rounds of graph propagation and a node embedding size of 35 recommended by [36], and we average the metrics over 5 random seeds. To evaluate the metrics for the Enzymes and SBM datasets, we follow [27] and [15], respectively. We generate 117 and 40 graphs, respectively, from the model and compare against the graphs in the test set. The number of generated graphs equals the number of graphs in the test set. Our evaluation code for MMD and V.U.N. follows [10], and our evaluation code for FID and RBF MMD follows [36]. 

Evaluation Details for Molecular Datasets. We evaluate model performance using the four standard metrics used in [10]: Valid % is the percentage of generated molecules that are valid without post hoc correction such as valency correction or edge resampling. Fr´ echet ChemNet Distance (FCD) [37] is the Fr´ echet distance between the feature vectors of generated molecules and the test set. Neighborhood subgraph pairwise distance kernel (NSPDK) [38] measures the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) between the graph distributions of the generated and test sets by comparing their local neighborhood subgraphs. Scaffold similarity (Scaf.) [39] is the cosine similarity of the frequencies of the Bemis-Murcko scaffolds, which evaluates the model’s ability to generate similar substructures as those in the test set. To evaluate these metrics, we generate 10,000 molecules and compare them against the test set. We use the preprocessed dataset and train-test split from [27, 10]: specifically, we kekulized the molecules using the RDKit library [48] and removed hydrogen atoms following the standard procedure.

Details about Baselines in Table 8. The noise-prediction baseline in Table 8 outputs the predicted noise ϵθ (zt, t ), which is converted to velocity via vθ (zt, t ) = ( zt − ϵθ (zt, t )) /t prior to computing the flow matching loss. To prevent division by zero, we clip the denominator t to a minimum value of 0.01. This is similar to the numerical stabilization used in our DiGFM model, which computes velocity via vθ (zt, t ) = ( xθ (zt, t ) − zt)/(1 − t). In this case, we bound the denominator 1 − t from below at 0.01 (equivalent to clipping t to a maximum of 0.99).

Scalability Comparison. Like other representative graph diffusion and flow models (e.g., the discrete models such as DiGress [25], DisCo [33], Cometh [51], and DeFoG [34], as well as the continuous model GruM [10]), DiGFM has an O(N 2) complexity, where N is the number of nodes in the generated graph. The reason is that these models all rely on dense adjacency matrices to represent all N (N − 1) node pairs, and they use a graph transformer backbone [25, 35] with O(N 2) time and memory complexity per layer. In this paper, our focus is on improving sampling efficiency within this standard regime, and extending DiGFM to achieve subquadratic complexity is an orthogonal direction for future work. Figure 5 demonstrates that DiGFM maintains high efficiency and continues to have a significant inference speed advantage over baselines as graph sizes scale, despite the quadratic complexity. To evaluate scalability up to 1000 nodes, we compared DiGFM against DeFoG, DiGress, and GruM (using SBM hyperparameters) across sizes N ∈ { 50 , 100 , 200 , 500 , 1000 }. Notably, at 1000 nodes, DiGFM requires just 3.13 seconds per graph, which is 2.7 ×, 56 ×, and 38 × faster than DeFoG, DiGress, and GruM, respectively. While the generated 1000-node graphs are not meaningful in this setup (as the SBM dataset caps at 187 nodes), the timing comparison is valid for comparing scalability. 17 E Additional Related Work 

Comparison with Few-step Graph Diffusion/Flow Models. Standard graph diffusion and flow matching models often need 500 to 1000 sampling steps to generate a clean graph [25, 27], making inference slow. Recent work has focused on reducing this step count to speed up generation [33, 51, 34]. For example, DeFoG [34] is a state-of-the-art discrete flow matching model that generates high-quality graphs in just 50 steps. Our proposed DiGFM has several main differences from DeFoG. First, DiGFM is the first continuous flow matching model that is able to generate high-quality graphs in the few-step regime (15–50 sampling steps). Our approach of using direct graph prediction to make few-step generation possible is completely different from DeFoG’s discrete designs, such as nonlinear sample time distortion and changes to the transition rate matrix. Second, even though both models use a similar number of steps, DiGFM is 5.3 × to 12 × faster in wall-clock inference time (Table 7). This is because DiGFM’s continuous formulation only requires a fast Euler solver update, while DeFoG has to build a discrete transition process and sample categorical states at every step.
