## Abstract 

Motion capture data is rarely directly usable, as they typically exhibit missing seg-ments, jitter, drift and contact artifacts. Traditionally, corrupted motions are cleaned by animators through the manual identification of keyframes from noisy motion, subsequent keyframe correction, and interpolation between corrected keyframes to reconstruct coherent motion. While the rise of generative motion models has made automatic cleanup feasible, most approaches operate as black box denoisers with limited controllability, making it difficult to preserve reliable segments or enforce specific user intents. Inspired by animation workflows, we present CleanMDM , a unified multimodal motion cleanup framework that formulates cleanup as masked conditional generation with plug-and-play conditions. This single model sup-ports arbitrary combinations of noisy 3D motion, sparse 2D keyframes, sparse 3D keyframes, and text. This design enables both automatic cleanup without additional

user annotation and controllable cleanup under multimodal guidance. To further improve motion realism, we incorporate the Latent Motion Quality Discriminator (LMQD ) to better match kinematic distributions and reduce skating, jitter, and interpenetration artifacts, and we apply Mesh-Aware Contact Projection as a test-time optimization step to enhance contact and physical consistency. Experiments across multiple datasets demonstrate that CleanMDM consistently outperforms prior cleanup and generation baselines, and that low cost conditions (text and 2D keyframes) provide reliable controllability gains in multimodal cleanup scenarios. 

## 1 Introduction 

Motion data in modern animation pipelines is increasingly acquired from video-based motion capture Shen et al. [2024], Goel et al. [2023] and generative motion models Tevet et al. [2023], Chen et al. [2023], which are more accessible and scalable than traditional studio capture but frequently introduce missing observations, temporal jitter, drift, and contact artifacts such as foot sliding, floating, and ground penetration. Because these artifacts are visually amplified after skinning and rendering, animators still spend substantial time on manual cleanup and inbetweening, making motion repair a persistent production bottleneck Williams [2012], Holden [2018], Kovar et al. [2002]. A key characteristic of real-world motion corruption, largely overlooked by existing methods, is that degradation is almost always localized: only certain temporal intervals or body parts are affected, while the remaining motion stays reliable. For example, video-based estimates may exhibit lower-body drift while the upper body is essentially correct, or jitter may appear in only a handful of joints over a short window. This partial reliability has a direct consequence for method design: an ideal cleanup system should identify and preserve the trustworthy portions of a motion while selectively regenerating only the corrupted regions, and it must accept diverse forms of guidance depending on what information is available for a given shot. Existing approaches largely fall into two categories, neither of which is specifically designed to handle partial reliability. Restoration-style denoisers Mu et al. [2025], Zhang et al. [2024b] map an entire noisy sequence to a clean motion in a black-box fashion. They improve average reconstruction error but treat every frame uniformly, providing limited controllability when animators need to lock key poses, preserve trustworthy segments, or repair only a specified interval. Inbetweening and conditional generation Cohan et al. [2024], Zhang et al. [2025a], Meng et al. [2025] take the opposite stance: they regard the corrupted region as entirely unobserved and reconstruct it from sparse constraints (keyframes, text). When applied to cleanup, this discards the partially reliable observations inside the corrupted interval, leading to over-reconstruction, style drift, and unstable contact behavior. Moreover, these two families of methods are typically developed and deployed as separate, modality-specific models, which fragments supervision, limits cross-modal knowledge transfer, and requires maintaining multiple pipelines. It is worth noting that motion cleanup is fundamentally different from motion generation: generation creates motion from scratch given sparse or abstract signals, whereas cleanup starts from an existing clip that is partially reliable and must preserve its trustworthy content. Recent multimodal generation models such as Li et al. [2025] accept heterogeneous inputs (2D/3D keyframes, text) but are designed for the generation task. They do not model per-frame reliability or enforce non-destructive preservation of the input draft, which limits their applicability to production cleanup scenarios requiring fidelity to the original performance. We present CleanMDM , a unified multimodal motion cleanup framework that directly addresses the partial-reliability challenge. Our key insight is to formulate cleanup as masked conditional generation in a shared latent space. The central mechanism is confidence-aligned multimodal fusion .Heterogeneous conditions, including noisy 3D motion drafts with per-frame confidence masks, sparse 2D keyframes, sparse 3D keyframes, and text prompts, are encoded into a common latent representation and fused via frame-wise confidence weighting (Eq. 2). Unlike raw-space additive fusion used in generation models Li et al. [2025], our confidence-aligned design explicitly encodes the reliability of each frame: high-confidence frames are preserved, low-confidence frames are regenerated, and the boundary between the two is determined by the per-frame weights rather than a hard binary mask. This enables a smooth continuum from fully missing intervals (inbetweening) to partially degraded segments (denoising-style cleanup), all within a single model. Text and sparse 2D keyframes serve as low-cost, plug-and-play conditions that provide semantic and geometric guidance without requiring expensive 3D annotation. By jointly training on all modalities, CleanMDM 2encourages cross-modal transfer: the richer supervision yields a stronger motion prior that benefits even the simplest auto-cleanup setting where no additional guidance is provided. Two additional technical contributions improve motion quality beyond standard regression objectives. First, we introduce the Latent Motion Quality Discriminator (LMQD) , a lightweight adversarial regularizer that operates on entire sequences in the motion autoencoder’s latent space. Unlike prior kinematic-feature discriminators used in physics-based control Peng et al. [2021], Luo et al. [2023], LMQD back-propagates gradients directly through the latent representation, better capturing distributional characteristics of natural motion and more effectively penalizing perceptually salient artifacts (skating, jitter, penetration) that have small ℓ2 magnitude but high visual salience. Second, we propose Mesh-Aware Contact Projection , a test-time optimization step that refines the full sequence with explicit constraints on SMPL mesh foot-sole vertices. Unlike conventional joint-level inverse kinematics Holden [2018], Yao et al. [2024], our formulation reasons about body-surface geometry relative to the ground plane, enforcing hard contact consistency that joint-only approaches structurally cannot achieve. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • Confidence-aligned multimodal cleanup framework. We formulate motion cleanup as masked conditional generation in a shared latent space with per-frame confidence weighting, enabling non-destructive repair that preserves reliable motion while regenerating corrupted regions — a capability that neither black-box denoisers nor generation-oriented models provide. A single CleanMDM model covers auto cleanup, inbetweening, and multimodal-guided repair. • Latent adversarial regularization. We introduce LMQD, a latent-space sequence discrim-inator that provides distributional supervision beyond regression losses, yielding measurable improvements on contact and temporal-quality metrics. • Mesh-aware contact projection. We propose a test-time optimization that constrains SMPL mesh vertices to enforce surface-level ground contact (foot sliding, penetration, and floating). 

## 2 Related Works 

2.1 Human Motion Generation 

Recent progress in human motion synthesis is largely driven by generative models. Diffusion approaches (e.g. Tevet et al. [2023], Chen et al. [2023], Zhang et al. [2024a], Zhao et al. [2024], Zhang et al. [2024c, 2023b]) show their ability to model long-range spatio-temporal motion relationships, while VQ/tokenized pipelines Zhang et al. [2023a], Guo et al. [2024], Li et al. [2024], Jiang et al. [2023], Pinyoanuntapong et al. [2024], Zhang et al. [2024d] introduce discrete motion token modeling. Representations evolve from kinematics-rich descriptors (e.g., HumanML3D Guo et al. [2022]) toward more concise forms, including redundancy reduced designs Meng et al. [2024] and absolute coordinate modeling Meng et al. [2025]. Control has expanded beyond text to geometric cues and interactive editing interfaces Wang et al. [2025a], Zhong et al. [2025], Karunratanakul et al. [2023], Xie et al. [2023], and more recently to multimodal guidance Li et al. [2025], Luo et al. [2024], Xu et al. [2025], Zhang et al. [2025b], Chen et al. [2025]; some works also pair generation with physics for executability Yuan et al. [2023], Tevet et al. [2024], Tessler et al. [2024], Fan et al. [2025], Wang et al. [2025b]. Despite these advances, semantic conditions often underconstrain fine geometry and contacts, while physics constraints can disrupt motion timing and cause local limb deformations. 

2.2 Motion Cleanup 

Repairing motion captured from videos or produced as drafts is critical for production. Existing work largely follows two directions. Cleanup-style methods map corrupted sequences to clean motion via end-to-end denoising Mu et al. [2025], Zhang et al. [2024b], reducing reconstruction error but offering limited fine-grained control (e.g., preserving reliable segments or enforcing locked poses). Some approaches detect corrupted segments within a motion sequence and subsequently regenerate them. However, these approaches treat missing regions as uninformative and do not explicitly combine partially reliable observations with heterogeneous constraints. In this sense, inbetweening can be viewed as a repair mechanism, as inbetweening and conditional completion synthesize motion from sparse constraints such as keyframes or text Cohan et al. [2024], Zhang et al. [2025a]. 2.3 Motion Quality and Physical Plausibility 

To mitigate motion artifacts such as sliding, floating, and penetration, prior work incorporates contact losses Tevet et al. [2023, 2024], physics-driven refinement Luo et al. [2023], Yao et al. ## 3 Data Representation 

We represent a motion clip as absolute joint positions X1: T ∈ RT ×J×3 with J=22 . Following ACMDM Meng et al. [2025], we adopt absolute coordinates as the interface for cleanup because they align naturally with the spatial signals used in our task. Sparse 3D keyframes can be inserted in the same coordinate system, 2D keypoints are obtained by direct camera projection, and contact reasoning or inference-time IK can be formulated directly in metric space. Compared with relative or velocity-based representations, absolute coordinates also avoid reconstructing global trajectories by accumulating local offsets, where small frame-wise errors can propagate into long-horizon drift. This makes the representation especially suitable for non-destructive cleanup: reliable observations, sparse controls, and regenerated regions all remain comparable in a single world-space coordinate system. ## 4 Method 

CleanMDM formulates motion cleanup as masked conditional generation in a shared latent space. The central design principle is confidence-aligned multimodal fusion: heterogeneous conditions are encoded into a common latent representation and fused via per-frame confidence weighting, so that trustworthy frames are preserved while corrupted regions are selectively regenerated. This fundamentally distinguishes CleanMDM from generation models (e.g., GenMo Li et al. [2025]) that synthesize motion from scratch without modeling input reliability. 

4.1 Latent Motion Modeling Backbone 

CleanMDM performs motion cleanup in a learned latent space. Given an absolute joint trajectory 

X1: T ∈ RT ×J×3, we encode it with a motion autoencoder E and decode with D. The encoder downsamples only in time by a factor s=4 , producing a latent sequence of length T ′ = ⌈T /s ⌉ while keeping the joint dimension J unchanged. We train a conditional flow matching Transformer Lipman et al. [2022], Peebles and Xie [2023], Ho et al. [2020] to predict a velocity field in latent space, and we sample with an ODE solver Chen et al. [2018]. Working in latent space reduces computation, improves training stability, and allows us to integrate heterogeneous conditions at a common temporal resolution. Our backbone is a conditional Transformer over the noised latent motion tokens. We use RoPE positional encoding Su et al. [2024] and QK Normalization Henry et al. [2020] in self attention, and inject conditions by AdaLN modulation with residual gating, which allows the model to smoothly activate or deactivate guidance under missing or partial conditions. Text features from a frozen CLIP encoder are provided as context to a gated cross attention layer. 

4.2 Multimodal Conditions and Fusion 

CleanMDM treats motion cleanup as masked conditional generation with plug-and-play conditions. The core mechanism is confidence-aligned fusion in latent space : instead of directly concatenating raw inputs or using additive fusion in joint space, we encode all geometric conditions through the shared autoencoder and fuse them via per-frame confidence weighting. This design is motivated by two reasons. First, the autoencoder provides a shared coordinate system and scale for 3D motion signals, which makes fusion well-posed and improves robustness to partial corruptions. Second, latent fusion follows the successful recipe of latent diffusion in vision, where rich conditions are projected to the model width and injected into a generative backbone without forcing all modalities into a brittle raw-space representation. Unlike raw-space additive fusion used in generation models Li et al. [2025], our confidence-aligned design explicitly encodes the reliability of each frame, enabling non-destructive cleanup. 

Condition construction For 3D conditions, we mask in input space first , then encode with the same E: (i) noisy 3D observations use a frame-wise confidence derived from an interval annotation or automatic corruption indicator so unreliable intervals contribute less; (ii) sparse clean 3D keyframes use a binary mask so only selected frames are kept. The resulting latents are flattened into per-time tokens, and their masks are downsampled to the latent rate to obtain frame weights. For 2D keyframes, we mask in 2D space, downsample to T ′, flatten each frame, and map it with a small MLP, without explicit 2D-to-3D lifting. Since the backbone learns a 3D motion latent from paired projected 2D cues, the MLP only needs to preserve image-plane geometry while the motion prior infers compatible 3D motion. This avoids the feature-space mismatch introduced by heavier pretrained 2D representations; Appendix A.7 (Table 9) confirms that it outperforms MotionBERT features.

4.3 Latent Motion Quality Discriminator (LMQD) 

Regression losses in flow matching do not fully capture perceptual motion quality. We draw inspiration from PHC Luo et al. [2023] in using adversarial supervision to improve motion realism, but instead apply the discriminator in the autoencoder latent space rather than on handcrafted kinematic features (joint positions, accelerations, power). Operating in latent space offers two advantages: (i) gradients flow directly to the generator without passing through the decoder, and (ii) the learned latent captures more intrinsic motion structure than kinematic descriptors. 4.4 Auto Cleanup and Inbetweening in One Model 

CleanMDM unifies mask-guided inbetweening and blind auto cleanup because animation repair often alternates between the two: an animator may correct corrupted poses, lock keyframes, and then inbetween the damaged span. Using separate cleanup and inbetweening models would form a cascade in which the first stage can pass drift or over-smoothed poses to the second stage, while reliable regions may be rewritten twice. We instead train one model with a shared condition interface. For half of each training batch, we remove explicit interval supervision by marking all frames as clean in the corruption indicator, forcing the model to perform blind auto cleanup from the noisy draft. For the other half, we keep the interval annotation and train mask-guided cleanup. At inference, users may provide no interval annotation for automatic cleanup, provide corrupted intervals for inbetween-style repair, or additionally supply sparse 2D/3D keyframes and text prompts. All cases use the same condition dictionary, and each modality is enabled or disabled by its confidence weight.

## 5 Experiment 

5.1 Experimental Setup 

We build training clips from AMASS Mahmood et al. [2019] and MotionLLaMA Ling et al. [2024], converting each sequence to a unified SMPL Loper et al. [2023] 22-joint skeleton and absolute joint-position stream. Each clip is normalized by centering the first-frame root on the horizontal plane and standardizing joint coordinates. To emulate video-mocap degradations, we inject localized artifacts into random temporal intervals of clean clips, following the protocol in StableMotion Mu et al. [2025] for part of the noise design. The artifact families include joint jittering, foot sliding, horizontal drifting, and vertical floating. We record a per-frame defect label ℓ ∈ NT , where ℓ[t] = 0 

denotes a clean frame and non-zero values indicate corrupted frames. Full data filtering, corruption construction, and localization details are provided in Appendix A.2. We use flow matching Lipman et al. [2022] with linear transport paths and velocity prediction. Sampling uses an Euler ODE solver with 50 steps. The generator learning rate is 4 × 10 −4 and the discriminator learning rate is 5 × 10 −5, both with AdamW Loshchilov et al. [2017]. We set λadv = 0 .01 , condition dropout probability 0.1, and EMA decay 0.9999 . The generator and discriminator are trained with alternating updates. Our model is trained on 2 H20 GPUs with a batch size of 128 for 600 epochs ( ∼16 hours) and tested on 1 NVIDIA V100 GPU. Metric definitions are provided in Appendix A.5.

Main results. Across all three settings, CleanMDM achieves the strongest overall motion quality. In auto cleanup , CleanMDM substantially improves reconstruction fidelity and distributional realism over StableMotion, while simultaneously reducing contact-related artifacts (skating and penetra-tion) and temporal noise (acceleration error and jitter). In inbetween , CleanMDM outperforms ACMDM and CondMDI on both geometric accuracy (MPJPE and keyframe error) and kinematic quality, indicating that the same backbone can handle interval completion and denoising within a unified formulation. In the most challenging multimodal cleanup setting, CleanMDM substantially outperforms GenMo (21.95 vs. 646.4 MPJPE), consistent with our expectation that a model designed for generation rather than cleanup does not explicitly preserve the input draft, resulting in lower reconstruction fidelity. Fig. 3 ( A.1) visualizes representative examples under the same three settings, highlighting improved contact quality (e.g., reduced foot sliding) and stronger keyframe adherence compared with task matched baselines.

Beyond synthetic corruption, we evaluate CleanMDM on real video-mocap outputs from animation, monocular video (HAA500, IDEA400, Kungfu) and multi-view video (HuMMan) sources, which contain artifacts beyond our training corruption types. On KungFu, CleanMDM cuts MPJPE roughly in half compared with StableMotion (69.4 vs. 127.8), while StableMotion attains a marginally lower jitter (9.2 vs. 12.0) by over-smoothing the motion in a way that alters its action identity; Appendix Fig. 5 visualizes this trade-off on a Tornado Kick 

clip, where StableMotion exhibits visible foot floating and a weakened rotational signature relative to the real-world input. Full public-dataset evaluations and real-world visualizations are provided in Appendix A.10 (Table 12, Figs. 5–6). These results confirm that CleanMDM’s motion prior generalizes to real-world degradations, not just the synthetic artifacts used during training. 

Effect of Mesh-Aware Contact Projection. C6 applies our Mesh-Aware Contact Projection as a post-process. While global MPJPE can increase (due to sequence-level adjustments of root/ground alignment), C6 consistently reduces artifacts, including skating, penetration frequency/distance, and jitter, approaching the statistics of the ground truth. This behavior matches the role of Mesh-Aware Contact Projection as a projection step: it enforces hard contact consistency and temporal coherence, which are only weakly captured by regression-style training objectives. Therefore, CleanMDM provides a strong generative cleanup prior, while C6 further improves physical plausibility when strict contact quality is required. 5.4 Ablation Studies Adding modalities improves controllable cleanup. Table 2 shows that motion cleanup benefits from progressively adding complementary modalities. Starting from C0 (noisy 3D draft only), adding text (C1) provides coarse semantic and style guidance but yields limited improvements on geometric metrics. Adding sparse 2D keyframes (C2) consistently improves accuracy and keyframe adherence, suggesting that 2D cues act as an effective geometric scaffold even without explicit 2D-to-3D lifting. Combining 2D and text (C3) further improves the overall balance between fidelity and controllability. Notably, the strongest gains appear when CleanMDM additionally leverages sparse 3D keyframes, which anchor the reconstruction inside corrupted intervals and reduce ambiguity. This trend supports the central motivation of multimodal cleanup: different cues compensate for each other under partial corruption, and a single model can exploit whichever signals are available at test time. 

LMQD improves distribution-level motion quality. Comparing C3 and C4 isolates the effect of LMQD. Although MPJPE may not monotonically decrease, LMQD improves contact and temporal quality metrics (e.g., reduced penetration distance and jitter) and increases diversity relative to ground truth motion, indicating a better match to the motion manifold beyond point-wise regression. This supports our design choice of imposing a sequence-level adversarial regularizer in latent space: it is lightweight, compatible with flow matching, and directly targets artifacts that are perceptually salient yet poorly captured by deterministic reconstruction losses. 

Why latent fusion matters. Replacing autoencoder-based 3D keyframe encoding with direct MLP projection in joint space (C5 (MLP), Appendix A.8 Table 10) degrades temporal quality dramatically (Jitter: 71 .02 vs. 5.54 ; FID: 2.99 vs. 0.11 ). We attribute the gap to the autoencoder’s temporal downsampling bottleneck, which provides a normalized coordinate system and temporal-coherence prior that makes weighted fusion well-posed across heterogeneous signals; raw-space MLP encoding lacks this structure and creates a modality gap with the AE-encoded noisy draft. Moreover, multimodal training itself improves the motion prior: C5 outperforms C0 even when no extra conditions are given at inference (Appendix A.6, Table 8). 

## 6 Conclusions, Limitations and Future Work 

We presented CleanMDM, a unified framework for motion cleanup that formulates repair and inbetweening as masked conditional generation with plug-and-play multimodal conditions, including noisy 3D drafts, sparse 2D/3D keyframes, and text prompts. CleanMDM fuses heterogeneous geometric cues in a shared latent representation with frame-wise weighting, enabling the model to preserve reliable observations while regenerating corrupted regions under incomplete or mixed constraints. To better address perceptual artifacts that are weakly captured by regression-style objectives, we introduced LMQD, and further applied Mesh-Aware Contact Projection to enforce contact consistency and reduce skating and penetration. Experiments across auto repair, inbetweening, and multimodal cleanup demonstrate consistent improvements over prior baselines, with particularly strong gains on contact and temporal quality metrics. A current limitation is that our evaluation relies on controlled corruption and a simplified ground model during Mesh-Aware Contact Projection. In future work, we plan to incorporate more realistic video mocap degradations, stronger learned contact priors, and additional control modalities and interactive authoring workflows for animation pipelines. 9References

A.2 Data 

To emulate video mocap degradations, we synthesize corrupted motions by perturbing SMPL Loper et al. [2023] joint rotations and root translations, following the artifact injection protocol in Stable-Motion Mu et al. [2025] for part of the noise design. We apply a random subset of artifact types to a random temporal interval [s, e ), including joint jittering, foot sliding, horizontal drifting, and vertical floating. To ensure that corruptions remain localized and do not alter the clean segment outside the interval, we inject translation based artifacts in the velocity domain and integrate them only within 

[s, e ), while enforcing zero net displacement over the interval (i.e., the integrated root translation offset satisfies ∆te − ∆ts = 0), so that frames after e keep the original root trajectory unchanged. We record a per-frame defect label det _mask ∈ NT , where det _mask[ t] = 0 denotes a clean frame and non-zero values indicate corrupted frames. 

Data Sources We build our training clips from AMASS Mahmood et al. [2019] and MotionL-LaMA Ling et al. [2024]. MotionLLaMA originally contains approximately 48K motion-text pairs; however, part of this collection is noisy, lacks reliable text annotations, or comes from auxiliary sources that are less suitable for our setting. We therefore discard all MotionGV Fan et al. [2025] pseudo-labeled data, samples without text annotations, and most low-quality subsets from Motion Union, and retain only the subsets marked in Table 5. After filtering, about 28K MotionLLaMA clips remain for training. The final dataset is split into training, validation, and test sets with a ratio of 80:15:5. 

Absolute Joint Positions We represent motion in absolute joint positions X1: T ∈ RT ×J×3,following ACMDM Meng et al. [2025]. This choice is motivated by the cleanup interface. First, absolute positions respond directly to spatial control signals: sparse 3D keyframes are already in the same coordinate system, and 2D keypoints are obtained through camera projection. Second, absolute coordinates avoid reconstructing global motion by accumulating local offsets, which can propagate small per-frame errors into long-horizon drift. Third, recent transformer and diffusion backbones Peebles and Xie [2023] can internalize many kinematic regularities from data, so a concise representation reduces modeling complexity without sacrificing fidelity. Finally, contact detection and inference-time IK operate naturally in the absolute domain, which facilitates stable enforcement of foot contact and ground constraints. 

Artifact types. We simulate four common families: (i) jittering (high-frequency rotational noise on a subset of joints), (ii) floating (vertical root drift), (iii) foot sliding (spurious horizontal root motion causing skating), and (iv) drifting (slow horizontal root drift). The corrupted interval length is sampled to be localized (longer during training, shorter at test time).

A.6 Does Multimodal Training Help Even for Noisy-only Denoising? 

We further test whether multimodal training improves motion denoising even when no additional modalities are provided at inference . Specifically, we compare C0 (noisy 3D only) and C5 (our full multimodal model) under the same auto repair setting: both models take the same noisy motion as input, and we do not provide interval masks or any extra conditions at test time. As shown in Table 8, C5 consistently outperforms C0 on reconstruction fidelity and motion quality metrics (e.g., skating, penetration, temporal errors), suggesting that multimodal supervision improves the learned motion prior beyond single-modality training. We attribute the gains to (i) richer effective training pairings that reduce overfitting to a single modality under limited motion data, (ii) complementary supervisory signals that encourage more accurate conditional modeling during training, and (iii) a more general representation of motion dynamics that transfers back to denoising even when extra cues are absent at inference. 

A.7 Additional Ablations on Condition Encoders 

We provide additional ablations on how textual and 2D conditions are encoded and injected. All metrics follow the main paper, and all settings are identical to the corresponding baseline variant unless otherwise specified. 

2D condition encoder: lightweight MLP vs. MotionBERT features. To verify that our model can effectively utilize 2D cues, we ablate the design of the 2D condition encoder while keeping all other components, training settings, and data identical. Specifically, we compare (i) a lightweight 2D encoder that directly flattens the downsampled 2D keypoints (22 ×2 per frame) and maps them to the model latent space with a small MLP, versus (ii) a MotionBERT-based 2D feature extractor whose outputs are used as the 2D condition tokens. As shown in Table 9, directly encoding 2D keypoints with a simple MLP consistently outperforms the MotionBERT-based alternative across our evaluation suite. This indicates that, under our training setup, the model can learn useful 2D-to-motion correspondence from raw keypoints and does not require an explicit 2D-to-3D lifting module before conditioning. Since the generative backbone already operates in a learned 3D motion latent space, the 2D branch mainly needs to preserve observed image-plane geometry and project it to the model width. MotionBERT features are stronger for generic 3D pose representation learning, but in our cleanup setting they may introduce a pretrained representation bias and a feature-space mismatch with the autoencoder latents used by the noisy 3D draft and clean 3D keyframes. The lightweight MLP therefore gives a better trade-off: it preserves direct geometric constraints while letting the cleanup prior infer the compatible 3D motion.

(iii) Distributional quality requires representation-level alignment. The 27 × FID degradation indicates that raw-space encoding does not merely reduce point-wise accuracy — it fundamentally disrupts the generated motion distribution. C5 (MLP) produces motions that deviate sharply from natural motion statistics, even though its skating ratio is nominally lower (0.10 vs. 0.18), suggesting that the low skating comes at the cost of unnatural, jerky motion rather than genuine physical plausibility.

A.11 User Study Protocol. We conduct a perceptual study to evaluate whether CleanMDM improves motion quality in ways that are salient to animators. We consider three practical tasks: auto cleanup , inbetween ,and multimodal cleanup . For each task, we randomly sample 10 clips from the test set and compare CleanMDM against a task-matched baseline (StableMotion for auto cleanup, CondMDI for inbetween, and GenMo for multimodal cleanup). For each clip, we present a paired comparison (CleanMDM vs. baseline) with randomized left/right order and anonymized method names. 

Questions. To reduce rater fatigue while capturing production-relevant qualities, raters score each method on three criteria: Artifacts , Naturalness , and Faithfulness . Artifacts summarize contact and temporal issues (e.g., foot skating, ground penetration, and jitter). Naturalness evaluates whether the motion looks physically and stylistically plausible. Faithfulness measures consistency with the reference (ground truth for our benchmark; or the provided constraints for multimodal cleanup). All scores are given on a 0–5 scale, where higher is better.
