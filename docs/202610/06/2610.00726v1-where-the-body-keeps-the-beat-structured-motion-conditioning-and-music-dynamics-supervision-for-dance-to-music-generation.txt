ABSTRACT 

Dance-to-music (D2M) generation aims to synthesize musically plausible sound-tracks whose temporal structure aligns with a given dance performance. Represen-tative D2M methods do not explicitly distinguish motion cues across body parts and frequency bands, while standard flow matching lacks a dedicated objective for supervising local music-latent dynamics. To address these issues, we propose 

Dyna2Music , a latent flow-matching framework that combines structured motion conditioning with explicit supervision of music-latent dynamics. An empirical anal-ysis on AIST++ quantifies how spatial partitioning and frequency separation affect raw music-to-kinematic beat alignment and motion-reference density, informing the conditioning design. Accordingly, Dyna2Music decomposes joint velocities into slow and fast components and hierarchically fuses the resulting part-wise motion energy with pretrained joint features to condition music generation. To complement this representation, we introduce latent dynamics consistency (LDC), an auxiliary objective that matches adjacent-frame change magnitudes between a single-step clean-latent estimate and the paired reference. LDC makes local music-latent variation an explicit training target without adding trainable parameters or inference computation. Dyna2Music supports variable-length music generation, and experiments on AIST++ and TikTok demonstrate improved rhythmic alignment and audio quality over representative D2M baselines. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

For dance videos shared on platforms such as TikTok and YouTube, the choice of background music shapes both the expressive quality of the performance and the viewing experience. This application motivates research into dance-to-music (D2M) generation (Gan et al., 2020; Di et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2022a;b; Yu et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Zhang & Hua, 2024; Liang et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2025). Unlike music generation for general video content, which addresses audiovisual compatibility through visual-semantic conditioning (Tian et al., 2025) and rhythmic controls derived from visual motion speed and saliency (Di et al., 2021), D2M places particular emphasis on synthesizing musically plausible soundtracks whose temporal structure aligns with a given dance performance. Beyond matching the overall style or tempo, the soundtrack should respond to changes in movement, including sustained body motion, brief limb articulations, and rhythmic accents. Addressing these requirements involves both representing dance dynamics and learning the temporal structure of the accompanying music. Existing diffusion-based D2M approaches typically condition music generation on visual features and pose-derived motion cues extracted from dance videos. Within this paradigm, LORIS (Yu et al., 2023) derives a single visual rhythm sequence from aggregated joint motion, while PN-Diffusion (Sun et al., 2025) uses a spatiotemporal graph convolutional network to encode skeletal sequences into a motion embedding. However, the resulting conditioning representations do not explicitly separate cues by body part or temporal frequency. Spatially , different body parts may provide complementary cues about musical beat timing. Aggregating motion across parts can obscure localized events, such as a brief wrist accent occurring alongside a larger torso movement, making these cues less distinguishable in the conditioning representation. We therefore investigate how part-specific slow and fast motion signals relate to musical rhythm, using the findings to inform the conditioning design. Beyond representing rhythmic cues in dance, D2M generation also depends on how temporal variation in the accompanying music is learned. For generators operating in audio latent space, the magnitudes of changes between adjacent latent frames characterize local temporal variation in the audio representation. The standard flow-matching objective (Lipman et al., 2022) regresses the generative velocity field without a dedicated term for comparing these dynamics between the predicted clean latent and the paired reference. We therefore augment velocity regression with an auxiliary objective that matches these change magnitudes at corresponding music-latent frames. This provides explicit supervision of local audio-latent dynamics, complementing part-specific slow and fast motion conditioning. To this end, we introduce Dyna2Music , a latent flow-matching framework for D2M generation that combines structured motion conditioning with explicit supervision of music-latent dynamics (Figure 2). To preserve motion cues across body parts and temporal scales, the Spatio-Spectral Motion Decomposition (SSMD) module separates joint velocities into low- and high-frequency components and computes their energy for each of five body parts. These energy signals are complemented by pretrained MotionBERT features (Zhu et al., 2023), pooled within each part to provide information about joint configurations and coordination that energy alone does not capture. The Hierarchical Slow-Fast Conditioning Encoder (HSF-Encoder) then integrates the energy signals and motion features through attention across body parts and adaptive gating, producing conditioning tokens that combine part-specific context with slow and fast motion dynamics. The resulting conditioning tokens guide a transformer adapted from DiffRhythm (Ning et al., 2025) through cross-attention to generate continuous music latents, which are decoded into music by a frozen waveform VAE. To complement structured motion conditioning, we introduce latent dynamics consistency (LDC) as an auxiliary objective for supervising music-latent dynamics. During training, we derive a single-step estimate of the clean music latent from the predicted velocity field. 2 RELATED WORK 

General Video-to-Music Generation. It targets background music generation for diverse content, including films, vlogs, and documentaries, emphasizing semantic compatibility, temporal alignment, and controllability. An early approach, CMT (Di et al., 2021), controls symbolic music generation by mapping visual timing, motion speed, and motion saliency to beats, note density, and note strength. For richer visual context, VidMuse (Tian et al., 2025) integrates local clip information and global video context through long-short-term modeling, while GVMGen (Zuo et al., 2025) selects visual features with learnable music queries and conditions generation through temporal cross-attention. For explicit control, MTCV2M (Wu et al., 2025) combines fine-grained feature selection, progressive temporal alignment, and dynamic fusion of time-varying beat, melody, intensity, and emotion conditions. Complementary approaches use pretrained multimodal and language models to link video understanding with music generation. M 2UGen (Liu et al., 2023) integrates modality-3Dyna2Music 

specific encoders with LLaMA 2 to condition MusicGen or AudioLDM 2 on inputs including videos. Similarly, SONIQUE (Zhang & Fuentes, 2025) converts video descriptions into musical tags for conditional diffusion, enabling soundtracking with unpaired video and music data. More broadly, AudioX (Tian et al., 2026) conditions a diffusion transformer on fused text, video, and audio representations, supporting video-to-music generation in a unified multimodal framework. These studies explore learned visual representations, explicit musical controls, and language interfaces for general video soundtracking. 

Autoregressive Dance-to-Music Generation. Autoregressive D2M methods sequentially predict symbolic events or discrete audio tokens conditioned on motion and preceding outputs. Early ap-proaches include Dance2Music (Aggarwal & Parikh, 2021), which generates notes online from pose and note histories, and RhythmicNet (Su et al., 2021), which converts motion-derived rhythm into percussion and melodic accompaniment. For multi-instrument generation, Dance2MIDI (Han et al., 2024) combines autoregressive drum generation with BERT-based track completion, while DanceComposer (Liang et al., 2024) progressively introduces rhythm and style constraints. Uni-MuMo (Yang et al., 2025) extends this paradigm to unified text, music, and motion generation through a shared music-motion codebook and mutually conditioned autoregressive streams, support-ing motion-to-music generation. Across representations, decoding retains sequential dependencies between output time steps. 

Non-autoregressive Dance-to-Music Generation. Non-autoregressive D2M methods use adversarial synthesis or iterative refinement without left-to-right token decoding. D2M-GAN (Zhu et al., 2022a) conditions vector-quantized adversarial generation on video and motion, whereas CDCD (Zhu et al., 2022b) introduces contrastive learning into discrete diffusion. In continuous latent space, Tan et al. (2023) incorporate motion and genre conditions, while Dance2Music-Diffusion (Zhang & Hua, 2024) uses a dedicated motion encoder. For richer conditioning, LORIS (Yu et al., 2023) integrates visual, rhythmic, and genre context for long-term synthesis, while PN-Diffusion (Sun et al., 2025) uses positive and negative rhythmic signals from forward and reversed dance sequences. To adapt pretrained diffusion models, encoder-based textual inversion (Li et al., 2024) maps dance rhythm and genre to text embeddings, whereas Kimura et al. (2026) combine beat-guided contrastive pretraining with a ControlNet adapter for a frozen AudioLDM backbone. Within this family, Dyna2Music combines part-specific slow-fast motion conditioning with explicit LDC supervision of local music-latent dynamics in a latent flow-matching framework. 

3 METHODOLOGY 

3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION 

Dyna2Music conditions music generation on the dancer’s motion, represented as a sequence of estimated 3D joint positions J spanning N video frames. The objective is to synthesize a musically plausible accompaniment whose temporal structure aligns with the observed dance dynamics. We formulate this task as conditional generation in music latent space. For each dance-music pair, a frozen pretrained waveform VAE (Ning et al., 2025) encodes the music waveform y as 

x1 = E(y) ∈ RT ×da , (1) where T denotes the number of music-latent frames and da is the latent dimensionality. We seek to learn the conditional distribution pθ (x1 | J). At inference, generated music latents are converted into waveforms by the frozen VAE decoder D. Joint features and temporal alignment. As shown in Figure 2, the HSF-Encoder maps S, F, and E to a conditioning sequence C ∈ RT ×d,where d = 256 . At each music-latent time step, the encoder retains distinct tokens for each part and band during context integration, uses energy-dependent gates to aggregate parts within each band, and then fuses the slow and fast representations.

4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 DATASETS 

We evaluate Dyna2Music on two complementary D2M benchmarks, AIST++ (Li et al., 2021; Tsuchida et al., 2019) and TikTok (Zhu et al., 2022a), covering dance performances captured in controlled studio environments and in-the-wild social-media videos, respectively. Together, they allow us to assess rhythmic alignment and audio quality across different recording conditions. (i) AIST++ .This dataset contains dance performances recorded in a controlled studio environment. Our split comprises 8,819 training and 180 test dance video-music pairs. (ii) TikTok . This dataset contains in-the-wild dance videos collected from social media, with diverse backgrounds and recording conditions. Our split comprises 391 training and 53 test dance video-music pairs. Evaluation Protocols. (a) Ob-jective Evaluation. We assess onset-level synchrony and au-dio quality on both datasets. Because LORIS’s one-second onset bins obscure fine tim-ing differences, we match on-set timestamps one-to-one within 

±50 ms. We report Beats Cov-erage Score (BCS), Beats Hit Score (BHS), and F1; defini-tions and across-clip variabil-ity statistics (CSD/HSD) are de-tailed in Appendix B.1. Audio quality is measured by Fréchet Audio Distance (FAD) (Kil-gour et al., 2018) using VG-Gish (Hershey et al., 2017) (FAD v ), PANNs (Kong et al., 2020) (FAD p), and CLAP (Elizalde et al., 2023) (FAD c) features, with lower values indicating closer agreement with reference music distributions. 

(b) Subjective Evaluation. An AIST++ user study assesses overall musical quality (OVL) and dance-music relevance (REL). The study protocol and results are provided in Appendix E. The supplementary material includes selected generation results from the AIST++ and TikTok test sets. 

Implementation Details. Dyna2Music operates on 64-channel music latents from a frozen waveform VAE with a temporal downsampling factor of 2048 and stereo output at 44 .1 kHz. The HSF-Encoder and flow-matching transformer are optimized jointly, while MotionBERT and the waveform VAE remain frozen. We use AdamW with a learning rate of 7.5 × 10 −5, linear warm-up followed by linear decay, FP16 mixed precision, and gradient clipping at a norm of 1.0. Training is configured for up to 100 epochs with random seed 42 , and conditioning dropout is set to 0.2. We set λLDC = 1 

on both datasets. At inference, we use exponential-moving-average weights and Euler integration over 32 uniformly spaced flow-time points. Architecture, sequence-length, and sampling details are provided in Appendix A. 5 CONCLUSION 

We presented Dyna2Music , a latent flow-matching framework for variable-length dance-to-music generation. It combines hierarchical fusion of part-specific slow-fast motion cues and pretrained joint features with latent dynamics consistency, which explicitly supervises local temporal variation in music latents. Experiments on AIST++ and TikTok show higher onset-level synchrony and lower FAD than the evaluated baselines, with consistent BAS advantages across motion-reference configurations. Future work will address finer motion-dependent phase control and long-range musical structure. 9Dyna2Music

B EVALUATION DETAILS 

B.1 ONSET -L EVEL SYNCHRONY 

This protocol specifies the onset-level metrics in Table 1 (Section 4.2). 

Relation to prior evaluation. LORIS (Yu et al., 2023) computes Beats Coverage Score (BCS) and Beats Hit Score (BHS) from one-second bins of detected onsets. Binning merges events and discards within-bin timing, potentially obscuring temporal mismatches. We retain the precision/recall interpretations but match continuous onset timestamps within ±50 ms. 

Detection and matching. Complete audio signals are resampled to 44 .1 kHz. Generated and reference music use identical onset detectors: a SuperFlux-style spectral-flux envelope (Böck & Widmer, 2013), median aggregation, and a 256 -sample hop ( 5.8 ms). Maximum-cardinality bipartite matching in mir_eval (Raffel et al., 2014) pairs onsets one-to-one within ±50 ms (Dixon, 2006). Scoring covers the full reference duration: excess generated events are discarded, and uncovered intervals in shorter outputs contain no generated events. 

Metrics and interpretation. For each clip, BCS and BHS equal the matched count divided by the generated and reference onset counts, respectively; F1 is their harmonic mean. Table 1 reports clip-averaged BCS, BHS, and F1, and the sample standard deviations of BCS and BHS (CSD and HSD). All five statistics are expressed as percentages. Because chance matches depend on onset density, these scores quantify event synchrony with a particular recording and alone do not establish dance-specific rhythmic alignment. The complementary beat-relative analysis in Section 4.5 uses the mismatched-track controls detailed below. B.2 BEAT -R ELATIVE ONSET STRUCTURE AND PERIODICITY

Table A1 shows that the full five-part, two-band model achieves the highest BCS, BHS, and F1 on both datasets. Merging the frequency bands at five-part resolution reduces F1 by 8.38 percentage points on AIST++ and 9.54 on TikTok. Retaining both bands but merging the five parts into upper/lower body reduces F1 by 12 .18 and 9.98 points, respectively. These results favor retaining both part and frequency distinctions in the reported models. However, band separation at two-part resolution does not improve F1, and finer partitioning alone gives no consistent gains among the one-band variants. The benefit therefore depends on the combined representation; differing training budgets limit attribution to conditioning structure alone.

E SUBJECTIVE EVALUATION 

We compare Dyna2Music with AudioLDM-ControlNet and SONIQUE on AIST++, selecting these representative baselines for their generation quality (Section 4.2). The study involves 10 participants, each completing 10 evaluation trials. Each trial presents three versions of the same dance video, paired with soundtracks generated by the three methods. Participants rate each version separately for overall musical quality (OVL) and dance-music relevance (REL) on a five-point scale, following Sun et al. (2025), yielding 100 ratings per method for each dimension. REL assesses perceived rhythmic correspondence with the dance. Presentation order is randomized and method identities are concealed. We report mean opinion scores (MOS) (Streijl et al., 2016) for each dimension, with higher values indicating better perceived quality or relevance. Table A2 reports mean OVL and REL scores of 4.3 and 4.5 for Dyna2Music, respectively. These exceed the highest baseline scores by 1.0 point for OVL (AudioLDM-ControlNet) and 1.1 points for REL (SONIQUE). The ratings support improvements in both perceived musical quality and dance-music relevance relative to the selected baselines. 18 Dyna2Music
