Abstract 

Modern graph generative models typically operate directly in the discrete graph space, ex-plicitly generating node and edge variables, which can become costly as graphs grow. In this paper, we perform generation explicitly on latent representations of entire graphs obtained from a pretrained Variational Autoencoder with high reconstruction fidelity. The generated repre-sentations, obtained through flow matching, are then decoded only at the final step. Across molecular benchmarks of increasing size, our approach achieves strong validity and FCD while offering a favorable quality-efficiency trade-off compared with state-of-the-art explicit graph gen-erative models. One of the main advantages of this formulation is that the graph representation only needs to be learned once, after which the same one can be reused across multiple generative objectives without retraining. We demonstrate generation guided by molecular properties and further introduce validity-aware generation though a classifier learned directly in latent space. All code will be made available upon acceptance. 

# 1 Introduction 

Recent advances in graph generation have been largely driven by diffusion and flow-based models operating directly on explicit node and edge representations [1, 2, 3]. These methods model the graph distribution in its original discrete space and have achieved strong results across several graph generation benchmarks, but they suffer from having to manipulate large objects (full adjacency ma-trices and node embeddings) as well as being potentially permutation-sensitive. An alternative line of work instead learns compact, permutation-invariant graph-level representations via autoencoder architectures [4, 5, 6, 7], decoupling representation learning from distribution modeling (see Sec-tion 2 for details). One main drawback is that such architectures are known to yield poor generative performance without domain-specific knowledge being injected [8, 9]. This raises the question of whether an approach could leverage the best of both world, by mov-ing distribution modeling entirely into such a continuous graph-level representation space; doing so should offer a competitive alternative to explicit graph generation in terms of sample quality, distributional fidelity, while retaining computational efficiency. This question is the central problem adressed in this work. Beyond unconditional generation, we ask whether the same latent repre-sentation can support controlled generation toward prescribed graph-level properties, and whether

# 2 Background and Related Work 

Flow matching and generative modeling Generative modeling is the process of learning how to sample from an unknown data distribution q, so as to obtain novel, valid and varied data points. A usual process is to sample a point z from a known and simple prior p and learn a transformation T

so that T (z) ∼ q. In a VAE [12], T is simply the decoder. More advanced methods, such as diffusion [13] and flow matching [10] rely on applying a multi-step process, where each step removes noise and injects signal. In particular, flow matching learns this distribution by regressing a time-dependent vector field vθ (zt, t ) that transports samples from a simple source distribution (e.g., a standard Gaussian) to p(z) along a prescribed probability path, avoiding the iterative denoising schedules of diffusion models while retaining comparable sample quality. Conditional generation [14] extends this formulation by conditioning q on desired properties of the generated object (i.e. a prompt). Flow matching enables this by conditioning the learned vector field directly [15]. 

Graph-Level Continuous Representations Let G denote the graph space, where a graph with 

n nodes is represented as an n×cn matrix of node features and an adjacency tensor of shape n×n×ce

representing edge classes. In contrast to node-level autoencoders [12] (which encodes the graph as a permutation-equivariant n × d matrix), a graph-level encoder E maps a complete graph G ∈ G to a fixed-dimensional permutation-invariant representation z = E(G) ∈ Rd, while a decoder D maps this representation back to graph space through ˆG = D(z), with ˆG an attempt at reconstructing G.Several recent architectures pursue this direction such as PIGVAE [4], GRALE [5], or GraViti [6] differing primarily in how permutation invariance is enforced and in the fidelity of reconstruction on larger, more heterogeneous graphs. These approaches, and more particularly GraViti, demonstrate that compact continuous representations can retain sufficient structural information for near-exact graph reconstruction on molecular datasets of different scales. In particular, PigVAE and GraViti provide a variational autoencoder architecture [16], which structures the latent space explicitly with downstream generation in mind (Section 3); nonetheless, while cheap, one-shot VAE sampling is known to fail at generating consistently valid molecules [17, 8, 18, 19], making it a poor generative model on its own. 

Generation in explicit graph space. Most recent graph generative models operate directly on node and edge variables, keeping the graph representation explicit throughout generation. Di-Gress [1] applies discrete denoising diffusion to categorical graph representations, while CatFlow [11] and DeFoG [2] similarly formulate generation directly in graph space through categorical or discrete Flow Matching. More recently, Categorical Flow Maps [3] introduced continuous trajectories over categorical variables by evolving them in the probability simplex, enabling few-step generation. Despite this continuous formulation, the generative state still explicitly represents node and edge categories rather than a learned representation of the graph itself. 

Generation through continuous representations. While latent generation approaches for graphs exist, they usually operate on node-level representations [20, 21, 22, 23] due to the only recent emergence of high-fidelity graph-level approaches. # 3 Method 

Latent Flow Matching We formulate graph generation as distribution learning in a graph-level continuous representation space. Representations are obtained from a pretrained GraViti-based variational autoencoder [6], since it is freely available and trainable and achieves high reconstruction quality. The encoder and decoder are considered as pretrained, and remain fixed, decoupling graph representation learning from generative modeling. Let E : G → Rd denote the graph encoder of our pretrained VAE and z = E(G) ∈ Rd the latent representation of a graph G. We learn the latent graph distribution using Flow Matching [10], transporting an isotropic Gaussian source distribution z0 ∼ N (0 , I ) ∈ Rd toward latent graph embeddings z1 ∼ pdata ∈ Rd, where pdata is the empirical distribution of encoder outputs obtained by applying E to the training graphs. We adopt the standard Flow Matching formulation, detailed in Appendix E. To improve the coupling between source and target samples, we employ mini-batch optimal transport [27], which promotes shorter and straighter transport trajectories while preserving the source and target marginals. In order to avoid data leakage from the VAE’s training, we adopt the same training, validation and test splits that are provided with GraViti. Therefore, both our model and the pretrained VAEs are trained on the same molecules exactly. We then learn a neural flow model over the distribution of graph representations, such that the complete generative trajectory is modeled in continuous latent space and only the final representation is decoded back to graph space. At generation time, the learned field is integrated from t = 0 to t = 1 and the resulting latent endpoint is decoded into a graph only after the last step. 

Validity Guidance We further exploit the differentiability of the latent dynamics to promote molecule validity. After an unconditional neural flow model is trained, a time-dependent classifier 

sω is trained on valid and invalid latent states. The classifier is time-dependent because, given 3an intermediate latent state zt and its flow time t, it predicts whether the corresponding sample is expected to decode into a valid graph at the end of the trajectory. To train this prediction throughout the flow, intermediate states are constructed along linear trajectories between noise samples and valid or invalid endpoint latents, with each state inheriting the validity label of its endpoint. Positive examples are obtained from encoded valid training graphs, while negative examples are generated by the latent Flow Matching model and retained when they decode into invalid graphs. The construction of this dataset is critical to obtaining informative guidance: rather than relying on easily separable negatives far from the graph latent distribution, we use hard negatives lying in regions actually explored by the generator. This encourages the classifier to learn the validity boundary relevant to the sampling trajectories. # 4 Experiments 

## 4.1 Experimental Setup 

Datasets. We evaluate our approach on QM9 [28] and PubChem [29], two molecular graph datasets with substantially different scales and structural complexity. For PubChem, we use two subsets clipped at a maximum size of 16 and 32 heavy atoms respectively. All datasets have 4 edge types, corresponding to simple, double, triple bonds, and one extra class for ”no bond”. Statistics are described in Table 1. 

Baselines. We compare latent generation against explicit graph generative models: the diffusion-based DiGress [1], a the flow-based Categorical Flow Maps [3]. We additionally report Gaussian sampling from the VAE prior as a latent-space baseline, allowing us to quantify the benefit of learning the latent graph distribution with flow matching. All models are trained and evaluated on the same splits. 

Evaluation protocol. For each configuration, we perform 20 independent generation runs of 1 ,024 molecules, for a total of 20 ,480 generated samples. Implementation details and hyperparameters are provided in appendix I.

## 4.3 Property-Guided Generation 

We study independent control of two complementary molecular properties, molecular weight (MW) and logP, prescribing one property at a time. MW mainly reflects molecular size and atomic composi-tion, whereas logP depends more strongly on molecular structure and functional-group arrangement. This allows us to assess whether a single continuous latent representation can support distinct forms of molecular control rather than a single property class. 

Independent property control. For each property, we condition the latent Flow Matching model on the target and optionally refine sampling with gradient-based guidance from a property predictor. Targets cover both high-density and more extreme regions of the training distribution, allowing us to assess controllability beyond frequent property values. Figure 2 compares requested and generated properties for the best guided configurations. Across QM9 and PubChem16, generated means closely track the targets over a broad range, showing that the same latent representation supports both unconditional generation and property control. Qualitative examples are provided in Appendix G. Although conditioning alone already provides effective property control, gradient-based guidance proves substantially more effective at improving target fidelity, with the largest gains observed for logP and consistent improvements for MW (Table 4). 

Influence of the latent representation. Both deterministic and variational GraViti represen-tations support effective property control, although the variational latent space consistently yields more accurate target matching (Appendix H). 8Validity under property control. Target fidelity alone is insufficient to characterize controlled generation: a model may reach increasingly extreme property values by moving toward latent regions that decode poorly. We therefore distinguish between global and local validity. Global validity evaluates the conditional generation regime in which target properties are sampled according to their empirical training distribution. It therefore gives greater weight to targets lying in high-density regions of the property space. Local validity instead evaluates validity separately at each prescribed target and reveals how generation quality evolves when moving toward rare or out-of-distribution property values. Under global conditioning, property guidance preserves high validity and uniqueness on both datasets, remaining close to the unconditional regime on QM9 and above 0 .92 validity with maximal uniqueness on PubChem16. Detailed results are reported in Appendix D. Global validity, however, can conceal substantial variations across the property range. Since tar-gets are sampled according to the empirical training distribution, the resulting average is dominated by high-density regions and provides limited information about the behavior of the model near the boundaries of, or beyond, the training distribution. We therefore additionally report local validity ,computed independently for each prescribed target value. Local validity and uniqueness remain high within the training distribution, while target-wise results in Appendix B show that validity degrades primarily for increasingly rare or out-of-distribution targets. These results also qualitatively indicate that our models remain reasonably robust beyond the training range. 

# 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we introduced a method for molecule generation using a flow matching algorithm in the latent space of a high-fidelity graph-level autoencoder, leveraging on the recent progress on whole-molecule representations. We showed that our approach reaches state-of-the-art distributional fidelity (measured as the FCD), even when set up against more complex explicit graph generation baselines. While a decrease in validity is observed compared to DiGress (up to 25 points on Pub-Chem32), the efficiency makes up for that by allowing to generate up to 10 times faster while retaining the larger distributional fidelity. We also demonstrate that our approach integrates guided generation seamlessly, leveraging the same latent representation and providing highly fidel results even outside of the dataset’s distribution ranges. Overall, this work offers a promising approach to graph and molecule generation processes that scale better, avoiding the high cost of in-graph-space explicit propagation.
