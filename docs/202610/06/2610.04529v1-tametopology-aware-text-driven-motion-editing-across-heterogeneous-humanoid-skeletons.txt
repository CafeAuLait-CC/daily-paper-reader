## ABSTRACT 

Text-driven motion editing modifies an existing motion sequence according to a text instruction while preserving the content of the source motion. Existing methods are typically built for a single, fixed skeletal topology, which limits their use in animation pipelines where characters differ in joint count and skeletal hierarchy. We present T OPOLOGY -A WARE MOTION EDITOR (TAME), a flow-matching transformer that edits motions on humanoid skeletons of varying topology. TAME represents motion as per-joint, per-frame tokens and models interactions among joints, across frames, and with the text instruction through skeletal, temporal, and text cross-attention layers. To make the skeletal attention follow each character’s hierarchy, TAME replaces full joint attention with Topology-Constrained Skeletal Propagation (TCSP), which restricts attention to one-hop kinematic neighbors in the skeleton’s adjacency matrix. We further introduce Edit-Focused Representation Alignment (EFRA), a self-distilled representation alignment strategy that aligns student features with cleaner EMA-teacher features exclusively on edit-relevant joint-time tokens, making edits faithful to the instruction. To make this setting train-able and comparable, we construct T OPO MOTION FIX , a multi-topology extension of MotionFix with seen- and unseen-topology evaluation protocols. TAME outper-forms previous methods in edit alignment and source preservation on MotionFix and reliably edits motions on unseen skeletons in T OPO MOTION FIX .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Character animation (Tevet et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024) is rarely completed in a single generation pass: an initial motion may not match the creator’s intent due to the ambiguity of high-level language instructions. Motion editing (Athanasiou et al., 2024; Delmas et al., 2023) therefore plays a crucial role in animation workflows: it refines an existing motion according to a text instruction, such as raising an arm higher, while keeping unedited motion unchanged. In animation production, a motion editor is expected to accommodate the varying skeletal topologies of different characters and to edit a motion faithfully according to the text instruction. Despite recent progress in text-driven motion editing (Li et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025), existing methods fall short in both respects. First, most existing methods overlook the broader need of diverse character animation: editing motions across different skeletal topologies. Instead, they are designed for a single fixed topology (Athanasiou et al., 2024). However, in real-world production environments (Gat et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024), human motion data commonly involves varying skeletal configurations beyond the canonical topology assumed by these methods. This topology mismatch limits the practical applicability of current motion editing methods. Second, existing methods struggle to realize the requested edit, so the edited motion is often misaligned with the instruction. For instance, when the required change is subtle, as in “stand without stretching leg,” current methods tend to ignore it, whereas when the instruction is complex or abstract, as in “act as if violin is being played,” the generated motion deviates substantially from the text semantics. To edit motion across different skeletal topologies while keeping each edit faithful to the instruction, we propose TAME (Topology-Aware Motion Editor), a flow-matching (Lipman et al., 2023) trans-former for text-driven motion editing. Existing motion editing methods (Athanasiou et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Delmas et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024) typically represent each pose as a single vector and focus mainly on temporal relationships while overlooking spatial interactions among skeletal joints. To address this limitation, TAME embeds motion as per-joint tokens at each frame, forming a skeleto-temporally structured representation that keeps the per-joint feature dimension fixed as the number of joints varies across skeletons. The network applies attention along the skeletal and temporal axes to model joint interactions and motion dynamics, while text cross-attention captures fine-grained interactions between the edit prompt and skeleto-temporal motion units. To handle motions on skeletons of varying topology, we introduce T OPOLOGY -C ONSTRAINED SKELETAL 

PROPAGATION (TCSP) for skeletal interaction: given the input topology, TCSP permits one-step communication only between a joint and its physical neighbors, so that topology becomes a structural communication rule rather than a learnable bias in full attention. Long-range coordination remains possible but must emerge through multi-layer propagation along the character’s kinematic chain. To make edits faithful to the instruction, we propose Edit-Focused Representation Alignment (EFRA), which imposes representation guidance on the edited parts of a motion (Fig. 2). Our key insight is that motion editing is an asymmetric learning problem: the source motion provides a direct reference for the unedited parts, whereas the edited parts can only be inferred from its interaction with the text instruction. The edited parts therefore call for stronger guidance. Representation alignment lends itself to this purpose: recent work has shown that a diffusion transformer generates better when its intermediate features are aligned to a stronger representation, and that this representation can come not only from an external encoder (Yu et al., 2025) but also from the network itself, whose features grow more informative as the noise decreases and the depth increases (Jiang et al., 2025a). Accordingly, EFRA realizes this guidance by self-distillation and applies the alignment to the edited parts. It aligns an earlier-layer student feature under higher noise to a later-layer teacher feature under lower noise. The alignment is restricted to the edit-relevant joint-time tokens, identified during training by a DTW edit map derived from each source–target pair. The teacher is an EMA copy of the student and is the network deployed at inference, so EFRA adds no inference cost. To train and evaluate TAME on heterogeneous humanoid skeletons, we construct T OPO MOTION FIX ,a multi-topology extension of MotionFix (Athanasiou et al., 2024) that retargets its source–target– instruction triplets onto a pool of humanoid rigs with different joint counts and hierarchies, and provides both seen- and unseen-topology evaluation protocols. In summary, our contributions are fourfold. First, we propose TAME, to the best of our knowledge the first text-driven motion editor for heterogeneous humanoid skeletons, whose topology-constrained skeletal propagation (TCSP) turns the input topology into a structural communication rule of the network. Second, we introduce EFRA, a self-distillation strategy that strengthens guidance on the 2Under review edited parts of a motion and thereby makes edits more faithful to the instruction. Third, we construct TOPO MOTION FIX , a multi-topology benchmark built on MotionFix with seen- and unseen-topology evaluation protocols. Fourth, TAME outperforms prior methods on MotionFix in both edit alignment and source preservation and generalizes robustly to unseen skeleton topologies in T OPO MOTION FIX ,with ablations validating the contributions of TCSP and EFRA. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 TEXT -D RIVEN MOTION EDITING 

Early 3D motion editing relied on handcrafted spatial-temporal constraints to manipulate trajecto-ries (Witkin & Kass, 1988; Gleicher, 1997), where each edit demands manually specified rules and does not generalize to free-form text instructions. Motion editing methods including DNO (Karun-ratanakul et al., 2024), MoMo (Raab et al., 2024), MotionCLR (Chen et al., 2024), and SALAD (Hong et al., 2025) manipulate the attention or initial noise of pretrained motion diffusion models without further training; in the absence of paired editing supervision, however, these methods struggle to deliver fine-grained semantic edits while preserving the source motion. LLM-driven editors such as Iterative Motion Editing (Goel et al., 2024) and CoMo (Huang et al., 2024) translate text into symbolic operations applied to a frozen motion model; however, they rely on a textual description of the source motion as input rather than the motion data itself. MotionFix (Athanasiou et al., 2024) introduces the canonical triplet-based supervised setting (source motion, target motion, instruction) for text-driven 3D motion editing. Building on it, SimMotionEdit (Li et al., 2025) and PartMotionEdit (Yang et al., 2025) add motion similarity prediction as an auxiliary task at the frame and part level, respectively, while MotionReFit (Jiang et al., 2025b) blends source and target motions through the MotionCut-Mix augmentation; more recently, OmniME (Shi et al., 2026) adds negative supervision through a text–motion triplet loss, and Han & Kim (2026) fuse frame-wise and joint-wise motion features and predict joint-wise motion differences as an auxiliary task. These methods, however, are designed for a single fixed topology and struggle to realize the edit instruction faithfully. TAME addresses both limitations with a topology-aware editing architecture that generalizes across heterogeneous humanoid skeletons and a self-distilled representation alignment that provides more informative guidance on the edited regions. 2.2 SKELETON -A GNOSTIC AND ARBITRARY -T OPOLOGY MOTION MODELING 

Existing methods modeling motion across diverse skeletal structures (Lee et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024; Gat et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Khani et al., 2025; Mo et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026b; Chen et al., 2025a; Xu et al., 2026) focus on generation, representation, or retargeting rather than editing. Early work such as SAME (Lee et al., 2023) learns a skeleton-agnostic motion embedding, and OmniMotionGPT (Yang et al., 2024) extends generation to animal skeletons. Recent generation works further diversify backbones and data scale, including AnyTop (Gat et al., 2025) with a Graphormer-style attention bias (Ying et al., 2021), UniMoGen (Khani et al., 2025), OmniSkel (Liu et al., 2025), PUMPS (Mo et al., 2025), X-MoGen (Wang et al., 2026b), OmniZoo (Chen et al., 2025a), and NECromancer (Xu et al., 2026). A separate retargeting thread pioneered by skeleton-aware networks (Aberman et al., 2020) and Motion2Motion (Chen et al., 2025b) transfers a given motion across skeletons rather than editing it. Architecturally, prior methods such as AnyTop (Gat et al., 2025) inject topology through attention biases that allow long-range joint communication within a single layer. TAME instead enforces a hard one-hop adjacency mask (TCSP), so that distant joints interact only via multi-layer propagation along the kinematic chain. 2.3 REPRESENTATION ALIGNMENT IN DIFFUSION MODELS 

Representation alignment has emerged as an effective auxiliary regulariser for diffusion transformer training. REPA (Yu et al., 2025) aligns the noisy hidden states of the denoiser with clean features from a pretrained visual encoder. Subsequent work traces its gain to spatial structure rather than global semantics (Singh et al., 2025), extends alignment to pixel-space transformers (Shin et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a), and builds the encoder into the latent space itself (Zheng et al., 2026). SRA (Jiang et al., 2025a) removes the external dependence by self-distilling across noise levels, matching an earlier student feature under higher noise to a later EMA-teacher feature under lower noise. To the 3Under review best of our knowledge, EFRA is the first to apply representation alignment to text-driven motion editing, through a token-wise dual-timestep schedule driven by a DTW-derived edit mask.

c, our goal is to generate an edited motion Mt ∈ RT ×J×D that semantically aligns with the instruction while preserving source motion content. The skeleton structure is represented as S = {N S , A S },where NS = {nj }Jj=1 is the set of joint names and AS ∈ { 0, 1}J×J is the binary adjacency matrix, with AS [i, j ] = 1 if and only if joints i and j are kinematic neighbors. As shown in Fig. 2, our method TAME has two components: a topology-aware flow-matching (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2023) transformer with topology-constrained skeletal propagation (TCSP) that serves as the editing backbone (Section 3.2), and EFRA, an edit-focused representation alignment strategy (Section 3.3).

Our design starts from a key observation: motion editing is an asymmetric learning problem. The unedited parts of the target closely follow the source motion, which serves as a reliable reference for them. The edited parts, in contrast, have to be inferred from the source motion and the text instruction, and therefore call for stronger guidance. We obtain this guidance from self-representation alignment (SRA) (Jiang et al., 2025a), which builds on the observation that the intermediate features of a diffusion transformer become more informative as the noise decreases and the depth increases, and therefore aligns the weaker features of the network to its better ones without any external representation component. Accordingly, EFRA aligns an earlier-layer student feature under higher noise to a later-layer teacher feature under lower noise, where the teacher is an EMA copy of the student (Fig. 2, upper right). Rather than applying the alignment to the whole target motion, EFRA restricts it to the edit-relevant joint-time tokens, identified by a DTW edit map derived from each source–target pair (lower centre). The noise levels of the student and the teacher are assigned by edit-aware dual-timestep scheduling: the student takes a globally higher noise (lower left), and the teacher takes the lower noise only on those tokens (lower right). Below we describe the three parts in turn: the DTW edit map, the edit-aware dual-timestep scheduling, and the alignment objective. 

DTW edit map. Restricting the alignment to the edited parts requires supervision on where the edit takes place, which can be recovered by comparing the source motion with the target motion. Prior work (Li et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025) derives such supervision only at the frame or body-part level, too coarse for joint-time tokens. To obtain a skeletal-temporally structured edit signal, we use 

Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) (Sakoe & Chiba, 1978) to align source and target frames, read out joint-level distances along the alignment, and threshold them after per-sample min-max normalisation into a binary edit mask E ∈ { 0, 1}T ×J , where E[i, j ] = 1 marks the joint-time tokens to be edited. 5Under review The mask is precomputed offline for each source–target pair. ## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We start by introducing the multi-topology dataset T OPO MOTION FIX (Section 4.1), together with our evaluation metrics and seen/unseen-topology protocols (Section 4.2). We then present the main quantitative results, comparing TAME with state-of-the-art motion-editing methods (Section 4.3). Next, we provide ablations on the key components of TAME (Section 4.4). Finally, we demonstrate qualitative results on both seen and unseen topologies (Section 4.5). 6Under review 4.1 TOPO MOTION FIX DATASET 

TOPO MOTION FIX comprises three components: textual edit instructions, source and target motion sequences, and the corresponding skeletal structures. Building on MotionFix (Athanasiou et al., 2024), we retarget its motion-skeleton pairs onto a pool of 83 humanoid rigs sourced from LAFAN1, ACCAD, TotalCapture, PFNN, and SFU motion-capture datasets, augmented with skeleton randomisation following (Lee et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025); the full construction procedure is detailed in Appendix A. TOPO MOTION FIX contains 25 ,794 distinct skeletal configurations, 40 ,304 unique text-motion-skeleton triplets, and 1,416 unique textual edit instructions, inheriting MotionFix’s train/test partition. 4.2 EVALUATION SETUP 

Evaluation protocols. We define two evaluation protocols, both using the test pairs and the evaluator provided by MotionFix (Athanasiou et al., 2024). In the seen-topology protocol, we train on MotionFix’s training set with only the input representation converted, enabling fair comparison with prior fixed-topology motion-editing methods. In the unseen-topology protocol, the test set uses 

10 rigs, the SMPL skeleton and 9 held-out rigs, while the training set uses 83 rigs. The held-out rigs are excluded from training and from the skeleton randomisation of Algorithm 1, adapted from SAME (Lee et al., 2023), so their topologies are fixed and unseen. The construction of the held-out test sets and the evaluation of motions generated on non-SMPL skeletons are detailed in Appendix B. 

Evaluation metrics. We evaluate the semantic alignment of edited motions with both the text instruction and the source motion. Following MotionFix (Athanasiou et al., 2024), alignment is measured via motion-to-motion retrieval using pretrained TMR as a feature extractor. We report R@1, R@2, R@3 and average rank (AvgR) on both generated-to-target ( g→t) and generated-to-source (g→s) retrieval, with a fixed candidate batch of 32 test-set motions (fixed across runs to eliminate randomness), and under the test-set setting (deferred to Appendix D). 

Baselines. For the seen-topology protocol, we compare TAME with current motion-editing meth-ods, including MDM (Tevet et al., 2023), MDM-BP and TMED (Athanasiou et al., 2024), Motion-ReFit (Jiang et al., 2025b), SimMotionEdit (Li et al., 2025), OmniME (Shi et al., 2026), and the cross-axis fusion model of Han & Kim (2026). For the unseen-topology protocol, no prior text-driven motion editing method operates on arbitrary skeletons; to validate the effectiveness of our method, we adapt AnyTop (Gat et al., 2025), an arbitrary-topology motion generation framework, by adapting its topological conditioning scheme into our TAME editing pipeline. 

Implementation details. We adopt rectified flow matching (Liu et al., 2023; Lipman et al., 2023) with 50 sampling steps at inference on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 ( 48 GB) GPU, 9 TAME blocks, and a latent dimension of 512 . Each model is trained on 2 NVIDIA RTX Pro 6000 (96 GB) GPUs for approximately one day. More implementation details can be found in Appendix E. 4.3 MAIN RESULTS

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We studied text-driven motion editing across heterogeneous humanoid skeletons, a setting that better reflects the diverse character data encountered in real animation pipelines. To make the setting trainable and evaluable, we constructed T OPO MOTION FIX , a multi-topology extension of MotionFix with seen- and unseen-topology evaluation protocols. We proposed TAME, a topology-aware flow-matching transformer that represents motion as joint-time tokens and uses topology-constrained skeletal propagation (TCSP) to confine joint attention to one-hop kinematic neighbors, so that the 9Under review model operates natively on skeletons of varying topology. Since the edited parts of a motion have to be inferred from the source motion and the instruction and therefore call for stronger guidance, we further introduced EFRA, a self-distilled representation alignment strategy that aligns the student to a cleaner EMA teacher only on the edit tokens identified by a DTW edit map. On MotionFix, TAME outperforms fixed-topology editing methods on both edit alignment and source preservation. ## A TOPO MOTION FIX CONSTRUCTION DETAILS 

This appendix details the T OPO MOTION FIX construction summarised in Section 4.1. We retarget each MotionFix editing pair onto a set of sampled skeletons while keeping the original text instruction unchanged. The retargeting pipeline largely reuses the protocol of SAME (Lee et al., 2023), which is also adopted by SkeleMotion-3D (Liu et al., 2025). The full pipeline is split into two stages. Algorithm 1 defines the per-skeleton augmentation routine: starting from a base skeleton sampled from a character pool, we randomly add or remove joints around the spine and neck, optionally insert dummy joints at the hip, lower back, and shoulder, zero out random end-effector offsets, scale per-axis bone offsets uniformly with [smin , s max ] = [0 .7, 1.3] ,apply left-right symmetric scaling, and randomly sample a global height. Algorithm 2 drives the overall dataset construction: for each MotionFix editing pair, we first append the original SMPL pair, then generate augmented skeletons via Algorithm 1, retarget both source and target motions onto each augmented skeleton (the editing pair must share the same target skeleton), and convert each retargeted clip into our per-joint motion representation. The resulting dataset packages the 6,730 source-target editing pairs into 40 ,304 editing instances (6,730 on the original SMPL skeleton plus 33 ,574 on randomised humanoid skeletons), spanning 

25 ,794 unique skeletal configurations. Each instance carries one source motion and one target motion on a shared skeleton, giving 80 ,608 motion-skeleton clips at the per-motion level. The 6,730 textual editing instructions are inherited from MotionFix and contain 1,416 unique sentences across 56 ,728 

total words, averaging 8.4 words per instruction.

## E IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

We adopt rectified flow matching (Liu et al., 2023; Lipman et al., 2023) with the linear interpolation of Section 3.3. The training timestep t ∈ [0 , 1] is drawn uniformly at random from a grid on [0 , 1] with spacing 1/1,000 , and inference uses 50 Euler sampling steps. Following Athanasiou et al. (2024), we apply two-way classifier-free guidance with a guidance scale of 2.5 for the text condition and 

2.0 for the source motion condition, with dropout 0.05 applied independently on each of the text, source-motion, and joint conditions during training. We encode the natural-language edit instruction with a frozen CLIP-ViT-L/14 text encoder (Radford et al., 2021) (injected through cross-attention), and the joint-name strings of the target skeleton with the pretrained T5-base text encoder (Raffel et al., 2020) (added to the per-joint token embeddings). The flow-matching transformer consists of L = 9 TAME blocks with 8 attention heads, a latent dimension of 512 , and an FFN dimension of 1024 . Our edit-focused representation alignment (EFRA) uses an EMA teacher with momentum α = 0 .9999 , a projection head with hidden dimension 1024 ,and an alignment loss connecting student layer 4 to teacher layer 7 via cosine distance restricted to the DTW-derived edit mask (threshold τg = 0 .2), with auxiliary loss weight λEFRA = 0 .5 relative to the rectified-flow objective. We train using the AdamW optimiser (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) with an initial learning rate of 

1×10 −4 decayed to 1×10 −6 along a cosine schedule with 300 warmup steps, weight decay 0.01 , for 

1,000 epochs at an effective batch size of 128 , taking approximately one day on two NVIDIA RTX Pro 6000 (96 GB) GPUs. We implement TAME in PyTorch Lightning and train every ablation from scratch with this configuration unless otherwise noted. 

## F PERCEPTUAL STUDY 

To complement the retrieval-based metrics in the main paper, we conduct a perceptual study with human participants to assess how well TAME’s edits align with text instructions and how plausible the resulting motions appear, compared with SimMotionEdit under the seen-topology protocol and AnyTop under the unseen-topology protocol. F.1 SETUP 

We run two parallel studies, one for the seen-topology protocol and one for the unseen-topology protocol of Table 1. Each participant in a study observes 10 sample sets, randomly drawn from the MotionFix test split. Within each sample set, the participant is shown the source motion, the text edit instruction, and three candidate edited motions: • Seen-topology study : ground truth (GT), TAME, and SimMotionEdit (Li et al., 2025). • Unseen-topology study : ground truth (GT), TAME, and AnyTop (Gat et al., 2025). The three candidates are presented in random order within each sample set to avoid positional bias. Participants rate each candidate on two metrics, both on a 3-point Likert scale (higher is better): • Alignment with text : how well the edited motion follows the instruction, irrespective of motion quality. 1 = the edited motion is completely different from the instruction; 2 = the edited motion follows only some parts of the instruction; 3 = the edited motion fully follows the instruction. • Plausibility : the quality of the edited motion, irrespective of instruction-following. 1 =severe issues (self-intersections, unrealistic poses or movements); 2 = minor issues (foot sliding, plausible but slightly non-humanlike movements); 3 = no issues.
