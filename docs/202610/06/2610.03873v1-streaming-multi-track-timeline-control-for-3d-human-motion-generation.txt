ABSTRACT
Text-driven human motion generation has advanced substantially, yet most meth-
ods assume instructions are available before synthesis. Interactive applications
require responding to new instructions while continuing ongoing actions, such as
answering a phone while walking. Existing approaches address streaming genera-
tion or simultaneous composition without explicitly combining streaming instruc-
tion arrival with independently timed, overlapping actions. We introduce stream-
ing multi-track timeline control and propose TimelineControl to incorporate new
instructions alongside ongoing actions. Interval-aware conditioning preserves in-
struction timing, while causal part-structured representations and part-aware de-
noising coordinate concurrent actions across body regions. We also construct
TimelineMotion, a dataset with overlapping instruction intervals and body-part
annotations. Experiments on TimelineMotion and MTT demonstrate improved
semantic alignment and temporal adherence over evaluated streaming baselines,
including models retrained on the same data. Ablations and human evaluations
validate our design, complemented by spatial conditioning and humanoid execu-
tion demonstrations. Our code, data and models will become publicly available.

INTRODUCTION
Text-driven human motion generation has made substantial progress in synthesizing realistic move-
ments from natural language (Petrovich et al., 2022; Tevet et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Guo et al.,
2024), including action sequences and simultaneous motions (Athanasiou et al., 2022; Petrovich
1

et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026). However, most approaches assume that instructions are available be-
fore generation begins. Interactive applications in gaming and robotics require both reactivity and
compositionality: a character may need to answer a ringing phone while walking, or avoid a mov-
ing obstacle while continuing another activity. Such interactions require incorporating new actions
while preserving compatible ongoing behaviors and their individual timings.
This setting poses three coupled challenges: generating motion without access to future instructions,
maintaining distinct temporal scopes for concurrent actions, and coordinating their effects across
body regions while preserving whole-body coherence. Previous work addresses these challenges
separately. Streaming methods such as MotionStreamer (Xiao et al., 2025), ActionPlan (Nazarenus
et al., 2026), and ARDY (Zhao et al., 2026) support changing text conditions, but their described
interfaces do not explicitly maintain independently timed, overlapping instructions. Conversely,
SINC (Athanasiou et al., 2023), STMC (Petrovich et al., 2024), and FrankenMotion (Li et al., 2026)
support simultaneous or temporal composition assume pre-specified conditions.
We introduce streaming multi-track timeline control, where instructions arrive at their start times,
retain separate temporal intervals, and may overlap with ongoing actions, as illustrated in Fig-
ure 1. Our framework, TimelineControl, extends ActionPlan with online multi-prompt conditioning
and part-aware denoising. Instructions are encoded separately, with interval encoding and visibil-
ity masks determining their temporal applicability. A causal part-structured autoencoder provides
anatomical motion latents, while text-derived relevance estimates guide each body region toward ap-
plicable instructions. We combine body-part and global features to produce coordinated whole-body
motion. New instructions guide future motion without modifying previously generated motions.
For training and evaluation, we construct TimelineMotion with overlapping instruction intervals
and body-part annotations through STMC-based composition of HumanML3D motions, followed
by refinement and filtering. Experiments on TimelineMotion and MTT demonstrate strong semantic
alignment and temporal adherence, outperforming evaluated streaming baselines, including Action-
Plan retrained on the same data. Ablations validate our conditioning and part-aware components,
while human evaluations favor our text consistency over ActionPlan and offline STMC. We
further validate our method by showing strong single-prompt generation, spatial conditioning, and
humanoid execution through a motion-tracking controller. Our main contributions are:
• We introduce streaming multi-track timeline control and develop TimelineControl to incorporate
newly arriving instructions alongside ongoing actions, combining interval-aware multi-prompt
conditioning with part-aware motion generation.
• We construct TimelineMotion, a dataset of motion sequences paired with overlapping instruc-
tion intervals and body-part annotations, supporting training and evaluation of streaming compo-
sitional generation.
• We demonstrate strong semantic alignment and temporal adherence on TimelineMotion and MTT,
supported by baseline comparisons, ablations, and human evaluations. We further demonstrate
spatial conditioning and humanoid motion execution.

RELATED WORK
Text-to-motion generation. Text-to-motion methods represent data with raw motion (Tevet et al.,
2023), continuous latents (Petrovich et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023) or discrete tokens (Guo et al.,
2022b; Zhang et al., 2023a; 2026a). Generation strategies include autoregression (Guo et al., 2022b;
Zhang et al., 2023a), diffusion (Tevet et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2023), masked
token prediction (Guo et al., 2024), and masked autoregression (Meng et al., 2025; He et al., 2026).
For sequential composition, TEACH (Athanasiou et al., 2022) conditions successive actions on mo-
tion history, while DoubleTake (Shafir et al., 2024) and FlowMDM (Barquero et al., 2024) syn-
thesize transitions through coordinated denoising. In discrete motion-token space, M2D2M (Chi
et al., 2024) combines independent and joint denoising to compose action sequences. For simulta-
neous actions, SINC (Athanasiou et al., 2023) constructs spatial compositions, while MultiAct (Sala
et al., 2026) uses attention guidance to strengthen underrepresented actions in composite prompts.
MotionMaster (Jiang et al., 2026) combines quantized motion tokens with a pretrained multimodal
language model for compositional generation and body-part editing. STMC (Petrovich et al., 2024)
brings temporal and spatial composition together by assigning text intervals to body regions and
combining denoising predictions across tracks, given a complete timeline before synthesis. Tem-

porally localized text conditioning also appears in video generation (Wu et al., 2025b; Shu et al.,
2026). None of these methods support streaming multi-track timeline control.
Part-aware motion modeling. Part-aware representations use hierarchical upper- and lower-body
streams for action composition (Ghosh et al., 2021) or body-part attention in the motion en-
coder (Zhong et al., 2023). PB-MLD (Liu et al., 2026) uses separate upper- and lower-body varia-
tional autoencoders with a joint Transformer denoiser. SALAD (Hong et al., 2025) preserves skeletal
structure in its latent representation and combines temporal, skeletal, and text attention for genera-
tion and editing. Part-specific generation uses inter-part communication in ParCo (Zou et al., 2024),
part-specific text with full-body refinement in LGTM (Sun et al., 2024), and part-aware text ground-
ing with holistic-part fusion in ParTY (Heo et al., 2026). Other approaches condition motion on spa-
tial and temporal descriptions (Zhang et al., 2023c) or compose atomic motions for open-vocabulary
generation (Fan et al., 2026). FineMotion (Wu et al., 2025a) provides detailed motion annotations,
while FrankenMotion (Li et al., 2026) pairs hierarchical temporal annotations with a diffusion model
conditioned on pre-specified, temporally structured part descriptions. We use six anatomical latents
in a causal autoencoder to support part-wise denoising during streaming generation.
Streaming and interactive motion generation. Causal tokenizers encode motion as discrete to-
kens (Jiang et al., 2025) or continuous latents, as in MotionStreamer (Xiao et al., 2025). Autoregres-
sive diffusion models generate motion incrementally from motion history (Shi et al., 2024; Chen
et al., 2024b). Diffusion Forcing (Chen et al., 2024a) introduces token-specific noise levels, which
CMDM (Yu et al., 2026) applies to causal motion generation and FloodDiffusion (Cai et al., 2026)
adapts to changing text. ActionPlan (Nazarenus et al., 2026) jointly generates motion and frame-
level action plans with latent-specific noise levels, while PRISM (Ling et al., 2026) combines per-
joint causal latents with flow matching and Self Forcing (Huang et al., 2025). We extend Action-
Plan’s joint formulation to prompts that arrive independently during generation and may overlap
in time. Spatial conditioning complements language through trajectories and keyframes (Karun-
ratanakul et al., 2023), joint-position constraints (Xie et al., 2024), and constraints for masked mo-
tion synthesis (Pinyoanuntapong et al., 2025). DartControl (Zhao et al., 2025) supports interactive
motion control, while ARDY (Zhao et al., 2026) combines explicit root trajectories with body latents
for online text and spatial conditioning. Related settings include online two-character interaction in
Ready-to-React (Cen et al., 2025) and motion generation with physics-based character control in
CLoSD (Tevet et al., 2025). In this paper, we mainly focus on online streaming timeline control and
treat spatial targets as optional conditions on the timeline generator.

Online multi-track timeline control.
We follow ActionPlan (Nazarenus et al., 2026) for the
streaming setup, but we also assume that motion instructions may arrive at arbitrary times, includ-
ing when another instruction is not completed. We aim to generate motion of each instruction in the
correct interval. An instruction i is represented as ei = (ℓi, [si, ui)), where ℓi is a textual prompt
describing the action and si, ui are start and end interval positions in the same window-local latent-
index coordinates as n. During streaming, z belongs to the current denoising window, containing
previously emitted motion as fixed history and a continuation to be generated. Thus, N counts
latent entries within this window, with index n. At a streaming step τ, the received instructions
overlapping the current denoising window Wτ form Cτ = {ei | si ≤τ, [si, ui)∩Wτ̸ = ∅}, where
Wτ is the current denoising window. The streaming time τ is expressed in the same window-local
latent-index coordinates as n, si, and ui; these coordinates are updated whenever the window
advances. Let nτ denote the first of the N latent entries to be generated in causal generation: z<nτ
is retained as context, while znτ :N is to be generated. The denoiser vθ(zt, t, Cτ) processes the
whole window, but noise updates apply only to the continuation. As completed motion is emitted,
it becomes fixed history and the window advances. When a new instruction arrives at time τ ′, we
update the conditions to Cτ ′ and regenerate the continuation znτ′:N in the current window. We now
describe how we enable the estimation of vθ(zt, t, Cτ).

METHOD
We introduce TimelineControl and show its architecture in Fig. 2. Our method is built on Action-
Plan (Nazarenus et al., 2026), and trained with Diffusion Forcing (Chen et al., 2024a) to impose
streaming generation. To allow multi-track conditioning, we use online multi-prompt conditioning,
aligning motion features with available streamed prompts (Sec. 4.2). We also find that part-aware
representations help disambiguate overlapping instructions, as different instructions may impact dif-
ferent body parts. We exploit this with a part-structured autoencoder (Sec. 4.1) and with part-aware
and global features (Sec. 4.3) to guide individual regions while maintaining whole-body awareness.
4.1
CAUSAL PART-STRUCTURED AUTOENCODER
Part-structured representation.
For multi-track control, we assume that different instructions
may impact different parts of the body. Hence, we benefit from part-structured representations in the
latent space in which the motion generator is operating, similarly to related works (Heo et al., 2026;
Hong et al., 2025). In practice, we partition body motion into six regions: torso, head, left and right
arms, and left and right legs. Each entry zn of the sequence introduced in Section 3 contains six body
latents bk
n ∈R8, where k ∈1, . . . , 6 indexes these regions. Following ARDY (Zhao et al., 2026),
root motion bypasses the body encoder, keeping global position and heading explicit for spatial
conditioning. The three position coordinates and the sine and cosine of heading over the same four
frames form rn ∈R20. The motion representation is zn = [rn; b1
n; . . . ; b6
n] ∈R68, with z ∈RN×68.

Architecture.
We define our autoencoder for causal streaming-oriented generation. The encoder
and decoder each comprise three causal temporal-convolution stages, compressing the motion by a
factor of 4. Within these stages, skeleton-graph message passing connects the torso stream to the
head and each limb (Hong et al., 2025). Temporal convolutions capture each region’s motion history,
while graph communication provides context from connected regions without merging their repre-
sentations. The decoder additionally conditions on root yaw rate, horizontal velocity, and height
derived from the explicit trajectory. Following MotionStreamer (Xiao et al., 2025), we train the
autoencoder with reconstruction and KL losses, and add additional acceleration loss to reduce jitter,
then freeze it during generator training. The detailed architecture is illustrated in Appendix B.1.
4.2
ONLINE MULTI-PROMPT CONDITIONING
Interval-aware text attention.
To implement the conditioning vθ(zt, t, Cτ), we must allow dif-
ferent instructions to influence different portions of the motion, with overlaps. We assume text cross
attention for conditioning. Let F = [f1; . . . ; fN] be the hidden features entering the cross-attention
layer. These are computed from the noisy motion input zt by the preceding layers. In the first layer,
they are zn. Each row fn retains the temporal index n of the motion sequence and supplies the query
for that position. Section 4.3 specifies how F evolves inside the network.
We encode each prompt ℓi ∈Cτ independently with a frozen CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) text en-
coder and stack the resulting sentence embeddings hi into H = [h1; . . . ; hJ], where J = |Cτ| is

tary interval encoding (RoTE) to keys using each instruction’s midpoint and duration, as in TIE (Shu
et al., 2026). We then define the score matrix W = RoPE(Q) RoTE(K)⊤/

4.4
TRAINING DATA
Since the most relevant timeline dataset MTT (Petrovich et al., 2024) has only testing data and there
is no training data for multi-track streaming motion generation, we construct TimelineMotion, a
dataset specific for the task of 44,944 motion sequences with overlapping text intervals, from Hu-
manML3D (Guo et al., 2022a) using STMC-based motion composition (Petrovich et al., 2024). Each
timeline uses source clips from a single HumanML3D split. We first use Qwen3-8B (Yang et al.,
2025) to extract impacted body parts and motion attributes for HumanML3D captions. We then com-
pose text labels in multiple tracks, using annotated labels for assessing motion compatibility. For in-
stance, it is not feasible to simultaneously clap and point a finger with the left hand, because both ac-
tions impact that hand. Composition of different instructions can be sequential or parallel. The body
part labels are also used to train the classifier in Section 4.3. We then generate motion associated
to the composed instructions. We first initialize non-overlapping intervals from their HumanML3D
source-motion crops. Within overlapping intervals, instead, we use STMC to perform body-part
stitching and temporal composition to combine source motions according to the annotated body-part
assignments. Composing motions with STMC from different clips can potentially introduce abrupt
changes around the boundaries. We refine these transition regions to improve continuity, by applying
a similar refinement pipeline to InterPose (Zhang et al., 2026b). In short, we check for discontinu-
ities by thresholding joints’ position and rotation differences across frames, and smooth detected
discontinuities with interpolation. Finally, we also discard sequences which still present abrupt
translations or excessive jitter. Further dataset construction details are provided in Appendix A.

EXPERIMENTS
5.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Datasets and baselines.
We evaluate on the test sets of timeline dataset TimelineMotion and
MTT (Petrovich et al., 2024), the most relevant dataset with multi-track instructions available. We
further evaluate the performance on non-streaming text dataset HumanML3D (Guo et al., 2022a).
Note no baseline natively supports streaming multi-track conditioning. For streaming baselines,
we compare with ActionPlan (Nazarenus et al., 2026), which also serves as our backbone, Motion-
Streamer (Xiao et al., 2025), DartControl (Zhao et al., 2025), and ARDY (Zhao et al., 2026). Our
model and all baselines marked with ∗are trained jointly on TimelineMotion and HumanML3D.
Streaming methods receive each prompt at its annotated start time. For baselines without native
conditioning on multiple text intervals, we update a single caption at every instruction start and
end: one active prompt is passed unchanged, and multiple active prompts are joined with “while”.
Retrained baselines also use “while” to join overlapping descriptions within each training window.
STMC (Petrovich et al., 2024) and FrankenMotion (Li et al., 2026) are evaluated offline with the
complete timeline with explicit body-part labels and body text intervals, respectively. FrankenMo-
tion additionally receives a sequence-level caption formed from the original event descriptions. On
HumanML3D, it receives only the sequence-level caption.
Evaluation metrics. We use TMR-based R-Precision, text-motion similarity (M2T), and similarity
to source (M2M) to assess sequence and body-part alignment following (Petrovich et al., 2023; Li
et al., 2026). We use FID (Heusel et al., 2017) to compare distributions of complete generated and
reference motions, also including FID+ following STMC (Petrovich et al., 2024). Transition dis-
tance (Trans) (Petrovich et al., 2024) assesses continuity at instruction boundaries and it is reported
in centimeters. Autoencoder reconstruction is evaluated with FID and mean per-joint position error
(MPJPE, in millimeters) following Xiao et al. (2025). We also measure if generated motions express
each instruction within its requested interval. To do so, we score each instruction against sliding mo-
tion windows across the complete timeline using TMR embeddings. We then quantify with average
precision (AP) and area-under-the-curve (AUC) if windows centered inside the requested instruction
interval receive higher scores than those outside. More details in Appendix C.
Training/inference. We train jointly on TimelineMotion and HumanML3D and each timeline win-
dow contains up to 78 prediction latents and 8 clean history latents. On a NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada
at batch size one, sampling takes 280 ms for the first motion token and 35 ms per subsequent token.
We also use the plan branch of ActionPlan unchanged from theirs. Full details are in App. B.

5.3
ABLATION STUDIES
Table 4 evaluates our architectural choices on TimelineMotion, to assess streaming performance, and
on HumanML3D, for evaluating loss of non-streaming capabilities. Further ablations on attention
normalization, self-conditioning, and training-window sampling can be found in Appendix D.
We ablate conditioning components in Table 4 (top). We first remove the M and S masks and rely
naively on RoTE only for multi-timeline conditioning (RoTE only). This reduces retrieval while
increasing FID on both datasets, demonstrating the need of our attention masking for streaming
multi-track motion generation. Removing RoTE (w/o RoTE) leaves the masks intact but removes
temporal sequence information from the encoded text. This has significant effects, with R@3 de-
creasing from 0.603 to 0.572 on TimelineMotion. This shows that to benefit from our conditioning
mechanism, we need appropriate encoding of the timeline arrival times and order.
We now ablate components of our part-aware denoising algorithm in Table 4 (bottom). We first
remove the bias B from the multi-prompt conditioning (w/o part-aware bias B). Most metrics de-
crease, showing that our representation exploits correctly the attention on multiple body parts, which
B enhances. We also propose a w/o part branch setup that only uses the global state g to predict ve-
locity. Note that g still receives information on the prompts with online multi-prompt conditioning.
From the results, we considerably increase FID (0.027→0.042 on HumanML3D, 0.017→0.030 on
TimelineMotion), showing that the task highly benefits from part-aware processing.

6
CONCLUSION
We introduced streaming multi-track timeline control, where independently timed instructions arrive
during motion generation and may overlap. Our framework, TimelineControl, combines interval-
aware text conditioning with part-aware denoising to coordinate concurrent actions. We also con-
structed TimelineMotion to support training and evaluation in this setting. Experiments show im-
proved performance over the evaluated baselines on TimelineMotion and MTT. We further demon-
strated spatial conditioning and execution of generated motions on a humanoid robot. Future work
will explore online revision and cancellation of instructions, as well as resolving conflicting requests
while preserving motion continuity.

A.2
TIMELINE CONSTRUCTION
We randomly sample source clips from the same HumanML3D split and consider sequential or
overlapping temporal arrangements. For overlapping clips, we exclude pairs that require the same
body regions, contain conflicting root motion or locomotion, involve incompatible poses or large
stance changes, or contain full-body actions. The primary body-part labels determine the source
motion assigned to each anatomical region during STMC composition, as described in Section 4.4.
We refine transition and overlap regions with interpolation to improve continuity while retaining
the surrounding source motion. Following InterPose (Zhang et al., 2026b), refinement uses spher-
ical interpolation for joint rotations and cubic Hermite interpolation where root trajectories require
smoothing. We additionally identify composition artifacts around timeline boundaries from decoded
joint motion. We first express joint positions relative to the pelvis and compute the magnitude of
their temporal second differences, averaged over joints. For each composition boundary, we take
the maximum value within two frames of the boundary as the boundary peak. As a local reference,
we compute the 95th percentile within 12 frames on either side of the boundary, excluding the three
frames closest to the boundary on each side. We reject a sequence when the boundary peak is at
least 0.03, exceeds the local reference by at least 0.025, and is at least 1.5× the local reference. We
also remove sequences containing non-finite motion values.

B.2
DENOISER ARCHITECTURE AND TRAINING SETTINGS
The global branch has four stages of four Transformer layers, with width 1024, feed-forward width
4096, and 16 attention heads. The input token encoder has one attention layer with width 128 and
four heads. The classifier-predicted attention bias uses λr = 0.25. The classifier takes projected
prompt embeddings as input. Body-part tokens use centered relevance probabilities to form Bk,
while the root uses B0 = 0. We stop gradients at the classifier input and through the probabilities
used to form Bk in Section 4.3. The generator learning rate is 1e-4, with weight decay 0.01. Hu-
manML3D examples are capped at 78 latents with a latent rate of 7.5 fps. RoTE uses frequencies
ωj = 10000−(j−1)/Drot for j = 1, . . . , Drot, where Drot is the number of rotary pairs. A common
timestamp scale γ = 4 is used for motion queries and interval centers and radii.
B.3
BACKBONE OBJECTIVES AND DETAILS
Following ActionPlan (Nazarenus et al., 2026), we construct action-plan targets from atomic action
annotations in BABEL (Punnakkal et al., 2021), aligned to the source motion clips and transferred
to the composed timelines. We average the CLIP embeddings of distinct active action labels at each
frame, temporally smooth the features, and compress them with a frozen autoencoder. We apply the
interpolation and velocity target in Eq. (1) to both streams. Each motion latent uses a noise level tm
n ,
while the action-plan stream shares one level tp per training example.
The weighted flow loss is computed over scalar targets in the minibatch and normalized by the
total weight jointly across the motion and action-plan streams. Valid motion targets use importance
weights that compensate for their nonuniform noise-level distribution. Valid action-plan targets
have unit weight. The shared action-plan noise level is sampled uniformly. Motion supervision
excludes the fixed prefix, while available action-plan targets at prefix positions remain supervised.
Padding, positions beyond the supervised horizon, and targets replaced by detached self-conditioned
predictions have zero weight.
The main generation objective is
Lgen = Lflow + 0.2Lpart + 0.02Lcls + Lroot.
(7)
Here, Lpart is the mean squared error of the auxiliary body-latent flow-velocity prediction. Let
Y ∈{0, 1}J×6 denote the annotated body-part labels, with the same instruction and part ordering
as Π. Lcls uses binary cross-entropy with Y . The root loss Lroot applies smooth L1 supervision to
frame-to-frame changes in horizontal position and heading.
B.4
STREAMING INFERENCE
Following ActionPlan (Nazarenus et al., 2026), each sampling window retains up to P generated
motion latents as a fixed prefix and initializes future motion and all action-plan latents with Gaussian
noise. The action-plan stream is first denoised jointly with the first motion token, while later motion
positions remain at higher noise levels. Once the action plan is complete, its latents remain fixed and
motion is denoised progressively. Prompt attention follows Eq. (4) throughout sampling. Completed
motion is committed to the output and extends the fixed prefix. When a new instruction arrives,
committed motion is preserved, while uncommitted predictions are discarded and regenerated using
the new instruction together with earlier instructions that remain active. For open-ended interactive
commands without a specified end time, we use a 5-second default interval, which can be extended
successively for continuous execution. We provide instructions in seconds and then map them to the
window interval that the network is operating on.
C
EVALUATION PROTOCOLS AND METRICS
C.1
BASELINE TEXT CONDITIONING
For baselines that accept only a single caption, we split the inference timeline at instruction start and
end times. Using the notation of Section 3, instruction ei = (ℓi, [si, ui)) contributes its text exactly
when si ≤τ < ui. Within each resulting segment, a single active prompt is passed unchanged,
17


Preprint
and multiple active prompts are joined with while. The caption is recomputed at each boundary,
removing instructions that have ended and adding instructions that have just arrived. For example,
prompts A on [0, 5) and B on [3, 8) produce A on [0, 3), A while B on [3, 5), and B on [5, 8). Future
prompts are withheld until their annotated start times, and caption updates retain the already emitted
motion through the baseline’s native continuation mechanism. This inference procedure is separate
from the window-level caption construction used during retraining.
C.2
EVALUATION METRICS
Sequence alignment.
We use TMR (Petrovich et al., 2023) to measure motion-to-text R-Precision
at ranks 1, 2, and 3, motion-to-text similarity (M2T), and similarity to the corresponding source
motion (M2M), with both similarities rescaled to [0, 1]. On timeline benchmarks, we evaluate text-
motion alignment within each prompt’s annotated interval. Overlapping prompts are scored sepa-
rately over their respective intervals, so shared frames contribute to the evaluation of each prompt.
On HumanML3D, alignment is evaluated over complete motions.
Body-part alignment.
For body-part alignment, we follow FrankenMotion’s released protocol (Li
et al., 2026), scoring each prompt’s full-body motion crop against its original text with the evaluators
for its primary body-part labels. The same evaluators measure M2M between each generated interval
and its corresponding source motion. We report R@3, M2T, and M2M averaged equally over the
six body regions on TimelineMotion and MTT.
Motion quality.
FID measures distributional similarity using whole motion sequences. FID+
compares five crops of up to five seconds per generated timeline with ground truth source motions,
following STMC (Petrovich et al., 2024). We report both on TimelineMotion, FID on HumanML3D,
and FID+ on MTT. Because MTT has no complete reference timelines, we omit its whole-motion
FID and reference-motion temporal-adherence scores. Transition distance (Trans) measures root-
relative joint displacement at instruction boundaries (Athanasiou et al., 2022; Petrovich et al., 2024).
Reconstruction.
FID measures distributional similarity between reconstructed and reference mo-
tions. MPJPE measures the Euclidean distance between corresponding reconstructed and reference
joint positions, averaged over joints and all valid frames and reported in millimeters.
Temporal adherence.
For temporal adherence, we measure whether each instruction is reflected
within its requested interval using frozen whole-body TMR embeddings. For every instruction
ei = (ℓi, [si, ui)), we score its text against 2-second motion windows sampled every 0.2 seconds
across the complete timeline. A window is positive if its center lies in [si, ui) and negative otherwise.
We report non-interpolated average precision (AP) and the area under the receiver operating char-
acteristic curve (ROC-AUC). AP is computed from the ranking induced by TMR similarity scores,
without selecting a fixed similarity threshold. We score overlapping instructions independently and
average each metric equally over instructions with both positive and negative windows. Temporal
adherence is not applicable to HumanML3D because its captions span complete motions.
C.3
TEMPORAL ADHERENCE VALIDATION
To assess sensitivity to temporal misalignment, we keep each motion and its text-motion similarity
scores fixed and shift the requested instruction interval earlier or later by 0.5, 1, or 2 seconds. We
evaluate GT instruction intervals from the TimelineMotion test set. Interval lengths and scoring
windows are unchanged across shifts. Table 5 shows that mean AP and ROC-AUC are highest at the
original intervals for GT. On average, shifting intervals in either direction lowers both metrics, with
larger shifts producing larger decreases. These results support using Temporal AP and ROC-AUC
to evaluate alignment between motion content and requested instruction intervals.

D
TRAINING STRATEGIES AND ADDITIONAL EXPERIMENTS
D.1
SELF-CONDITIONED TRAINING
During streaming inference, previously generated latents become context for subsequent predictions.
Following MotionStreamer (Xiao et al., 2025), we train with detached model predictions as context.
For examples selected for replacement, a first forward pass estimates clean motion and action-plan
latents without gradient propagation. We insert the selected predictions into the second-pass input as
clean latents and set their noise levels to zero. Gradients are computed only through the second pass.
Replaced positions receive zero flow-loss weight in the corresponding stream, while the remaining
valid targets retain supervision.
Table 6 compares the full model with its variant without self-conditioned training. Removing self-
conditioned training lowers retrieval and increases FID on both datasets, and increases boundary
displacement on TimelineMotion. Temporal AP is unchanged at the reported precision, while ROC-
AUC decreases slightly.

D.5
ABLATION OF BLOCK-CAUSAL SELF-ATTENTION
We replace block-causal motion self-attention with a standard causal mask (w/o block-causal self-
attention). Each motion token can attend only to itself and preceding tokens, removing bidirec-
tional interactions within the jointly denoised block. The online instruction-arrival policy and text-
conditioning mechanism are retained. Table 10 shows that this replacement lowers sequence R@3
on TimelineMotion from 0.603 to 0.586 and body-part R@3 from 0.374 to 0.364. Temporal AP
and ROC-AUC decrease from 0.832 to 0.826 and from 0.772 to 0.767, respectively. These results
suggest that within-block bidirectional interactions are beneficial for timeline alignment, temporal
adherence, and distributional motion quality on TimelineMotion.
D.6
ABLATION OF ONLINE MULTI-TRACK ATTENTION
We ablate separate instruction conditioning by merging the captions of currently active instructions
into a single caption (w/o online multi-track attention). The merged caption is updated at instruc-
tion start and end boundaries during training and inference, and future instructions remain unavail-
able before arrival. The model therefore conditions on one combined description in each interval
rather than attending independently to concurrent instructions. Table 11 shows that sequence R@3
on TimelineMotion decreases from 0.603 to 0.552 and whole-motion FID increases from 0.017 to
0.046. The performance on HumanML3D also drops. The comparison supports separate online in-
struction conditioning for semantic alignment, temporal adherence, and whole-motion distributional
quality.

D.8
ABLATION OF ROOT-VELOCITY LOSS
We remove Lroot from Eq. (7), disabling auxiliary smooth L1 supervision of frame-to-frame root
displacement and heading changes. Root features remain supervised by the main flow objective.
To assess smoothness beyond instruction boundaries, Table 13 additionally reports root and body
jitter over complete sequences. Root jitter measures the magnitude of third-order finite differences
of the global root trajectory, while body jitter measures the corresponding root-relative quantity
averaged over the other 21 joints. Removing the root loss increases root jitter from 1.417 to 1.757
and body jitter from 0.614 to 0.680. On TimelineMotion, whole-motion FID increases from 0.017
to 0.021, sequence R@3 decreases from 0.603 to 0.595. HumanML3D FID is similar between
the two variants (0.026 vs. 0.027). These results indicate that root-velocity supervision improves
sequence-wide smoothness by reducing both root and body jitter.

E.2
SPATIAL CONDITIONS
Spatial targets are represented in the root and body feature space with binary availability masks.
Only available controls on future frames are supplied, using five control types:
• Root waypoints: horizontal positions at sparse frames, optionally with heading.
• Root paths: horizontal positions and heading at future frames with supplied root controls.
• Body keyframes: root features and non-root joint positions at sparse frames.
• End-effector keyframes: root features and selected foot or wrist positions and orientations.

E.3
ADAPTATION
Spatial adaptation initializes from the exponential-moving-average weights of the trained generator.
We jointly fine-tune the denoiser and condition projection while keeping the autoencoder frozen,
with learning rates 2 × 10−5 for the denoiser and 10−4 for the condition projection. Following the
spatial-conditioning setup of ARDY (Zhao et al., 2026), the objective is
Lctrl = Lgen + 0.25Ldec + Lgoal + Lkin.
(8)
The reconstruction term uses L1 error on decoded, normalized body features, while the goal term
matches supplied root and body targets. Kinematic consistency compares the explicit and forward-
kinematic positions of the 21 non-root joints using mean Euclidean distance. Training mixes sin-
gle, combined, and absent spatial conditions, gradually increasing the maximum number of sparse
keyframes. Reconstruction and kinematic losses use valid future frames, while the goal loss applies
only to supplied targets.
E.4
EVALUATION
Each motion is randomly assigned to one of six control settings including five individual control
types and one combined setting. On TimelineMotion, control availability is synchronized with text-
prompt arrivals. When a control becomes available, its world-space targets are translated by the
horizontal displacement between the generated and source root positions at that time. The heading
is unchanged. Errors are measured relative to the transformed targets. We report position errors for
root, body, and end-effector targets in centimeters, and F1 for specified foot-contact labels. Control
metrics average applicable examples and evaluate only frames and joints with supplied targets.

LIMITATIONS
TimelineControl has several limitations. First, TimelineMotion is constructed by composing Hu-
manML3D source motions rather than collecting motions from real streaming multi-track interac-
tions. Although we filter incompatible actions and refine composition boundaries, the resulting data
may not capture the full diversity and complexity of naturally co-occurring actions. Second, our
formulation uses explicit temporal intervals to determine when each instruction is active. When an
end time is not provided, the system can use a fixed-duration interval (e.g., 5 seconds) and extend
the instruction in successive intervals for continuous execution. However, it does not currently infer
action duration or termination directly from language or motion state. Finally, our part-aware super-
vision relies on automatically generated body-part annotations and a fixed decomposition into six
anatomical regions; annotation errors or actions involving more complex cross-part interactions may
therefore limit the part-aware guidance. Extending the framework to naturally collected interactive
data, learned action termination, and more flexible part grounding provides promising directions for
future work.
