ABSTRACT
Flow matching models excel at generative modeling, and many downstream ap-
plications require their samples to satisfy prescribed constraints, such as observed
measurements and physical laws. However, existing constrained samplers often
face a trade-off: enforcing constraints can substantially displace samples from the
pretrained data distribution. To address this trade-off, we introduce MintFlow, a
training-free constrained sampling framework that formulates constraint enforce-
ment as a minimal intervention on the pretrained flow trajectory. MintFlow seeks
the minimal perturbation of an intermediate flow state such that its subsequent evo-
lution under the pretrained flow field satisfies the target constraint. By minimally
perturbing the flow state while keeping the pretrained flow field unchanged, Mint-
Flow enforces the constraint while minimizing unnecessary deviation from the
pretrained distribution. An adjoint formulation yields a closed-form expression
for this perturbation, eliminating expensive iterative optimization. Furthermore,
MintFlow adaptively selects the intervention time to balance the required pertur-
bation magnitude with its amplification by the remaining flow. Across a range
of tasks in generative vision and physical system modeling, MintFlow achieves
competitive constraint satisfaction while preserving the pretrained generative dis-
tribution substantially better than state-of-the-art constrained methods.

INTRODUCTION
Flow matching has emerged as a powerful paradigm for generative modeling, efficiently transport-
ing simple reference distributions into complex data distributions through learned continuous-time
dynamics (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2026). Yet many real-world applications demand more
than plausible generation. Generated samples need to satisfy measurement constraints, structural
requirements, physical laws, or other application-specific conditions (Utkarsh et al., 2026; Martin
et al., 2025a; Patel et al., 2025). In these settings, the central objective is to enforce specified con-
straints while preserving the distributional structure already learned by the pretrained model.
Existing methods approach this problem either during training or at inference time. Training-time
methods (Li et al., 2026a; Tauberschmidt et al., 2026) incorporate constraints through specialized
objectives, regularization, or architectural design, but require costly retraining for each new con-
straint, which is often impractical. Inference-time methods avoid this by enforcing the constraint
during sampling, either by modifying the pretrained dynamics via guidance (Chung et al., 2023a;
Feng et al., 2025), flow states through trajectory optimization (Ben-Hamu et al., 2024; Patel et al.,
2025), or generated samples using post-hoc projection (Cheng et al., 2025; Utkarsh et al., 2026).1
However, these approaches face a key challenge: constraint satisfaction alone does not prevent large
deviations to the pretrained generation process, which can push samples away from the pretrained
distribution (Figure 1).
Therefore, we adopt a simple principle: modify only what is necessary, and only as much as nec-
essary. As the desired constrained samples remain within the distribution learned by the pretrained

Motivated by this principle, we propose MintFlow, a training-free framework that formulates con-
strained generation as minimal trajectory intervention (Figure 2). Given a trajectory generated by
the pretrained flow, MintFlow finds the smallest perturbation to an intermediate state such that its
subsequent evolution under the unchanged pretrained flow satisfies the terminal constraint. The
minimum-norm intervention is computed directly from the terminal constraint sensitivity via an
adjoint equation, avoiding explicit construction of the high-dimensional flow Jacobian and costly
iterative optimization through the full generation trajectory. MintFlow further adaptively selects the
intervention time to balance the correction magnitude against the remaining flow propagation time.
Under mild regularity conditions, we characterize the constraint satisfaction error and show that the
Wasserstein distance between the corrected and pretrained distributions is bounded by the interven-
tion magnitude and the remaining flow propagation time. Experiments on image and physics data
with diverse constraints, including image inverse problems, image editing, and physics-informed
generation, demonstrate that MintFlow effectively satisfies constraints while preserving the learned
distribution across a broad range of applications.

As the pretrained velocity field fθ is learned to transport the base distribution µ toward the uncon-

such, we seek a constrained generation procedure that generates samples from the terminal distribu-
tion νT that satisfy the prescribed constraint using only the pretrained flow field fθ and the constraint
operator H without additional model training.
The key challenge is to simultaneously satisfy the constraint H(u) = 0 while keeping the samples
close to the pretrained distribution νT . Existing approaches often enforce the constraint at the cost of
deviating from νT (see Figure 1). To address this, we formulate constrained generation as a minimal-
intervention problem, seeking the smallest perturbation to the intermediate states of the pretrained
flow trajectory necessary to enforce the constraint while maintaining the pretrained distribution.

EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate MintFlow on three constrained generation settings: (i) image inverse problems, (ii)
image editing, and (iii) physical system modeling. The experiments span different flow models, data
domains, and constraint types, ranging from local to global and from linear to nonlinear, demonstrat-
ing the broad applicability of our approach. We compare MintFlow against existing training-free
constrained generation methods, using the same pretrained flow for all methods.2 We assess each
method along three core dimensions, constraint satisfaction, preservation of the pretrained distribu-
tion, and computational efficiency, supplemented by task-specific performance metrics. The code is
publicly available at https://github.com/Yebbi/MintFlow.
4.1
IMAGE INVERSE PROBLEMS
Setup.
Following Patel et al. (2025), we evaluate MintFlow on noise-free image inverse problems,
including inpainting, super resolution, and deblurring, using the AFHQ-Cat and FFHQ datasets. We
use the same pretrained Rectified Flow++ (RF) models (Lee et al., 2024) across all experiments,
and resize all images to 64 × 64. For each task, given a degraded observation uobs, we define the
measurement constraint as H(u) = A(u)−uobs = 0, where A denotes the task-specific degradation
operator. We compare MintFlow with existing image-constraint methods—including D-Flow, DPS,
Freedom, PNPFlow, and FlowChef—while sharing the same frozen RF model and source noises. In
addition to constraint satisfaction, distribution preservation, and efficiency, we report reconstruction
quality using PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS. For further details, please refer to Appendix E.

the pretrained generative prior. Importantly, the improved constraint satisfaction does not come at

Setup.
Following the benchmark protocol of Utkarsh et al. (2026), we evaluate MintFlow on
physical-system generation using Functional Flow Matching (FFM), which extends flow match-
ing to function-valued data (Kerrigan et al., 2023). We consider two spatiotemporal systems: the
periodic heat and a one-dimensional reaction–diffusion (RD1D) equations. Each system uses its
own pretrained FFM checkpoint, and all samplers share the same frozen weights and source noises.
The constraint operator jointly encodes conservation, initial-condition (IC), and boundary-condition
(BC) residuals. Heat uses structural periodicity, while RD1D imposes prescribed Neumann fluxes.
We compare MintFlow against Vanilla FFM, DiffusionPDE, D-Flow, ECI, and PCFM without re-
training or fine-tuning the pretrained model. In addition to the introduced core criteria, we evaluate
physical consistency using the governing-equation residual and the error to a numerical trajectory
initialized from each generated sample’s own IC. Distributional agreement is measured against a
numerical reference set generated under the same FFM-derived physical conditions, so the reported
set-level distances quantify agreement with the corresponding conditioned numerical distribution
rather than divergence from Vanilla FFM itself. Further details are provided in Appendix G.

CONCLUSION
This work introduces MintFlow, a training-free framework for constrained generation in flow
matching models. By formulating constrained generation as a minimal trajectory intervention, Mint-
Flow preserves the learned generative dynamics while minimally perturbing the flow state. Leverag-
ing an adjoint-based sensitivity formulation, it efficiently computes the minimum-norm intermediate
perturbation with theoretical analysis of constraint satisfaction and distributional stability. Extensive
experiments across diverse domains, constraints, and backbone models demonstrate that MintFlow
effectively satisfies constraints while preserving the pretrained distribution. While MintFlow relies
9


on a first-order linearization for high efficiency, it can be seamlessly complemented by iterative re-
finements or higher-order approximations when dealing with highly nonlinear constraints. Looking
forward, extending MintFlow to more challenging nonlinear or non-differentiable constraints, reduc-
ing its computational overhead, and broadening the framework to other dynamics-based generative
models pose promising directions for future work.

RELATED WORK
We consider inference-time constraint enforcement for pretrained flow matching (Lipman et al.,
2023) and rectified flow models (Liu et al., 2023a). Existing approaches can be broadly categorized
according to how they incorporate constraints during sampling: modifying the velocity field, pro-
jecting onto the constraint manifold, or optimizing the source or intermediate states, as illustrated in
Figures 2a, 2b, and 2c, respectively.
Velocity modification.
A line of work modifies the pretrained velocity field to steer the generated
trajectory toward a desired target or constraint. General flow matching guidance derives exact or
approximate corrections for energy-tilted laws (Feng et al., 2025). Image inverse solvers correct
the path toward the data manifold with gradient-based guidance (Chung et al., 2022; 2023b; Song
et al., 2023), adapting the same idea to flow matching via likelihood gradients, endpoint estimates,
measurement interpolants, or posterior-score surrogates (Pokle et al., 2024; Song et al., 2026; Yan
et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025). TOCFlow formulates terminal constraints as optimal control with
geometry-aware feedback based on the downstream-flow metric (Gao et al., 2026). Lagrangian Dual
Flows augment the ODE with dual and slack dynamics that drive equality and inequality residuals
to zero near terminal time (Kurtz & Davydov, 2026). These methods directly alter the sampling dy-
namics and require recomputing constraint-specific velocity corrections for each constraint, whereas
our method leaves the pretrained vector field unchanged.
Projection.
Projection methods alternate flow integration with corrections that project the state
onto a feasible or observation-consistent set (Christopher et al., 2024). ECI extrapolates an interme-
diate state to the endpoint, applies a constraint correction, and interpolates the result back into the
trajectory (Cheng et al., 2025). PCFM combines terminal shooting with a Gauss-Newton projection
and optimal-transport-based reinsertion (Utkarsh et al., 2026). ProFlow replaces the terminal cor-
rection with a proximal problem over physical and observational constraints, while CCFM projects
onto time-dependent chance-feasible sets (Yu et al., 2026; Liang et al., 2025). Image inverse solvers
combine data-consistency corrections with flow-path reprojection (Pourya et al., 2026), and SNAP-
FM accelerates PCFM-style nonlinear projections through sparse optimization (Kolli et al., 2026).
While projection can effectively enforce the constraints, the explicit state corrections may induce
deviations from the distribution learned by the pretrained model. In contrast, our method avoids
explicit projection and instead seeks to reduce constraint violations through small perturbations of
the intermediate states.
Optimization.
Another family formulates constraint enforcement as an optimization problem over
the generated trajectory. These methods treat the source noise, intermediate states, or distributed
controls as decision variables and optimize them to produce samples that satisfy the constraints.

D-Flow optimizes the source noise by differentiating a terminal objective through the complete flow
solver (Ben-Hamu et al., 2024). However, backpropagating through the entire sampling ODE re-

expensive. FlowChef improves efficiency by adapting this formulation to rectified flows, which
eliminates the need to backpropagate through the ODE integration (Patel et al., 2025). Rather than

receding-horizon trajectory-optimization problems with hard constraints on an estimated terminal
state (Li et al., 2026b), whereas OC-Flow optimizes distributed controls using adjoint costates and a
running deviation penalty (Wang et al., 2024). FlowGrad (Liu et al., 2023b) likewise optimizes dis-

propagate terminal gradients to intermediate controls and optimize corrupted trajectories (Liu et al.,
2023b; Zhang et al., 2024). These approaches leave the pretrained velocity field unchanged, allow-
ing them to accommodate a broad range of constraints without altering the underlying sampling
dynamics. However, solving the resulting optimization problems can be computationally demand-
ing, and optimizing intermediate states may introduce deviations from the distribution learned by
the pretrained model. In contrast, our method uses minimal perturbations of intermediate states and
replaces iterative optimization with a single adjoint system, reducing deviations from the learned
distribution while enabling more efficient constraint enforcement.

This appendix provides additional experiments on image generation that are not included in the
main manuscript, including benchmark definitions, model and sampling configurations, evaluation
protocols, and additional diagnostics. We evaluate a standard flow matching model on image gen-

D.3
EVALUATION PROTOCOL
To evaluate the quality of constraint-generated samples, we consider two aspects: constraint satis-
faction and preservation of the data distribution.
Constraint Satisfaction.
We measure the constraint satisfaction using the average constraint value
over generated samples. For each method, including the ground-truth conditional samples, we gen-
erate 2000 samples and report the average constraint value.
Conditional Distribution Preservation.
To evaluate whether the generated samples preserve the
distribution learned by the flow matching model under the imposed constraint, we measure the
discrepancy between constraint-generated samples and reference samples. The goal of constraint
generation is to obtain samples that satisfy the imposed constraint while remaining within the distri-
bution learned by the pretrained flow matching model. Therefore, the reference distribution used for
evaluation is not the ground-truth data distribution, but a conditional distribution constructed from
samples generated by the pretrained flow matching model.
Specifically, we first generate samples from the pretrained flow matching model and collect 2,000
samples that satisfy the given constraint. These constraint-satisfying samples are used as reference
samples for evaluation. We then measure the discrepancy between the constraint-generated samples
and the reference samples to quantify how well the generated samples preserve the flow-matching-
induced distribution.
For image data, we evaluate the conditional distribution matching using FID, KID, MMD, and
Wasserstein-2 (W2) distance. For MMD, we use a Gaussian RBF kernel:

RESULTS
D.4.1
MNIST
Intensity Constraint.
We evaluate MintFlow on MNIST under an average-intensity constraint
with a target value of −0.6, generating 2,000 samples for each method. All methods generate
images using the same pretrained flow-matching (FM) model. To construct a reference distribution
for evaluating distributional metrics, we generate samples from the pretrained FM model and retain
those that satisfy the target constraint, obtaining 2,000 constraint-satisfying samples. We use these
samples as the ground-truth samples from the conditional distribution induced by the pretrained flow
model. Table 6 summarizes the quantitative results, while Figure 8 presents qualitative comparisons
across methods.
Among existing constrained generation methods, ECI and PCFM first generate samples and then
apply a post-hoc projection to enforce the constraint. Consequently, these methods achieve nearly
exact constraint satisfaction, as indicated by their extremely small constraint errors. However, their
distribution-matching performance deteriorates substantially, suggesting that strict post-hoc con-
straint enforcement can cause the generated samples to deviate from the distribution learned by the
flow model. This behavior is also evident in the qualitative results. In particular, their final projec-
tion adjusts the generated samples to satisfy the target average intensity without explicitly preserving
the visual structure of the original samples. Since the projection only enforces the global intensity
constraint, it can significantly alter the appearance of the image.
D-Flow achieves the strongest distributional performance among the baselines. However, it incurs
substantial computational overhead because its optimization requires retaining the computational
graph throughout the sampling ODE from the latent noise to the generated sample in order to com-
pute the required gradients. This makes D-Flow considerably more memory-intensive and time-
consuming than methods that do not require backpropagation through the entire sampling trajectory.
In contrast, MintFlow provides a better balance between constraint satisfaction and distributional
fidelity. Although its constraint error is slightly higher than those of PCFM and ECI, it achieves
substantially better FID, KID, MMD, and W2 scores, indicating closer agreement with the condi-
tional distribution induced by the pretrained flow model. MintFlow is also faster than the iterative
correction methods, D-Flow and PCFM, despite requiring Jacobian-vector products in its adjoint
ODE formulation.

nonlinear constraint. Projection-based correction methods struggle to effectively satisfy the AE-

struggles to handle such nonlinear learned constraints effectively. D-Flow incurs additional com-

remains faster than D-Flow and PCFM. These results demonstrate that our approach can effectively
incorporate learned nonlinear constraints without substantially compromising either distributional
fidelity or computational efficiency.
Overall, this experiment demonstrates two important aspects of our framework: (1) its versatility in
incorporating learned reliability constraints to improve generated sample quality, and (2) its ability
to extend constrained generation beyond manually designed operators. We acknowledge that the
effectiveness of such learned constraints naturally depends on the quality and generalization capa-
bility of the auxiliary model used to define them. Therefore, the purpose of this experiment is not
to introduce the AE reconstruction constraint as a universally optimal fidelity metric, but rather to
demonstrate the potential of our framework to flexibly integrate learned nonlinear constraints during
inference and improve pretrained flow matching models without additional training.

Selected Intervention Times.
To provide empirical evidence on the intervention times selected by
our adaptive criterion in Section 3.3, we visualize the distribution of s∗obtained from (8). Specif-
ically, for each generated sample, we evaluate the objective J(s) over the predefined candidate
intervention times and select the minimizing time s∗. Figure 10 shows the resulting histograms for
two constraints on MNIST: the intensity constraint and the fidelity constraint.
The selected intervention times exhibit distinct patterns across the two constraints. For the linear
intensity constraint, the selected s∗values are concentrated more heavily toward the earlier part
of the trajectory. In contrast, the nonlinear fidelity constraint, defined using a trained autoencoder,
produces a more dispersed distribution of intervention times, with selections spread across a wider
range of the trajectory.
This difference can be understood from the structure of the two constraint functions. The intensity
constraint is linear in the terminal state, resulting in a relatively simple and consistent constraint
geometry. Consequently, the sensitivity of the constraint to an intermediate perturbation tends to
vary more systematically along the trajectory, and the objective in (8) frequently favors earlier inter-
vention times. The fidelity constraint, on the other hand, is induced by a nonlinear trained AE and
therefore has a more complex local geometry. The corresponding constraint sensitivity can vary sub-
stantially across both samples. As a result, there is no single region of the trajectory that consistently
minimizes the trade-off encoded by J(s), leading to a broader distribution of selected intervention
times.
Overall, these results suggest that the proposed time-selection criterion is not merely selecting a fixed
or universally preferred intervention point. Instead, s∗adapts to the structure of the constraint and
to the individual generated trajectory. The contrast between the two constraints provides empirical
evidence that the adaptive intervention mechanism responds to the geometry and sensitivity of the
constraint rather than imposing a predetermined intervention schedule.

APPLICATION 2: IMAGE INVERSE PROBLEMS
This appendix provides the benchmark definitions, model and sampling configurations, evaluation
protocol, and additional diagnostics supporting the image inverse problem results in Section 4.1. In
particular, we separately quantify three aspects of reconstruction quality:
• Constraint satisfaction: how well the reconstructed image satisfies the measurement con-
straint associated with each inverse problem;
• Pairwise reconstruction fidelity: how closely the reconstruction matches the correspond-
ing reference image;
• Distributional fidelity: how well the distribution of reconstructed images is preserved
relative to the reference image distribution.
E.1
DATASETS AND CONSTRAINTS
We evaluate the image inverse problems on two standard image datasets, AFHQ-Cat (Choi et al.,
2020) and FFHQ (Karras et al., 2019). All images are resized to 64×64 before applying the inverse
operators.
For each reference image uref, we construct a measurement from the corresponding forward operator
and solve for a reconstruction u that satisfies the resulting measurement constraint.

For inpainting, we consider a centered 20 × 20 box mask M. The measurement
consists of the pixels outside the masked region. The constraint is defined as

E.2
FLOW-MATCHING MODEL AND BASELINES
Following (Patel et al., 2025), we conduct all experiments using the same pretrained Rectified
Flow++ models (Lee et al., 2024). RFM models are trained to make the flow matching trajectories
as close to straight lines as possible, resulting in simpler and more direct generative flows. Owing
to this property, pretrained rectified flow models have been widely adopted in recent approaches
for solving inverse problems with flow-based generative models. For all methods, we use 10-step
Rectified Flow sampling to ensure a consistent generation budget across methods.
We compare our method against several existing approaches for solving inverse problems with flow-
based generative models. We first consider FlowChef (Patel et al., 2025) and D-Flow (Ben-Hamu
35


et al., 2024), which enforce constraints by optimizing either an intermediate state along the genera-
tive trajectory or the initial Gaussian noise, respectively. We additionally compare against PnP-Flow
(Martin et al., 2025b), a plug-and-play approach that incorporates the measurement operator into the
flow-based sampling process. Additionally, we include two diffusion-based baselines, DPS (Chung
et al., 2023a) and FreeDoM (Yu et al., 2023), using the Rectified Flow-compatible implementations
developed in (Patel et al., 2025). For all baselines, we use the implementations provided in the offi-
cial FlowChef repository4 without modification. We also use the hyperparameters specified in (Patel
et al., 2025) for each baseline. Our method is implemented for RFM following the formulation de-
scribed in Appendix A.4. Unlike our previous formulation developed for general Flow Matching
models, our RFM-based implementation exploits the linearity of the RFM flow path. This property
allows us to obtain the optimal correction without explicitly solving the adjoint equation backward
in time, as described in (15).
E.3
EVALUATION PROTOCOL
For AFHQ-Cat, we use all images in the official test split. For FFHQ, we randomly select 10,000
images from the dataset using a fixed random seed and use the same set of reference images for
all inverse-problem evaluations. For each dataset and each inverse-problem task, we evaluate the
reconstructions from four complementary perspectives.
Constraint Satisfaction.
For each inverse-problem task, we evaluate how well the reconstructed
images satisfy the corresponding measurement constraint. Specifically, given a reconstruction ˆu, we
compute the mean squared error of the constraint residual H(ˆu) over the entire evaluation set. A
lower value indicates better satisfaction of the imposed constraint.
Pairwise Reconstruction Fidelity.
We measure the reconstruction fidelity by comparing each re-
stored image ˆu with its corresponding reference image uref, i.e., the image before applying the
degradation operator. We report PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS to evaluate pixel-level, structural, and
perceptual similarity, respectively. Higher PSNR and SSIM and lower LPIPS indicate better recon-
struction fidelity.
Distributional Fidelity.
We further evaluate whether the restored images preserve the overall im-
age distribution. To this end, we compute the Fr´echet Inception Distance (FID) between the set of
restored images and the ground-truth images of AFHQ-Cat and FFHQ. A lower FID indicates that
the distribution of the restored images is closer to that of the GT distribution.
Efficiency.
Runtime is measured as synchronized wall-clock time per generated sample, and mem-
ory is reported as peak allocated GPU memory.

APPLICATION 3: IMAGE EDITING
This appendix provides the benchmark definitions, model and sampling configurations, evaluation
protocol, and additional diagnostics supporting the image editing results in Section 4.2. In particular,
we quantify not only how well the edited images satisfy the given constraints and the computational
efficiency of each method, but also how well they fulfill the task-specific text guidance.
• Constraint satisfaction: how well the edited image satisfies the constraint, i.e., preserving
the regions outside the provided mask;
• Prompt adherence: how well the edited image follows the given text prompt, measured
using CLIP score;
• Image similarity: how visually consistent the edited image remains with the reference
image, measured using CLIP score;
• Efficiency: the computational efficiency of each method in terms of inference speed and
memory usage.
F.1
DATASETS AND CONSTRAINTS
We perform large-scale image editing experiments on PIE-Bench (Ju et al., 2024). Specifically, we
use the images from the 0 random 140 subset available in the official PIE-Bench data repository,
along with the provided ground-truth masks. We use the same masks to control all methods for a
fair comparison. The editing task is subject to an inpainting-style constraint: regions outside the
provided mask should remain identical to the corresponding regions in the reference image. Unlike
the aforementioned inverse problem, however, the edited region is additionally required to follow the
given text guidance, making it challenging to satisfy both the preservation constraint and the desired
semantic edit. Thus, the key challenge of this task is to simultaneously preserve the unmasked
regions faithfully while performing the intended text-guided edit within the masked region.
F.2
FLOW-MATCHING MODEL AND BASELINES
Following (Patel et al., 2025), we use InstaFlow (Liu et al., 2023c), a Stable Diffusion v1.5 variant,
as the flow-matching model. We evaluate the same set of baselines used for the aforementioned
inverse problems in Appendix E on InstaFlow. As observed in the previous experiments, D-Flow
requires differentiating through the entire forward flow ODE integration, resulting in substantially
higher memory consumption. For the image editing task on InstaFlow, this computational overhead
exceeds the available memory in our experimental setup. We therefore exclude D-Flow from this
experiment.
F.3
EVALUATION PROTOCOL
We evaluate the editing results using the following metrics.
Constraint Satisfaction.
We measure how faithfully the edited image preserves the reference im-
age outside the provided mask. Specifically, we compute the mean squared error (MSE) between
the edited and reference images over the unmasked regions.
Prompt Adherence.
We measure how well the edited image follows the given text prompt us-
ing CLIP-based similarity (Radford et al., 2021). Specifically, we use the OpenAI-pretrained CLIP
ViT-B/32 model implemented in OpenCLIP 5. We report CLIP-T, computed as the cosine similar-
ity between the CLIP image embedding of the edited image and the CLIP text embedding of the
corresponding prompt. Higher scores indicate better text-image alignment.
Image Similarity.
We measure the visual similarity between the edited image and the reference
image using CLIP-I. Specifically, CLIP-I is computed as the cosine similarity between their CLIP
image embeddings using the same OpenAI-pretrained CLIP ViT-B/32 model. Higher scores indicate
greater visual similarity to the reference image.

Efficiency.
We measure the computational cost of constrained sampling in terms of inference time
and peak GPU memory allocation. Inference time measures the wall-clock time required to generate
an edited image, while peak memory allocation measures the maximum GPU memory allocated

Each PDE has a separately pretrained checkpoint with the same architecture: a four-layer Fourier
Neural Operator with 32 Fourier modes per dimension, 64 hidden channels, 256-channel lifting and
readout layers, and a 32-dimensional sinusoidal embedding of generative-flow time. The two grid

Each benchmark contains N = 1000 samples per method. Heat uses source-noise seed 20260811,
and RD1D uses seed 20260820. Reported means and standard deviations therefore summarize
sample-to-sample variation in one fixed-seed run; they are not uncertainty estimates across train-
ing runs or sampling seeds.
Target bank and numerical reference.
For each equation, Vanilla is first evaluated on the shared
source-noise bank. Its initial slices define the 1,000 IC targets. For Heat, a diffusivity in [1, 5] is fitted
once from each complete Vanilla trajectory; for RD1D, constant Neumann fluxes are fitted once and
clipped to the training ranges. These latent quantities are held fixed across methods at a given sample
index. The numerical reference pool then solves each of these 1,000 physical problems: Heat uses
the periodic Fourier semigroup and RD1D uses the data generator’s exact-reaction, explicit-midpoint
finite-volume scheme. Thus the reference is neither the unconditional training distribution nor an
unconstrained FFM pool. It is a solver distribution conditioned on the same finite bank of FFM-
derived ICs, BCs, and latent physical parameters used by the constrained samplers. Set-level metrics
ignore index pairing after constructing the two sample sets.

ABLATION STUDIES
H.1
ABLATION ON INTEGRATION RESOLUTION
We ablate MintFlow on the Heat benchmark using the same N = 1000 source-noise bank, target
bank, numerical reference pool, and frozen POD transform as in the main comparison. We vary only
sampling integration resolution, holding all other sampler settings fixed. Consequently, differences
across configurations cannot be attributed to resampling or refitting the representation.
As shown in Figure 6, Increasing the number of Euler steps of flow matching model from 50 to
100, 200, and 400 monotonically improves FID, the constraint residual, and same-IC simulation
error. Importantly, the discretization of Euler steps affects not only the forward sampling integration
error but also the numerical error in solving the backward adjoint equation. A finer discretiza-
tion therefore yields both a sampling trajectory and a adjoint that more closely approximate their
continuous-time counterparts. This leads to a more faithful preservation of the pretrained trajec-
tory and, consequently, improved distributional fidelity, while also providing a more accurate local
correction direction for enforcing the terminal constraint. Nevertheless, the framework remains rel-
atively robust across different resolutions, with more noticeable degradation only when fewer than
100 sampling steps are used.
The finite-difference PDE residual improves through 200 steps but rises slightly at 400 steps. The
headline Heat comparison nevertheless uses 100 steps to match the integration resolution across all
fixed-step methods; the higher-resolution settings quantify numerical sensitivity rather than define
the reported operating point. The headline absolute scores remain those in Tables 3 and 10; the
ablation is used to assess relative sensitivity to individual design choices.
H.2
ABLATION ON CANDIDATE TIME SELECTION AND OPTIMAL-TIME REGULARIZATION.
We further ablate the regularization parameter λ in the objective function J in (8) for adaptive
intervention time selection. We use the same data and experimental setup as in the preceding section,
keeping the source-noise bank, target bank, numerical reference pool, and frozen POD transform
fixed. We vary only λ, while holding all other sampler settings fixed. Consequently, differences
across configurations cannot be attributed to resampling or refitting the representation.
Reults in Figure 6 shows that varying λ reveals a trade-off between constraint satisfaction and dis-
tribution preservation. Since λ does not directly regularize the intervention magnitude but instead
determines the preferred intervention time s, a larger λ favors later interventions. When the in-
tervention occurs very close to the terminal time, the correction increasingly resembles a direct
perturbation of the terminal sample toward the constraint manifold, leaving less opportunity for the
pretrained flow to absorb the intervention. Although such a correction can more strongly enforce
the constraint, a large perturbation may move the sample farther from the original terminal distribu-
tion νT . In practice, the performance remains relatively stable for a moderate range of λ, indicating
that the proposed objective provides a robust balance between constraint satisfaction and distribu-
tional preservation. The weak sensitivity at small λ indicates that the candidate grid, rather than the
regularization term, governs the selected correction times in this regime.
H.3
ABLATION ON INTERVENTION STEPS
We conduct an ablation study on the number of intervention steps to investigate how our method
responds to repeated trajectory interventions. In image editing, the generated image must simulta-
neously follow the target prompt and satisfy the constraint, making a single intervention generally
insufficient to achieve both objectives. Therefore, in all preceding experiments, we consistently use
N = 20 intervention steps, following the setting of baseline methods (Patel et al., 2025). In this
section, we vary the number of intervention steps N and examine its effect on the editing results.
As shown in Fig. 24, using too few intervention steps often produces images that are aligned with the
target prompt but deviate substantially from the input image, indicating that a single or small number
of interventions is insufficient to generate an image that satisfies the target prompt while preserving
the input constraint. As N increases, the editing results progressively become more consistent with
the input image while continuing to satisfy the target prompt. After a certain number of intervention
