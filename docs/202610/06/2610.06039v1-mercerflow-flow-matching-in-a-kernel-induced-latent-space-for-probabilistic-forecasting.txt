# ABSTRACT 

Recent work has shown that probabilistic flow matching for time series forecasting benefits from a data-matched prior. The resulting prior introduces local correla-tions, which a sequential architecture usually absorbs: a recurrent neural network (RNN), a structured state-space model (S4), or a Transformer. However, such a backbone costs GPU memory and time per epoch. A cheaper alternative is MLP-based latent-space flow matching: embed the time series via an invertible map to a single latent vector and learn the flow there, so a tabular MLP can treat the series as a set of features. The relationship between the prior and the choice of linear latent map is understudied in conditional flow matching (CFM) forecasting, yet we found it strongly affects performance. Fixed transforms such as Fourier or discrete cosine (DCT) are only well-conditioned for Ornstein–Uhlenbeck priors, while a principal-component (PCA) map fit to the data is a strong but training-set-dependent reference sensitive to train–test shift. Instead, we propose to use the Mercer eigenbasis of the prior kernel: it diagonalises the centred covariance exactly, decouples from training data, and adapts to non-stationary and periodic priors. On five GluonTS benchmarks (ETTh1, ETTh2, Weather, Electricity, Traf-fic) under a shared protocol with TSFlow, the resulting MLP matches or beats it on CRPS at about 4.7× less training memory and 3.5×–4.4× less time per epoch. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

The usual approach to probabilistic forecasting is to devise an algorithm which samples from a predictive distribution over future windows given the observed past. A sampler that is expressive, while staying cheap to train and cheap to run, is the practical target. Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2024) is a prominent modern way to build such samplers: one learns a velocity field along a probability path from a tractable prior to the data, and training is ordinary regression onto a known interpolant. Since each forecast requires numerically integrating this velocity during inference, the cost at test time is dominated by the backbone that evaluates the field; cheapness at training time matters too, since the backbone also dictates GPU memory and time per epoch. If the prior already matches the data, the flow starts closer to the target and needs less capacity to finish the trip. TSFlow (Kollovieh et al., 2025) commits to a Gaussian-process (GP) prior on the forecast window for that reason. However, under that prior, neighbouring time coordinates stay correlated, so a global multilayer perceptron (MLP) spends capacity on local smoothness the GP already encodes. TSFlow absorbs those correlations with a bidirectional state-space backbone (Gu et al., 2022), which is accurate on long smooth series but expensive in memory and time per epoch. Orthonormal projections are a classical way to decorrelate coordinates in a latent space (Mercer, 1909; Karhunen, 1947; Gabor, 1946). Several recent generative models run denoising or score matching in orthonormal latents: DCTdiff (Ning et al., 2025) trains image diffusion in discrete cosine coordinates, Crabb´ e et al. To address this, we conduct a controlled comparison of five linear codecs for projection on a shared architecture and matched prior: Fourier (discrete Fourier transform), discrete cosine (DCT), principal components (PCA), the identity (Raw), and Mercer-based eigenbasis. Mercer’s theorem supplies the orthonormal eigenbasis of the kernel one has already committed to (Mercer, 1909; Karhunen, 1947), so the coefficients are uncorrelated under the corresponding GP. Thus, that decorrelation is exact for the centred prior and approximate for the conditional law and for the interpolant on which the network trains. Across codecs, Mercer is the most versatile data-free choice: it works on data which requires a non-OU prior (Fourier and DCT best diagonalise OU-like priors, which are not always optimal for the data) and where PCA suffers from the train–test shift since it fits only the training path (§3.2). However, that equivalence is optimiser-specific: it holds for Euclidean SGD (Proposition 1), but fails under Adam because of the first-layer input geometry. # 2 METHOD 

We study probabilistic time series forecasting, aiming to model the conditional distribution of a future window given the observed past. Following the channel-independent setting of TS-Flow (Kollovieh et al., 2025), each channel is treated as a separate univariate series. Let Lp and 

Lf denote the context (past) and forecast horizon (future) lengths, respectively, yielding a total se-quence length L = Lp +Lf . We define the regular discrete time grid S = {0, . . . , L −1}, partitioned into the observed past Sp = {0, . . . , L p − 1} and the future forecast horizon Sf = {Lp, . . . , L − 1}.The observed past series is denoted by yp ∈ RLp , the true future window by yf ∈ RLf , and their joint concatenation by y = [ yp, yf ] ∈ RL.

MercerFlow overview. Figure 1 summarises three stages of M ERCER FLOW . First, we condition a Gaussian-process prior on the observed past and sample an initial future window (Subsection 2.1). Second, we concatenate this sample with the past and encode the joint window in the orthonormal eigenbasis of the same kernel matrix (Subsection 2.2). Third, during training, a lightweight MLP learns the velocity along linear interpolants between the encoded source and target windows (Sub-section 2.3). Finally, at inference time, we integrate the learned velocity from a newly sampled source, apply the inverse transform, and extract the forecast horizon (Subsection 2.3). Repeating this procedure produces samples from the predictive distribution. 2.1 CONDITIONAL GP P RIOR

The equivalence established in Proposition 1 is strictly Euclidean, as standard stochastic gradient descent applies isotropic updates across all coordinate axes. Modern probabilistic forecasting mod-els are instead routinely optimized with adaptive algorithms such as AdamW (Kingma & Ba, 2015). These optimizers maintain an exponential moving average of squared coordinate-wise gradients to rescale parameter updates individually, thereby dampening oscillations along steep directions and accelerating progress along plateaus. Because this coordinate-wise scaling is defined relative to the canonical parameter axes, it is not rotation-equivariant. Rotating the input-space basis fundamentally alters Adam’s trajectory, whereas SGD’s trajectory undergoes a simple linear conjugation (Zhang et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025). Consequently, when training the velocity field vθ of Section 2.3 with AdamW, optimisation dynamics are governed not by the raw input Gram matrix, but by its geometry following diagonal preconditioning. # 3 EXPERIMENTS 

Benchmark Setup and Protocol. Experiments cover five standard time series benchmarks: ETTh1, ETTh2, Weather, Electricity, and Traffic. The context and forecast lengths are fixed at Lp = Lf =192 , giving a joint window of length L = 384 , with channels treated independently. Baselines include four alternative codecs (Raw, Fourier, DCT, and PCA), the flow-based forecaster TSFlow, and the deterministic forecasters DLinear, PatchTST, and DMamba. All five codec variants use the same MLP architecture, joint-window conditioning scheme, and GP prior. Prior hyperpa-rameters are selected by validation likelihood (Appendix C.4). Architecture and training details appear in Appendix C. For each probabilistic model, evaluation uses S = 100 forecast trajectories {ˆy(s)f }Ss=1 per test win-dow. Normalised continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) measures distributional forecast ac-curacy (Alexandrov et al., 2020; Kollovieh et al., 2025), while mean absolute error (MAE) measures point-forecast accuracy. For probabilistic models, the point forecast is the empirical median of the generated samples. Scores are aggregated over rolling test windows, with metric definitions and aggregation details provided in Appendix C.1. 3.1 MAIN RESULTS : F ORECASTING QUALITY AND EFFICIENCY

We therefore use Traffic to compare the default OU prior against a better periodic prior, exposing the differences between fixed harmonic transforms, empirical bases, and MercerFlow’s analytical basis. Under the stationary OU prior, DCT is near-optimal because its fixed harmonic basis approximates the Karhunen–Lo` eve expansion of Markovian processes. # 4 CONCLUSION 

Gaussian process priors provide structured probability paths for conditional flow matching, but raw temporal coordinates leave dense correlations that create severe conditioning bottlenecks under AdamW. First-layer preconditioning separates these representations on challenging benchmarks: the unrotated baseline consistently trails behind and collapses under learning rate stress, while rotation-equivariant SGD leaves all orthonormal codecs tied. Fixed harmonic transforms fail when the co-variance departs from stationary Toeplitz structures, and empirical PCA degrades under distribution shifts between train and test windows. By contrast, the Mercer eigenbasis diagonalizes the chosen prior covariance analytically without data fitting, maintaining stable preconditioning across both stationary and periodic processes. Aligning the latent coordinate frame with the prior kernel allows a lightweight feed-forward MLP to match or surpass sequential state-space models on probabilistic calibration, while reducing peak GPU memory by 4.7× and per-epoch training time by 3.5×–4.4×.LIMITATIONS 

Our theoretical analysis idealises Adam’s coordinate rescaling as exact Jacobi preconditioning and assumes rotation-invariant SGD initialisation. Empirically, performance depends on selecting an appropriate prior kernel and length scale via validation likelihood; also, our evaluations are restricted to univariate channels on a single fixed-width MLP backbone. Finally, the condition number metric 

κ(DAD) captures the relative performance among Mercer, DCT, Fourier, and PCA bases primarily under structured departures from stationary Toeplitz priors (such as elevated learning rates, periodic kernels, or train–test distribution shifts). # A RELATED WORK 

Probabilistic generative forecasting. Denoising diffusion is a standard sampler for probabilistic time series: TimeGrad (Rasul et al., 2021) forecasts autoregressively with a diffusion decoder, and CSDI (Tashiro et al., 2021) performs conditional score matching for imputation and forecasting in observation coordinates. Conditional flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2024) replaces the reverse SDE with regression onto a known interpolant velocity. TSFlow (Kollovieh et al., 2025) applies that recipe to forecasting with a Gaussian-process prior on the forecast window, Euclidean CFM in observation coordinates, and a bidirectional S4 backbone (Gu et al., 2022). We keep that prior and interpolant, and move the flow to a linear latent of the same window so that an MLP can replace the sequential backbone. 

Latent-space generative models. Several recent generative models run the denoising process in an orthonormal latent rather than in observation coordinates. DCTdiff (Ning et al., 2025) trains im-age diffusion in block DCT coordinates and reports gains in sample quality and training cost over pixel-space diffusion. Crabb´ e et al. (Crabb´ e et al., 2024) dualise time-series diffusion to the Fourier domain under mirrored Brownian motion, so that frequency-localised series are scored where they concentrate. The DCT is near Karhunen–Lo` eve for smooth stationary Grams on a finite interval, and the DFT diagonalises every circulant covariance; Whittle and Szeg˝ o treat it as an approximate KL basis for stationary Toeplitz Grams (Gabor, 1946; Baraniuk & Jones, 1995; Whittle, 1951; Kac et al., 1953; Grenander & Szeg˝ o, 1958; Gray, 2006; Chan, 1989). Phillips et al. (Phillips et al., 2022) go further toward a kernel eigenbasis: they expand functional data in truncated Mercer coefficients and train a score model on the retained modes, with the kernel taken as the empirical covariance or a stationary alternative such as RBF or Mat´ ern. Mercer’s theorem supplies that eigenbasis for a continuous positive-definite kernel (Mercer, 1909), and the Karhunen–Lo` eve theorem yields uncor-related coefficients for a second-order process, independent under a GP (Karhunen, 1947; Lo` eve, 1978; Rasmussen & Williams, 2006). Although data-matched GP priors are natural for structured time series, existing approaches do not examine how such priors interact with latent projections un-der flow matching. Our contribution is to study this interaction: fixing a forecasting GP prior, we compare five full-order codecs on one MLP and one CFM loss. 

Rotation equivariance of optimisers. Euclidean SGD is equivariant to orthogonal reparameter-isations of the weights: conjugating the network by a rotation conjugates the iterates and leaves the loss trajectory unchanged. Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015) is not: its per-coordinate second-moment rescaling depends on the parameter basis, so rotating the coordinates changes the opti-misation path (Zhang et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025). That gap has motivated rotation-aware and approximately rotation-equivariant optimisers, including Kronecker-factored preconditioners such as Shampoo (Gupta et al., 2018), Shampoo–Adam hybrids such as SOAP (Vyas et al., 2025), and orthogonalised updates such as Muon (Jordan et al., 2024). Proposition 1 extends to any optimiser whose update commutes with W 7 → W Q , which includes Muon and Shampoo without grafting; we test SGD only. 12 Preprint. Under review.

All time-series channels are treated as independent univariate sequences. The observation-space prior is the Gaussian Process defined in Section 2.1, governed by the stationary Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) covariance kernel with length scale ℓ selected via validation-likelihood maximisation (Table 8) and shared across all five MLP codecs. The context past and forecast horizon lengths are fixed at 

Lp = Lf = 192 , yielding a total sequence window L = Lp + Lf = 384 . Source prior samples and target data trajectories are paired independently during training; minibatch optimal transport is not required due to the structured probability paths induced by the GP prior. 

Joint Window Conditioning. The complete joint sequence of length L = 384 is mapped into the latent coefficient space at once via the square orthonormal matrix Φ ∈ RL×L. The lightweight MLP described in Section C.3 maps the joint coefficient vector c ∈ RL to a hidden dimension of width 

64 (a 2-layer MLP with 1 hidden layer, ≈ 55 k parameters), applies adaptive flow-time conditioning, and projects back to predict the velocity field. Parameter counts across Mercer, Fourier, Raw, DCT, and PCA are strictly matched.

This mismatch increases the prior κ(DAD ) from 1.00 to 1.71 and the interpolant κ(DAD ) from 98 .5 to 192 .0,resulting in a mild CRPS degradation ( 0.6063 → 0.6125 ± 0.0057 ). Crucially, this performance gap (0.0062 ) remains within seed standard deviation, confirming that M ERCER FLOW maintains stable optimization even under moderately misspecified prior parameters. 

SGD Equivariance Control. Training with Nesterov SGD (momentum 0.9, learning rate 10 −3,matched step budget) completely eliminates all performance discrepancies: Mercer, Fourier, and Raw achieve identical test CRPS ( 0.6033 ± 0.0034 ), with a relative gap from Raw of exactly 0.00% .This empirical result provides definitive proof of Proposition 1, demonstrating that coordinate rep-resentations have zero effect under rotation-equivariant optimization. F.3 SENSITIVITY TO PRIOR DIAGONAL JITTER 

The regularising diagonal jitter εI L added to the Gaussian process covariance matrix K serves two distinct purposes: it ensures numerical stability during matrix inversion in the conditional prior (Equation 3) and sets the diagonal regularisation floor for the Mercer eigenspace. A minimal jitter level ( ε ≤ 10 −3) preserves exact alignment with the continuous analytical kernel, whereas excessive jitter forces the prior covariance toward isotropic white noise ( K ≈ IL). Stability Under Standard Regularisation ( ε ≤ 10 −2). For standard jitter scales ( ε ∈

[10 −4, 10 −2]), M ERCER FLOW maintains tightly bounded, well-conditioned first-layer geometry (κ(DAD ) ≤ 30 .6 on ETTh1 and ≤ 95 .3 on ETTh2 ), yielding stable predictive performance (CRPS 

0.186 –0.188 on ETTh1 ). Conversely, diagonal jitter fails to resolve the conditioning pathology of the unrotated R AW baseline, whose condition number remains several orders of magnitude higher (κ(DAD ) ∼ 10 5–10 6) across all evaluated jitter scales. On the simple ETTh2 benchmark, where the GP prior mean accounts for the dominant dynamics, CRPS remains virtually invariant to the choice of codec ( 0.131 –0.135 ). 

Breakdown Under Extreme Jitter ( ε ≥ 0.1). At extreme jitter levels ( ε ≥ 0.1), the kernel’s continuous temporal correlation is drowned out by artificial diagonal dampening ( K → IL). This distorts the kernel’s eigenspace, driving Mercer’s condition number up to 3.64 × 10 3 on ETTh2 .More critically, because the prior degenerates toward uninformative white noise, the flow loses its data-matched starting state, causing pronounced CRPS degradation on the harder ETTh1 benchmark (0.188 → 0.255 for Mercer and 0.192 → 0.256 for R AW ).
