Abstract 

One-step generative models construct a static generator through iterative training-time transport. Existing transport objectives primarily assess distributional motion, although a neural generator needs to realize the requested sample displacements jointly through shared parameter updates. The training-time construction raises the question: once training becomes the iterative process that constructs the fi-nal one-step map, what to optimize: the next distributional move, or the route by which the finite generator learns the final map? To address the question, we introduce Training 

Dynamics Action ( TDAction ), which selects transport tar-gets according to local shared-parameter realization cost while retaining a prescribed level of distributional progress. We formulate the cost as a soft-terminal control problem and derive a closed-form Batch Tangent Action-to-Go value that accounts for parameter effort and terminal mismatch. The criterion captures cross-sample interactions omitted by independent pairwise costs; under isotropic mobility, the criterion agrees with quadratic Euclidean assignment for deterministic balanced couplings. Randomized tangent probes provide a low-rank implementation that constructs shared detached targets without adding an inference-time trajectory. Controlled studies examine the relationship be-tween generator geometry, transport selection, and realized local action. On ImageNet 256 × 256 , TDAction attains an FID below 1.1 without distillation. 

1. Introduction 

Generative modeling learns a map Gθ whose pushforward of a simple reference distribution matches a complex data distribution, qθ = ( Gθ )#pref ≈ pdata . Diffusion and flow models make the difficult transport tractable by decompos-ing the transport into a sequence of simpler transforma-tions [18, 27, 28, 36, 38]. The sequence is then executed at

inference, requiring repeated network evaluations for every sample. One-step generation instead seeks the same end-point with a static map evaluated once. The challenge is to retain the flexibility of iterative transport while construct-ing the transport during training rather than executing the transport at inference. Figure 1 summarizes the training-time perspective by comparing the resulting distributional trajectories, target mismatch, and generator action across representative training-dynamics methods. Drifting [7] makes the training-time shift explicit. Drift-ing lets the generator pushforward evolve through the itera-tions already present in neural-network optimization, while the final trained network remains a one-step map at infer-ence. The drifting field supplies local sample displacements from the current generated and data distributions. Training dynamics become part of the generative construction rather than only the numerical procedure used to fit a fixed ob-jective. Sinkhorn-Drifting [17] uses two-sided Sinkhorn scaling to relate the Sinkhorn-Drifting training field to a Sinkhorn-divergence construction. The identifiability re-sults apply under the settings stated in the cited work. W-Flow [16] separately formulates training-time updates through descent of a distributional energy. The W-Flow Sinkhorn-divergence construction gives the target field an optimal-transport interpretation before the static generator absorbs the resulting targets. Drifting, Sinkhorn-Drifting, and W-Flow share an optimal-transport viewpoint, but none is a prerequisite for the others. However, a distributional energy does not measure the difficulty of realizing a move with the finite generator cur-rently being trained. Samples in a neural generator do not move independently: one shared parameter update needs to jointly produce the displacement of the entire batch. Two couplings that make comparable distributional progress can require different optimizer effort, or can differ in how much of the requested displacement for each coupling lies outside the generator’s local tangent space. Energy evaluates the de-sirability of a distributional state; action prices the motion required of the generator. 2. Related Work 

One-step generation and training-time transport. Dif-fusion, score-based, flow-matching, and rectified-flow mod-els use continuous or discretized transport constructions [18, 27, 28, 36, 38]. Direct generators and distillation meth-ods seek to reduce the resulting sampling cost [1, 15, 34, 39]. Drifting, Sinkhorn-Drifting, and W-Flow accumulate training-time transport in a generator while retaining one-step inference [7, 16, 17]. Sinkhorn-Drifting relates the drifting field to Sinkhorn-divergence flow through full two-sided balancing, whereas W-Flow starts from a distribu-tional energy and follows the corresponding Wasserstein gradient flow. TDAction uses the same deployment setting, but evaluates a candidate coupling through the optimizer-time effort required to realize the coupling’s joint batch dis-placement. 

Distributional geometry and action. Optimal transport and Sinkhorn divergences provide discrepancies and ge-ometries for generative modeling [9, 12, 33]. The Benamou–Brenier formulation characterizes kinetic action for a probability path [2], and Wasserstein gradient flow specifies local steepest descent of a functional on mea-sures [21]. Parameterized Wasserstein gradient flow stud-ies the effect of restricting such geometry to a model fam-ily [20]. Action Matching also studies variational actions for probability-path learning [31]. The distinction between a drift field and a state-energy gradient is also formalized for non-conservative drifting fields [11]. TDAction instead uses the optimizer-time tangent geometry of a persistent generator and does not optimize a generative-time density path. 

Model-aware and minibatch couplings. Model-Aligned Coupling (MAC) observes that geometric OT pairs can dis-agree with directions that a learned flow-matching model predicts reliably, and selects pairs using model error [26]. TDAction differs by pricing a joint endpoint displacement with a shared-parameter control value, rather than scor-ing pairs with a generative-time prediction error. Repeated minibatch OT also induces an expected batch plan whose cost and coupling can differ from population OT [3]. Batch size, coupling persistence, and held-out action prediction are method-level quantities rather than implementation de-tails. 

3. Training Dynamics Action 

We organize the method as an optimizer-time persistent one-step map. The map is static at inference, while train-ing selects the endpoint and update targets using the action required by the current generator to realize the selected tar-gets.

3.7. Finite-sample executor 

Algorithms 1 and 2 summarize the core executor. The outer loop retains ordinary detached-target regression. The inner problem uses a fixed mirror-step budget and Bregman pro-jections that enforce the transport marginals and the linear progress constraint. 

Φ( π) = Vρ(π) + ε KL( π∥ab ⊤) + λp KL( π∥πprev ).

PROJECT is the KL Bregman projection onto U(a, b ) and the half-space in Eq. 15. The projection step can be im-plemented with Sinkhorn marginal projections and a scalar dual update for the progress multiplier. Each mobility sketch is used only with the canonical support on which the sketch was estimated; a newly sampled canonical sup-port triggers a support-and-mobility refresh. Between re-freshes, the executor reuses the canonical support, keeping the mobility sketch aligned with the coupling coordinates. The mathematical executor has one shared coupling iden-tity per canonical batch and one optimizer update. The physical implementation may still use gradient accumula-tion, feature partitions, particle replay, and multiple back-ward calls to control peak memory. The large-scale cost is dominated by canonical features, coupling storage, probe refreshes, and distributed communication rather than by a dense batch-mobility matrix.

We organize the empirical evidence into controlled toy experiments, the available ImageNet diagnostic, and the causal ablation design required for a completed large-scale comparison.

4.2. ImageNet Experiments 

Evidence scope and protocol. We separate mechanism evidence, training diagnostics, and formal ImageNet eval-uation. Only a completed, compute-matched protocol with fixed latent caches, training seeds, checkpoints, guidance selection, metric statistics, and 50K generated samples can support a comparative quality claim. Building on the training-time transport perspectives of Drifting and W-Flow, TDAc-tion asks which sufficiently progressive transport a fi-nite shared-parameter generator learns. The Batch Tan-gent Action-to-Go formulation incorporates generator re-alizability directly into target selection: the local con-trol value captures optimizer effort and soft terminal mis-match, while the progress constraint prevents a low ac-tion from being obtained by under-moving the distribu-tion. The formulation recovers quadratic Euclidean as-signment in the isotropic deterministic case, distinguishes distribution-optimal from generator-action-optimal trans-port under anisotropic shared-parameter dynamics, and confines all action-based planning to training; inference re-mains a single evaluation of a static generator. Empirically, TDAction attains FID below 1.1 on Ima-geNet 256 × 256 with one-step generation, improving on the preceding Drifting and W-Flow training-time transport baselines while preserving one-NFE deployment. The con-trolled studies and the ImageNet result support the cen-tral hypothesis that reducing the realization cost of a useful transport helps a finite generator turn distributional progress into a high-quality one-step map. The present implemen-tation still relies on a local tangent approximation, a cho-sen representation and optimizer metric, and finite-sample mobility estimates; longer-horizon or curvature-aware ac-tion values, more efficient estimation, and richer conditional or multimodal generators remain open directions for future work.

Reuse the selected coupling to construct detached barycentric targets at all frozen regression levels. 5. Evaluate the ordinary regression loss, apply one optimizer update, update the EMA generator, and write the audit record.

A.16. Status of the available evidence 

The completed low-dimensional studies in the appendix document the action question and include adverse controls. The studies do not validate the active Batch-ATG executor. The corresponding Fr´ echet quantities are two-dimensional Gaussian-moment proxies, not ImageNet FID. The fixed-checkpoint ImageNet record is included below as a diagnostic of the passive precursor, not as a formal comparative result. 

Matched outer-loop protocol. A completed TDAction comparison holds the DiT backbone, latent representation, class-conditioning protocol, optimizer schedule, EMA rule, effective global batch, data order, and evaluation statistic file fixed. TDAction changes only the target construction: the method replaces an Euclidean or energy-derived coupling with the progress-constrained Batch Tangent Action-to-Go problem in Eq. 15. We do not present inherited baseline settings as com-pleted TDAction hyperparameters. 

Canonical batch and distributed execution. Each mobility sketch is tied to the source latents and real examples that define the global canonical batch (U, Y ) through the frozen map ψ in Eq. 7. The plan may reuse the canonical support within a refresh window, but a newly sampled support requires a new sketch; applying a sketch to a different batch is not part of the method. Feature extraction may be partitioned across devices or microbatches, but the planning supports are gathered before forming the coupling. The resulting π⋆k is the sole latent-to-endpoint identity for all feature levels in Eq. 18; each worker receives only the rows required for local backward processing. Independent per-device couplings are not equivalent to TDAction because independent couplings remove the cross-particle blocks of Kθ and change the optimization problem. Gradient accumulation is a memory implementation of one global plan, rather than a sequence of independently planned microbatches. 

Mobility probes and plan optimization. The geometry refresh uses primary random probes to construct the low-rank approximation bKθ ≈ BB ⊤ + σ2I and conditionally independent shadow probes to assess the selected plan. The control metric Rθ is specified as a positive, frozen planning metric at the refresh; observed AdamW updates cannot replace the 26 probes because the updates confound the current loss, data batch, and optimizer state. Between aligned support refreshes, the sketch is held fixed and the coupling solver uses the prescribed terminal tolerance ρ, entropy ε, persistence weight λp,progress fraction γ, and a fixed mirror-step budget. The persistence term is enabled only when replay supports are aligned. If the Euclidean anchor has nonpositive progress, the implementation returns the Euclidean anchor, as required by Eq. 15, instead of selecting a low-action near-identity plan. 

Targets, memory, and audit trail. The plan, mobility sketch, and barycentric targets are detached before the ordinary multi-representation regression loss in Eq. 20. Low-rank Woodbury products are used for the action value; forming the dense global nd × nd mobility is neither required nor representative of the intended scalable implementation. A completed run logs the primary and shadow split, refresh cadence, support reuse, sketch rank and ridge, planned progress ratio, post-update energy change, projection residual, target-realization cosine, plan churn, solver marginal error, progress violation, and held-out shadow action ratio, in addition to step time and allocated and reserved memory. The audit records distinguish a lower regularized surrogate value from an improvement in realized action at matched progress. 

Guidance and evaluation separation. Classifier-free guidance is an inference control and is not used to choose a training coupling retrospectively. For the available frozen 56K-step precursor, the code scale s = 1 .25 was selected on the disjoint 10K-sample seed-0 sweep and evaluated once with 50K class-balanced samples at seed 2027. The same disjoint selection rule, fixed generated-sample count, and held-out statistic file are required for a completed matched TDAction experiment. The precursor did not record active primary and shadow probes or the required held-out action audit; the precursor remains excluded from claims about the Batch-ATG implementation.
