Abstract 

A rare 3D human pose in a motion sequence may reflect either a genuine movement or an estimation error, making correction difficult when reference databases contain few compara-ble examples. We investigate whether local geometric structure can support the recovery and generation of rare configurations, using motion-capture sequences from AMASS, which unifies datasets through a common parametric human-body model, together with video-based pose estimates. We develop the cylindrical manifold attention transformer (CMAT), which extends local manifold fitting through geometry- and task-dependent neighborhood weights. For motion correction without a pretrained correction network, Self-Action CMAT retrieves comparable windows from within a sequence, adjusts their contributions for neighborhood imbalance and temporal dependence, and integrates local geometry with robust temporal regularization. Ex-periments on perturbed and estimated motions show reductions in position and acceleration errors, with component analyses examining how weighting and temporal processing contribute to recovery. The controlled correction comparisons are exploratory and conditional on the selected motion-amplitude bound. For generation, Rarity-preserving CMAT explores locally supported configurations subject to independently calibrated rarity and separation from stored observations. Under common screening criteria, it achieves a higher yield of eligible rare poses than the evaluated generation baselines. 

Keywords: 3D human pose; geometric statistics; local manifold; long-tailed motion; rare-pose generation; training-free correction. 

# 1 Introduction 

1.1 Background and related work 

Human motion sequences describe changes in joint positions and orientations over time, but esti-mated trajectories may contain spatial errors and temporal jitter. This makes correction of rare motions particularly challenging: denoising must both reduce these artifacts and preserve genuine motion variation. We define rarity relative to a specified reference motion database: a whole-body configuration is rare when it has few similar observations, but low frequency alone does not imply an estimation error. For example, arm raising and torso rotation may each be common, while their combination 1arXiv:2610.24463v1 [stat.AP] 17 Oct 2026 with a particular leg configuration occurs less often. Correcting such a pose solely because of its low population frequency risks distorting valid motion, especially when nearby phases, repeated movements or coordinated joint changes within the same sequence provide local evidence of its structure. To distinguish global rarity from local motion structure, we use standardized motion-capture representations. The SMPL parametric body model (Loper et al., 2015) maps joint rotations and body-shape parameters to the body surface and joint positions, and the AMASS archive (Mah-mood et al., 2019) unifies multiple motion-capture datasets in this representation, enabling direct comparisons of whole-body configurations across sequences while preserving the temporal order of each recording. Existing methods learn pose or motion priors across sequences to supplement unreliable obser-vations. At the single-pose level, Pose-NDF (Tiwari et al., 2022) learns pose geometry for assessing and correcting departures from supported configurations, whereas NRDF (He et al., 2024) models distances and projections in joint-rotation space. For continuous motion, HuMoR (Rempe et al., 2021) incorporates learned motion regularities into sequence optimization, while RoHM (Zhang et al., 2024) recovers trajectories affected by noise or occlusion using a learned motion model. The usefulness of these priors for rare configurations depends on their coverage in the training data: because consecutive frames are correlated, even a large archive may represent only a narrow range of poses and provide little information about rare movements. For trajectory denoising, we can also draw on relationships between frames in the observed sequence. In video-based estimation, VIBE (Kocabas et al., 2020) combines temporal modeling with a motion prior, whereas SmoothNet (Zeng et al., 2022) uses longer-range temporal relationships to reduce jitter in trajectories that have already been estimated. When applying temporal correction to rare motions, however, we must distinguish estimation noise from genuine rapid changes to avoid smoothing away the movement being recovered. We also study the synthesis of new three-dimensional poses in sparsely represented regions of a reference database. Starting from rare reference poses and their neighborhoods, we seek con-figurations that remain rare, differ from stored observations and have local geometric support. Related augmentation strategies include PoseAug (Gong et al., 2021), which learns pose transfor-mations from a three-dimensional estimator’s error feedback, and PoseTrans (Jiang et al., 2022), which transforms images and two-dimensional keypoints to increase coverage of low-frequency con-figurations. For direct three-dimensional generation, however, local support, rarity relative to the reference distribution and separation from stored samples must hold together: replaying observa-tions cannot expand geometric coverage, whereas unconstrained perturbations may lack structural support. We therefore evaluate generators by the fraction of all attempts that jointly satisfy these requirements. Despite their different objectives, rare-motion correction and rare-pose generation face a com-mon finite-sample problem: neighboring observations may be unevenly distributed and dependent, making local geometric estimates unreliable. When population coverage is limited, can geometric relationships among local observations support both the recovery of rare motions and the explo-ration of supported new configurations? Manifold fitting offers a way to organize this local evidence by estimating a smooth, lower-dimensional structure in the original observation space from noisy samples. Yao and Xia (2025) study recovery under unbounded Gaussian noise using tangent-space estimates at latent projected locations. Yao et al. (2023) develop a two-step construction that first estimates a local contraction direction and then takes a weighted local average within a cylinder aligned with that direction. These developments provide a statistical basis for recovering geometry from noisy observations under explicit sampling and noise assumptions. Manifold fitting has also been used in settings where analysis depends on recovering geometric 2relationships among high-dimensional observations. For example, Yao et al. (2024b) learn smooth mappings between latent and ambient spaces to support manifold estimation, projection and non-linear interpolation. In single-cell analysis, Yao et al. (2024a) use manifold fitting to denoise RNA-sequencing data and refine cell clustering and visualization. Li et al. (2025) use fitted manifolds to characterize metabolomic heterogeneity and examine disease associations in UK Biobank popula-tions. These applications illustrate how estimating geometry can support denoising, interpolation and the analysis of heterogeneous populations. Comparable motion phases and neighboring poses may provide local evidence of joint coordi-nation even when a configuration is globally rare. We develop the cylindrical manifold attention transformer (CMAT) to use this evidence for correcting rare trajectories and generating new, locally supported poses. 

1.2 Main contributions 

Our main contributions are as follows: 

• We extend neighborhood weighting in cylindrical local fitting to construct the cylindrical man-ifold attention transformer (CMAT). While retaining the geometric support of the cylindrical neighborhood, we allow neighbors’ contributions to vary with local geometry, observation reli-ability and relationships among observations, and select or combine local estimates according to the task. 

• We develop two task-specific implementations with different sources of information. Self-Action CMAT retrieves comparable windows from the current sequence, including similar nonadjacent motion phases. These windows may share errors and be unevenly distributed along the local structure, motivating neighborhood weighting and local-constraint selection to adjust their contributions. Combined with robust temporal regularization, this yields joint-trajectory correction without a pretrained correction network. Rarity-preserving CMAT instead draws local support from neighboring poses in the reference database. It explores new configurations while constraining independently calibrated rarity and separation from stored poses, to avoid concentrating on common configurations or merely reproducing existing observations. In the controlled AMASS evaluation, Self-Action CMAT achieves lower errors than the evaluated comparators within the selected evaluation population. 2.3 Adding attention to local manifold fitting 

CMAT adds attention to local manifold fitting by assigning task-dependent weights to neighboring observations. In the present implementations, attention weights are determined by explicit rules or constrained optimization, without training a neural network. Following the terminology in the review by Yao and Su (2026), we refer to the cylindrical local fitting operator introduced above as the Yao–Yau estimator. The original construction first estimates a direction from a local mean, then averages observations within a cylinder along that direction. CMAT retains this local support and changes how observations contribute to the second average.

3.2 Rarity stability across reference sizes 

To assess whether a fixed reference can provide reproducible rarity classifications for generation, we construct a benchmark with three million AMASS reference poses and separate calibration and evaluation cohorts. With at most 16 uniformly sampled poses per sequence, the calibration cohort contains 2,272 poses from eight subjects, and the fixed evaluation cohort contains 3,302 poses from 207 sequences and nine different subjects. We vary the reference size across 100,000, 500,000, one million, two million, 2.5 million and three million poses. At each size below three million, we draw three reference subsets, recalibrate rarity separately for each subset and evaluate stability across all three pairwise comparisons. For pose i in sequence s of subject h, we assign weight wi = ( HL hmhs )−1, where H denotes the number of evaluation subjects, Lh the number of sequences for subject h, and mhs the number of evaluated poses in sequence s. This weighting gives equal contributions to subjects and, within each subject, to sequences. We summarize selection size by the weighted rare fraction and reproducibility by weighted overall label agreement and rare-set Jaccard overlap; unweighted Spearman correlation and median absolute percentile change measure ranking and numerical stability (Supplementary Section B.4). 20 Figure 8 shows that larger references improve agreement of labels and rare sets across repeated draws while reducing changes in calibrated percentiles. 3.3.3 Comparison of pose generation approaches 

We consider five baseline generators to examine different ways of expanding rare-pose samples. All methods output poses in the SMPL body-joint rotation representation. 

Rare replay. This control outputs previously screened rare reference poses, testing whether direct duplication can satisfy the screening requirements for expanding the sample. 

Axis-angle Gaussian. Composing existing poses with random joint-rotation increments pro-duces nearby configurations, providing a test of unstructured perturbation. 

Part mix. After partitioning joints into body parts, we recombine corresponding part rotations from different observed poses. The resulting full-body configurations preserve observed rotations within each part while changing the coordination across parts. 

PoseTrans-inspired 3D. We adapt the tail-expansion strategy of PoseTrans to the shared SMPL joint-rotation representation, transforming existing rare poses. 

PoseAug-style 3D. PoseAug’s transformation-based augmentation approach motivates a sec-ond comparator in SMPL joint-rotation space. The five baselines and Rarity-preserving CMAT use the same three million reference poses and 16,440 rare anchors, with 50,000 attempts per method and the same acceptance criteria. Each attempt uses the same anchor across generators, together with its source, excluded subject and corresponding calibration context. For every method, the non-memorization distance threshold is the weighted 1st percentile of distances from independent calibration poses to their nearest complete-reference poses, and the local deviation ratio must not exceed 0 .55. # 4 Discussion 

This work introduces attention into local manifold fitting so that the contribution of each neighbor-ing observation can be adapted to the task. Correction and generation specify their own weights and output rules, applying the same local construction to trajectory recovery and the expansion of rare configurations. This design also leaves room for learned manifold fitting: attention scores or combinations of heads could be parameterized and learned from independent training data while retaining explicit neighborhood and geometric constraints. In single-sequence correction, retrieving many similar windows from one trajectory does not provide the same information as independent replicates: nearby windows share frames and estima-tion errors, and matches may lie asymmetrically around the local structure. Self-Action CMAT accounts for these features through distance weighting, center balancing and correlation-aware weights, followed by candidate comparison to determine the contribution of local geometry to tra-jectory recovery. The component comparisons support the roles of weighting and selection while 24 indicating that improvements in spatial accuracy can involve a trade-off with temporal acceleration smoothness. Under the common screening criteria in this experiment, Rarity-preserving CMAT yields more accepted poses than the tested generators. The individual pass rates show that local fitting support is only one requirement: sufficient separation from stored poses also affects the final yield. These results show that generated poses pass the specified screens, but do not establish improvements in downstream models. Both applications demonstrate that manifold fitting without a separately trained network can support rare-motion recovery and constrained pose generation. In the controlled AMASS evalua-tion, Self-Action CMAT has lower errors than the pretrained SmoothNet comparator. These results support sequence-specific local geometry as an effective basis for correction without a pretrained correction network under the evaluated conditions. The two applications together demonstrate the feasibility of constructing outputs directly from available observations. For correction, independently designed perturbations and new subjects can test the present find-ings; candidate selection under other error-dependence structures and constraints on bone length, contact and body geometry also merit study. For generation, accepted poses should be used to train downstream models under fixed training budgets and evaluated on independent rare-pose test sets. Continuous motion, mesh collisions and calibration across populations remain further directions. In addition, comparisons of untrained, partly learned and end-to-end learned approaches should include accuracy, latency, memory and the cost of adapting to new tasks, to establish when methods without task-specific training offer practical advantages.

For rarity retrieval, we project rotation-matrix embeddings to 64 PCA coordinates, screen candi-dates with an IVF index, and rerank them by product rotation distance. We use this configuration for both calibration and queries, explicitly converting between scalar-first quaternions for geometry and scalar-last quaternions for rarity retrieval.
