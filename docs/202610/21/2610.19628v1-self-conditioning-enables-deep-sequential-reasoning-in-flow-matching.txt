# ABSTRACT 

Recent work has explored visual reasoning through image generation, where mod-els solve visual-reasoning problems by directly generating solution images. Intu-itively, iterative samplers such as flow matching are well suited to this task, since each sampling step may resemble one reasoning step. However, in this work, from a circuit complexity perspective, we show that this intuition is misleading. We prove that as long as the model outputs satisfy certain mild regularity conditions, the trajectory of the vanilla flow-matching sampler can be simulated by a constant number of sequential rounds of parallel model evaluations. Consequently, addi-tional sampling steps cannot increase the expressive power of a constant-depth backbone model under the standard complexity-theoretic model of transformers. We then reveal that self-conditioning , which conditions the model on the previ-ous step’s estimate of the final image, plays a previously unrecognized role in removing this bottleneck. Controlled experiments on diverse visual-reasoning tasks show that flow matching with self-conditioning achieves higher accuracy and greater reasoning depth than vanilla flow matching, and its performance improves with more sampling steps. Theoretically, we prove that with self-conditioning, a constant-depth backbone can achieve expressive power equivalent to that of space-bounded Turing machines. Our study suggests a design principle for future visual reasoning research: carry more information between sampling steps, so that addi-tional steps translate into deeper reasoning. 1

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in visual generative models have sparked growing interest in generative visual reasoning , where models plan and reason directly in image space (Su et al., 2025b; Hu et al., 2024; Tong et al., 2026). This paradigm is natural for spatial planning, combinatorial optimization, and structured rendering (He et al., 2025; Dai et al., 2026). Intuitively, flow matching (Lipman et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022) is well suited to this setting: it turns noise into an image through K evaluations of a velocity model, providing K sequential rounds for reasoning to unfold. He et al. (2025) put this intuition into practice, solving visual reasoning tasks via flow-matching image generation. However, in this work we show that this apparent reasoning depth is an illusion. Through circuit complexity analysis, we find that the expressive power of vanilla flow matching (V ANILLA FM) does not grow with the number of sampling steps K. The intuition lies in how flow matching generates images: the sampling process discretizes an ordinary differential equation (ODE), making only a small incremental update of scale O(1 /K ) to its state at each step. We first review flow matching and circuit complexity (§2.1), then formalize the modeling conven-tions and regularity conditions underlying our theoretical analysis (§2.2). 2.1 BACKGROUND 

Flow matching. Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022) is a generative modeling framework. At inference time, a sample is generated by solving the ODE with a numerical scheme such as Euler’s method (Hairer et al., 1993; Liu et al., 2022; Esser et al., 2024). For conditional generation, such as text-to-image and image editing, the model is conditioned on context ctx , with pdata now a joint distribution over pairs (ctx , x data ). This amounts to learning a velocity field uθ (x, t ; ctx) minimizing Jcond := Et,ϵ, (ctx ,x data )∥uθ (˜ xt, t ; ctx) − (xdata − ϵ)∥22.

Circuit complexity. We measure the expressive power of a model by the complexity of the lan-guages it can decide. A language is a subset L ⊆ { 0, 1}∗, and to decide L is to determine whether a given input w ∈ { 0, 1}m belongs to L. We abstract the model as a circuit family (Cm)m≥1, where each Cm is a Boolean circuit that handles inputs of length m (Arora et al., 2009). The size of a circuit is the number of gates and its depth is the length of the longest input-to-output path. Depth measures sequential computation, whereas size measures total work. For k ≥ 0, NC k, AC k, and TC k denote the non-uniform classes of languages decided by polynomial-size, depth-O(log k m) circuit families with bounded-fan-in Boolean, unbounded-fan-in Boolean, and unbounded-fan-in Boolean plus MAJORITY gates, respectively. We call circuits of unbounded-fan-in AND, OR, NOT, and MAJORITY gates majority circuits . The class NC := S

These classes bound the power of common neural network architectures: a constant-depth log-precision transformer without chain-of-thought (CoT) lies in TC 0 (Merrill et al., 2022; Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023; Chiang, 2024), and variants such as unique hard-attention transformers (Hao et al., 2022) and constant-precision transformers (Li et al., 2024) lie in AC 0 instead. Surpassing these bounds requires more layers or, as with CoT, additional sequential calls (Li et al., 2024). # 3 SELF -C ONDITIONING INCREASES EXPRESSIVE POWER 

In this section, we first show that, under the mild regularity conditions below, vanilla flow-matching inference is confined to constant-depth circuit classes (§3.1), which restricts the problems it can solve. We then consider self-conditioning (§3.2), a simple mechanism that allows the same models to attain substantially greater expressive power (§3.3). 3.1 VANILLA FLOW MATCHING IS CONFINED TO CONSTANT DEPTH 

Although V ANILLA FM makes K sequential model evaluations, as long as the model outputs satisfy certain mild regularity conditions, this sequential depth is an illusion : the same decision can be reached in O(L) sequential rounds of parallel evaluations of the model, regardless of how large K

is. When L is a constant, these are constantly many rounds, so the decided language stays within the model’s own complexity class. Below we view K as a function of the problem size m, and state the theorem under the setup of §2.2.

L-Lipschitz continuous. Then as long as the state dimension satisfies n = poly( m), and the number of fractional bits satisfies

Self-conditioning in practice. Self-conditioning is particularly natural for visual-space reason-ing: the fed-back prediction zi approximates the clean sample xdata and is therefore near the target data manifold in image space. Modern image generation models already accept images as con-ditions (Black Forest Labs et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025), so it is reasonable to expect knowledge from image-conditioned pretraining to transfer to self-conditioning, even though that pretraining involves no self-conditioning. v(1) = uθ (˜ xt, t ; w) and at the first sampling step, without affecting empirical performance. 3.3 SELF -C ONDITIONING REACHES PSPACE /poly 

We now formally show that self-conditioning expands the expressiveness of flow matching. Since the extra input zi+1 is a fresh prediction rather than an O(1 /K ) increment on xi, it is unconstrained by the step size and can change abruptly between sampling steps. Thus, the convergence behind Lemma 1 is lost, and the constant-depth barrier disappears altogether: constant-depth models with self-conditioning can decide P/poly in polynomially many steps and PSPACE /poly in at most exponentially many steps .

Theorem 2 (Lower bounds on the expressiveness of flow matching with self-conditioning) . For any language L in PSPACE /poly , there exists a model u that accepts L with margin 1 under 

SELF COND FM and satisfies the following conditions: (a) u can be computed by AC 0 circuits; (b) u is 4-bounded and 8-Lipschitz continuous; (c) b = O(log m), n = poly( m), K ≤ 2poly( m). If moreover L ∈ P/poly , then the same result holds with (1) satisfied. 

The proof makes the sampler simulate a Turing machine with advice. The prediction zi holds the machine’s tape and state, and each model call advances the machine by one step, so K calls run the machine for Ω( K) steps. See Appendix A.3.2 for the construction. In the P/poly case, all the regularity hypotheses of Theorem 1 are retained, so the two theorems concern the same model class and the gap comes from the inference algorithm alone. For D ∈{AC 0, TC 0}, the two samplers therefore separate across the barrier P/poly \ D: given any language 

L ∈ P/poly \ D, Theorem 2 supplies an AC 0 model accepting it under S ELF COND FM, whereas Theorem 1 leaves no D model satisfying its hypotheses capable of accepting it under V ANILLA FM. For D = AC 0, the separation always holds since AC 0 ⊊ TC 0 (Furst et al., 1984); for D = TC 0, it holds under the conjecture TC 0 ⊊ NC 1 (Vollmer, 1999), which implies that TC 0 ⊊ P/poly .3.4 EXTENSIONS 

Beyond the main results above, the analysis extends to more general settings. Lemma 1 di-rectly implies an NC upper bound for V ANILLA FM when the Lipschitz constant L grows as 

L(m) = poly(log m), or TC k upper bounds when further L = O(log k m) for any constant in-teger k ≥ 0 (Appendix B.1). Restricting u to be Lipschitz continuous under ℓp norms rather than 

ℓ∞ norms preserves both Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 (Appendix B.2). Replacing the Euler method with any fixed explicit Runge–Kutta method (Hairer et al., 1993) whose nodes lie in [0 , 1] , such as Heun’s method (Karras et al., 2022), leaves the asymptotic results for V ANILLA FM unchanged (Appendix B.3). On the other hand, the O(L(m)) bound of Lemma 1 is tight with respect to circuit depth: any polynomial-size majority circuit of depth d can be simulated under V ANILLA FM in O(d) sequential rounds by an O(d)-Lipschitz continuous model u computed by TC 0 circuits. The bound of Lemma 1 therefore cannot be improved to o(L(m)) rounds of the same form unless the depth hierarchy for polynomial-size majority circuits collapses (Appendix B.4). 

# 4 EXPERIMENTS 

The analysis in §3 establishes an expressivity separation under the stated assumptions: SELF COND FM can decide languages beyond the reach of V ANILLA FM. Whether models trained on finite data realize this expressive advantage remains an empirical question. Therefore, we con-duct controlled experiments to empirically validate the expressivity separation (§4.2). We further 6Preprint extend the evaluation to the five structured visual-reasoning tasks used by He et al. (2025) through controlled comparisons and larger-scale experiments (§4.3). Finally, we provide supporting analyses that assess the applicability of our theory to trained models and characterize the inference behavior of V ANILLA FM and S ELF COND FM (§4.4). 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Within each task, V ANILLA FM and S ELF COND FM share the same backbone architecture, training dataset, optimizer, and noise schedule. The only difference is that S ELF COND FM passes the end-point prediction from one backbone model evaluation to the next as an additional condition (§3.2). During training, S ELF COND FM makes two forward passes per example instead of one, costing ex-tra compute, so we match the two methods on cumulative training FLOPs. Appendix D.1 provides the complete configurations and FLOPs accounting. Since both V ANILLA FM and S ELF COND FM make one backbone call per sampling step, we com-pare them by default at the same number of function evaluations (NFE), which directly measures sequential computation and equals the step count K in Algorithms 1 and 2. Unless otherwise stated, we use 50 NFEs; the NFE-scaling study in §4.2 varies the number of evaluations. 4.2 EMPIRICAL VALIDATION OF THE EXPRESSIVITY SEPARATION 

To empirically validate the expressivity separation, we first compare the two methods on three tasks with natural step-by-step solutions: line tracing , long multiplication , and long text rendering (Fig-ure 1). Each task, formally defined and analyzed in Appendix C, has circuit complexity matching the separation barrier identified in §3.1 for either AC 0 or TC 0 backbones (the latter case assumes 

TC 0̸ = NC 1). Consequently, we expect these tasks to lie beyond the expressive power of vanilla flow matching with the corresponding backbone. 

Tasks. We denote the problem scale for each task by m. Line tracing ( NC 1-complete) requires col-oring five long polylines that go through m permutation stages; long multiplication ( TC 0-complete) entails completing a step-by-step long multiplication worksheet to compute the product of two inte-gers, each with at most m digits; and long text rendering ( TC 0-complete) requires rendering some paragraphs containing a total of m words onto a fixed-width canvas. We compare the methods on finite-scale synthetic datasets. For line tracing, we report the mean longest correct prefix (LCP), which is the average number of initial stages through which all five polylines remain correctly col-ored; for the other tasks, we report instance-level accuracy. See Appendix D.2 for more details. 

Backbone architectures. All three tasks use FLUX.2 -based architectures with their pretrained VAE kept frozen for image encoding and decoding (Black Forest Labs, 2026). For line tracing, we train a model from scratch; for the other two tasks, we fine-tune FLUX.2-klein-base-4B to leverage its prior knowledge of character recognition and generation.

# 5 RELATED WORK 

Generative visual reasoning. Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) typically treat images as static inputs and externalize reasoning through textual CoT. The emerging thinking with images 

paradigm instead turns vision into an intermediate workspace that can be repeatedly inspected or modified (Su et al., 2025b). Early approaches manipulate images through tool calls (Su et al., 2025a; 2026; Lai et al., 2026), while other approaches internalize visual intermediates within the model, in the form of generated images, latent visual states, or learned visual representations (Xu et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025; Qin et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026a; Gu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Cheng et al., 2026). These intermediates give the model a richer workspace for reasoning than text alone. Video generation offers another medium for reasoning, representing evolving intermediate states as a sequence of frames (Wiedemer et al., 2025; Tong et al., 2026). Recent works further suggest that video diffusion models (Ho et al., 2022; Xing et al., 2024) reason across denoising steps: they explore candidate solutions, correct earlier mistakes, and commit early to high-level plans (Wang et al., 2026b; Newman et al., 2026). Recent work moves reasoning into image generation itself: DiffThinker uses direct image-to-image generation (He et al., 2025), while EndoCoT bridges latent states in an MLLM’s CoT to image generation (Dai et al., 2026). However, the computational structure of generative visual reasoning remains underexplored. Liu (2025) establishes a TC 0 upper bound for optimal denoising diffusion probabilistic models, but requires the model to match the score function exactly, an assumption that rarely holds for practical trained models. 

Self-conditioning. Self-conditioning feeds a model’s endpoint prediction back into a subsequent denoising step. It was introduced by Chen et al. (2022) for diffusion over discrete data, and has since been adapted to continuous diffusion language modeling, image and video generation, and molecular flow matching (Strudel et al., 2022; Jabri et al., 2022; Gupta et al., 2024; St¨ ark et al., 2023). Prior work primarily studies generation quality and efficiency empirically; we instead treat self-conditioning as maintaining a computational state and analyze its expressive power. 9Preprint 

Computational Expressiveness. The expressive power of transformers (Vaswani et al., 2017) has been studied extensively through circuit complexity, formal languages, and logic (Strobl et al., 2024). Depending on the attention mechanism and numerical precision, constant-depth transformers are upper-bounded by AC 0 or TC 0 (Hao et al., 2022; Merrill et al., 2022; Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023; Chiang, 2024), and such circuit bounds extend to modern components such as rotary positional em-beddings (Chen et al., 2025). Chiang et al. (2023) further tighten both the upper and lower bounds through a logical characterization. Repeated model calls can lift computation beyond these single-evaluation bounds. With chain-of-thought (Wei et al., 2022), a constant-depth transformer writes its intermediate results into the autoregressive context and can thereby solve inherently serial problems (Li et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2023; Merrill & Sabharwal, 2024). Amiri et al. (2025) complement this with lower bounds on the number of chain-of-thought steps that hard-attention transformers need for various algorithmic prob-lems. Beyond autoregressive generation, Feng et al. (2025) establish theoretical bounds for masked diffusion language models, proving that while constant sampling steps suffice for distribution match-ing, sequential reasoning requires sampling steps that scale with sequence length. 

# 6 CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION 

This work reveals a previously unrecognized role of self-conditioning in generative reasoning. We show that vanilla flow matching cannot deepen sequential reasoning through additional sampling steps. Self-conditioning breaks this bottleneck by passing endpoint predictions across steps, which lets additional sampling steps translate into reasoning depth. Experiments across diverse visual-reasoning tasks confirm this separation: self-conditioning achieves better performance and consis-tently benefits from more sampling steps. These theoretical guarantees, however, rely on certain regularity assumptions. In particular, the upper bound in Theorem 1 requires a fairly strong Lipschitz restriction. Moreover, the Lips-chitz estimates in §4.4 are relatively large and provide empirical rather than certified bounds (Ap-pendix D.5.1). Since the dependence on the Lipschitz constant is tight up to constant factors (§3.4), future work may explore alternative forms of regularity conditions. Although we focus on image-space visual reasoning, our theoretical analysis does not depend on the data modality. It therefore applies wherever flow matching serves as the sampling backbone, including video generation (Kong et al., 2024; Ifriqi et al., 2026), continuous diffusion language modeling (Chen et al., 2026), and visuomotor policy learning (Black et al., 2024). Whether self-conditioning delivers similar empirical gains remains an open question. Nevertheless, this perspec-tive may inform future sampler design in settings where iterative generation is expected to reason.

We replace them by a Picard iteration (Hairer et al., 1993) that updates all K positions at once, and whose propagated error decays factorially across iteration rounds, up to an additive error floor (Appendix A.2.1). Therefore, O(L) rounds already approximate the sampler’s output to within a constant error; this proves Lemma 1 in Appendix A.2.2. Aside from the K parallel model calls, each round computes normalized prefix sums of the velocities, which Appendix A.2.3 approximates in AC 0 up to a constant error, small enough for acceptance with margin (see §2.2). D.1 OVERALL SETUP 

Backbones. For line tracing, we train a multimodal diffusion transformer (MMDiT) with 9M pa-rameters from random initialization, following the FLUX.2 architecture (Black Forest Labs, 2026). It has three joint-attention blocks and three single-stream blocks, each with four attention heads and head dimension 64 . Other runs fine-tune pretrained backbones with LoRA adapters (rank and alpha 

32 , no dropout). Long multiplication, long text rendering, and the controlled comparison of §4.3 use FLUX.2-klein-base-4B (Black Forest Labs, 2026). The large-scale comparison in §4.3 instead uses Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 (Qwen Team, 2025). 

Implementation of self-conditioning. All backbones encode images into VAE latents and pack each 2 × 2 latent patch into one image token. In the second training pass and at each sampling step after the first, S ELF COND FM concatenates the tokens of the endpoint prediction with the tokens of the noisy target and of any conditioning image before the transformer blocks. The FLUX.2 

backbones append the tokens of the endpoint prediction with a distinct positional encoding offset, and Qwen-Image-Edit places them after the tokens of the target image. No attention mask separates these blocks of tokens. Both placements follow the backbones’ own design for image-conditioned generation. Thus self-conditioning changes the input sequence but not the transformer architecture. 

Training FLOPs. We match training compute by cumulative FLOPs, the total number of floating-point operations a run performs. We estimate it as the training FLOPs per micro-batch, averaged over 100 micro-batches, multiplied by the number of micro-batches processed across all devices. Each measurement covers the complete training computation, including VAE encoding, the second SELF COND FM training pass, and the longer self-conditioned token sequence, so the extra cost of self-conditioning is fully counted. The plotted budgets in Figure 1 are 4.50 × 10 18 FLOPs for line tracing, 2.32 × 10 19 for long multiplication, and 4.70 × 10 19 for long text rendering.
