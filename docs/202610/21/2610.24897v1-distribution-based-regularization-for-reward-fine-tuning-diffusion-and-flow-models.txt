ABSTRACT
Reward fine-tuning is an effective way to adapt pretrained diffusion and flow-
matching models to downstream objectives, but aggressive optimization often
leads to mode collapse and distributional drift.
Existing methods attempt to
control this drift with reverse-KL regularization, implemented as an ℓ2 penalty
on the velocity field; however, this functions by indirectly constraining sampling
dynamics rather than directly acting on the generated distribution. To address
this, we propose DFR (Distributional Functional Rewards), which augments the
task reward with a distribution-dependent, per-sample reward that acts explicitly
on the generated distribution.
Formally, we generalize the KL-regularized
objective from expected rewards, which are linear in the model’s distribution, to
convex functionals of it. The first variation of this objective defines an effective,
distribution-dependent reward that directly penalizes deviation from the pretrained
distribution. This formulation yields additive per-sample rewards for the Fr´echet
distance (FD), maximum mean discrepancy (MMD), and DreamSim diversity,
recovering several recently proposed rewards as special cases. Across various
text-to-image fine-tuning tasks, DFR matches the peak reward of KL-tuned
baselines while increasing diversity by up to 29% at equal or lower drift.

INTRODUCTION
Diffusion and flow-matching models (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021;
Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Albergo et al., 2023; Peluchetti, 2023) have become a dominant
paradigm for generative modeling across various data modalities, including images (Rombach
et al., 2022; Labs et al., 2025), videos (Wan et al., 2025), and molecular structures (Abramson
et al., 2024). These models learn stochastic or deterministic dynamics that transport a simple source
distribution to a complex target distribution. However, downstream applications rarely value all
samples equally: the goal is often to shift probability mass toward high-reward samples, while
preserving the diversity and capabilities of the pretrained model. Reward fine-tuning achieves this
by adapting the pretrained model toward a downstream reward (Uehara et al., 2024).
For non-differentiable rewards, existing approaches broadly fall into two families: (1) Trajec-
tory-based methods, which formulate sampling as a multi-step Markov decision process (MDP),
treating each step of the sampling process as an action (Black et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2023); and
(2) ELBO-based methods, which instead treat the final sample as the action and approximate
its intractable log-likelihood with an evidence lower bound (McAllister et al., 2026). Recently,
ELBO-based methods have emerged as particularly attractive options for large-scale text-to-image
fine-tuning, reporting up to ∼50× faster training than trajectory-based methods at comparable
reward and image quality (Zheng et al., 2026; Xue et al., 2026; Bergmeister et al., 2026).
Across both families, however, reward maximization alone does not specify which properties of
the pretrained distribution ought to be preserved. In particular, optimizing toward increasingly
high-reward samples can reduce sample diversity and move the generated distribution away from the

pretrained model. Existing methods often address this tension through KL regularization, typically
implemented as an ℓ2 penalty between the fine-tuned and pretrained velocity fields (Fan et al., 2023;
Liu et al., 2025b). However, this regularization acts on the model’s sampling dynamics, whereas the
properties of ultimate interest—diversity and retention of the pretrained distribution—are properties
of its output distribution. This raises the natural question:
Can we regularize the generated distribution directly during reward fine-tuning?
We answer this question affirmatively with Distributional Functional Rewards (DFR), a gen-
eral framework for distribution-regularized reward fine-tuning.
DFR unifies trajectory- and
ELBO-based methods by augmenting reward optimization with objectives defined directly on the
generated distribution. This allows models to improve reward while better preserving the pretrained
distribution. Our main contributions are:
• A unified framework for distribution-regularized reward fine-tuning.
DFR generalizes
KL-regularized reward optimization to convex functionals of the clean-sample distribution,
including maximum mean discrepancy (MMD), Fr´echet distance (FD), and diversity objectives.
Their first variations induce distribution-dependent per-sample reward corrections, enabling direct
control of the generated distribution in both trajectory- and ELBO-based methods.
• Improved distribution preservation at matched reward. On GenEval and OCR with SD3.5-M,
DFR reduces drift from the pretrained model by up to 12% and increases diversity by up to
29% relative to KL-regularized baselines at matched reward, while training 36–41× faster than
Flow-GRPO.
• Compatibility with both feature- and latent-space objectives.
We instantiate DFR with
feature-space objectives such as FD and DreamSim diversity, as well as latent-space MMD,
demonstrating strong performance even without the use of pretrained feature encoders.
• Minimal overhead over KL-regularized fine-tuning. Compared with KL-tuned RAM, DFR
adds little training cost: DFR-MMD reaches GenEval 0.95 with only 7% more training time than
RAM (β = 3) while achieving 9% higher diversity, and DFR-DS reaches OCR 0.96 with only
1% more time than RAM (β = 5) while achieving 17% higher diversity.

DFR: DISTRIBUTIONAL REGULARIZATION FOR REWARD FINE-TUNING
The velocity anchor in Eq. (4) only indirectly controls the reverse-KL divergence in Eq. (2), as it
penalizes changes in the sampling dynamics even when the endpoint distribution pθ is unchanged.
In ELBO-based methods, heuristic time weighting at forward-noised states further weakens this
correspondence. We nevertheless retain the velocity anchor for training stability and to limit drift,
yet we complement it with a term that acts directly on the generated distribution. Importantly,
the two objectives are compatible: the optimal fine-tuned process preserves the pretrained model’s
conditional noising process (demonstrated in Appendix C.2).
We generalize the KL-regularized objective of Eq. (2) from expected rewards to convex function-
als of the generated distribution (Section 3.1). The first variation of such a functional acts as a
per-sample reward, which any policy gradient method can optimize without further changes (Sec-
tion 3.2), and instantiate the framework with MMD, FD, and DreamSim diversity (Section 3.3).

EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate whether DFR improves the trade-off between reward and reward hacking beyond what
tuning the reverse-KL coefficient of the baseline can achieve.
4.1
SETUP
Tasks and methods. We fine-tune Stable Diffusion 3.5 Medium (SD3.5-M; Esser et al., 2024) on
two tasks: (1) Compositional generation (GenEval; Ghosh et al., 2023), with reward thresholds 0.95
and 0.96, and (2) Visual text rendering (OCR), with threshold 0.96. We adopt RAM (Bergmeister
et al., 2026) as the ELBO-based framework on which DFR builds on. We sweep its reverse-KL co-
efficient β over {1, . . . , 4} for GenEval and {2, . . . , 6} for OCR. DFR-MMD, DFR-FD, and DFR-
DS augment RAM with the distributional rewards introduced in Section 3.3 (Algorithm 1). For each
variant, we use the largest β for which RAM reaches the highest reward threshold, or the next smaller
value when the distributional reward itself provides substantial regularization toward the pretrained
model. All configurations are trained with three random seeds under identical compute budgets.
Baselines.
We compare DFR against other ELBO- and trajectory-based methods includ-
ing DiffusionNFT (Zheng et al., 2026), AWM (Xue et al., 2026), RSM (Lee et al., 2026), and
Flow-GRPO (Liu et al., 2025b) on both the compositional generation and visual text rendering tasks.
Protocol. Because reward hacking intensifies over the course of optimization, we compare models
at matched target reward levels. For each seed, we evaluate the first checkpoint that reaches a given
threshold; a configuration is considered to reach that threshold only if all three seeds do so.
Metrics.
Following Liu et al. (2025d;c); Domingo-Enrich et al. (2025), we measure reward
hacking by drift and diversity, each computed per-prompt over 64 images. Drift is the Fr´echet
distance to the 64 images of pref in Inception-v3 and DINOv2 features. Diversity is measured by
DreamSim diversity, computed with its DINO and OpenCLIP branches. Preference rewards such as
ImageReward (Xu et al., 2023) and PickScore (Kirstain et al., 2023) are reported but not used, since
they score each image in isolation and cannot detect a model that generates near-identical images
for every seed. Two configurations are tied on a metric if their means differ by at most the larger of
their standard deviations; otherwise, the configuration with the better mean wins. Entries computed
with an encoder that a DFR instance uses in its reward, or a closely related one, are counted as
neither wins nor losses.
Consequently, the main text reports the OpenCLIP branch, which no
instance trains on. Additional training, evaluation, and baseline details are provided in Appendix D.

RELATED WORK
Regularization for reward fine-tuning. Most reward fine-tuning methods for flow-based models
use the velocity anchor in Eq. (4), which constrains the sampling dynamics rather than pθ itself
(Section 3.1). DDRL (Ye et al., 2025) instead regularizes toward an off-policy data distribution
using the forward-KL divergence, implemented through an ELBO objective on real data or
pretrained-model samples. Flow Density Control (De Santi et al., 2025) also optimizes functionals
of the generated distribution, using gradients of the effective reward. DFR instead uses the effective
reward only as a scalar signal in policy-gradient updates, without differentiating through it, which
makes it directly applicable to non-differentiable rewards, and we provide extensive empirical
evidence of scalability through systematic experiments on SD3.5-M.
Diversity and distributional rewards.Objectives defined as functionals of a distribution have been
studied in reinforcement learning (Hazan et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020). Several works augment
the target reward with diversity or distributional signals.
GARDO (He et al., 2025) increases
the rewards of high-quality samples far from their nearest neighbors, while DiverseGRPO (Liu
et al., 2025a) assigns bonuses based on semantic-cluster sizes; neither signal is derived from an
explicit distributional objective. DRIFT (Liu et al., 2026) rewards each sample by its mean squared
DreamSim distance to the other samples in its group, which coincides with the DreamSim instance
of DFR (see Appendix C.7).
Other methods match a prescribed target distribution. LOO-FID (Baran et al., 2026) and Miao et al.
(2024) reward samples by their leave-one-out change in, respectively, the FD to real-image statistics
and the MMD to curated reference images; these are finite-difference estimates of the corresponding
first variations (Appendix C.6.2). kCGM (Diamant & Trippe, 2026) minimizes MMD to a target
feature distribution using an estimator of the form in Eq. (16). DistWise (Li et al., 2026) uses the
FID between a large generated set and real data as a distribution-level reward, while DRAGON (Bai
et al., 2025) contrasts positive and negative generation sets under distribution-level rewards. In these
9


Preprint.
methods, matching a real or curated target distribution—typically in a pretrained feature space—
is itself the objective. By contrast, DFR derives per-sample rewards as first variations of convex
functionals of pθ, yielding the explicit objective in Eq. (12), where deviation from the pretrained
model distribution regularizes target-reward maximization alongside the KL anchor. Consequently,
DFR requires neither an image dataset nor, for its MMD instance, a pretrained encoder.

CONCLUSION
In this work, we introduce Distributional Functional Rewards (DFR), a general-purpose framework
for reward fine-tuning that complements KL regularization with objectives defined directly on
the model’s output distribution.
By deriving per-sample rewards from the first variations of
distributional functionals, DFR provides explicit control over diversity and distributional drift.
Our findings clearly demonstrate that KL regularization has a limited effective range: beyond it,
stronger regularization slows optimization, limits reward improvement, and/or destabilizes training.
On two distinct tasks: compositional generation and visual text rendering, we find that DFR
consistently improves the reward–distribution trade-off beyond what is attainable by tuning the KL
coefficient alone. At matched task reward, every evaluated DFR variant improves diversity, reduces
drift, or both, while matching or improving all eligible distributional metrics relative to KL-only
RAM baselines that reach the same reward threshold. These gains require only a simple reward
augmentation, adding 3–9% overhead per training step, while not relying on a pretrained encoder.
Overall, DFR enables reward fine-tuning to achieve high task rewards, while better preserving
diversity and remaining closer to the pretrained model distribution than KL regularization alone.
Limitations and future work.
Although presented as a general-purpose reward fine-tuning
framework, our experiments are focused on validation in image settings; extending DFR to other
modalities such as video and molecular structures is a natural next step to more effectively evaluate
the generalization of the framework. Further, since the distributional reward regularizes toward
pref, it preserves the pretrained model’s biases along with its diversity, so its coefficient λ must
be tuned jointly with β.
Balancing DFR’s repulsive and attractive terms more carefully is a
promising direction for improving this trade-off. Our characterization of the optimum assumes
exact optimization of the population objective, which the ELBO surrogate and the finite-sample
estimates of the distributional rewards approximate in practice.

A
EXTENDED RELATED WORK
Differentiable rewards. When the reward is differentiable, its gradient can be propagated directly
through the generative process. DRaFT (Clark et al., 2024) backpropagates through the sampling
chain, optionally truncating the gradient to the final steps, whereas ReFL (Xu et al., 2023) backprop-
agates through a single late denoising step from which the clean sample is predicted directly. Adjoint
Matching (Domingo-Enrich et al., 2025) formulates fine-tuning as stochastic optimal control under
a memoryless noise schedule, whose optimum solves Eq. (2), and regresses the velocity toward a
target defined by an adjoint state that propagates the reward gradient backward along the trajectory.
Shin et al. (2026) reduce its cost using a linear base drift, enabling few-step deterministic sampling
and a closed-form adjoint. VGG-Flow (Liu et al., 2025c) matches the difference between fine-tuned
and pretrained velocities to the gradient of a learned value function, while ∇-GFlowNet (Liu et al.,
2025d) derives gradient-informed GFlowNet objectives; both seek to preserve the pretrained prior
and sample diversity. For broader unifying perspectives on reward fine-tuning, we refer readers to
Uehara et al. (2024); Choi et al. (2026); Lee et al. (2026); Xu et al. (2026).
Non-differentiable rewards. Trajectory-based methods formulate denoising as a multi-step MDP
and apply policy gradients to per-step transition likelihoods (Black et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2023;
Liu et al., 2025b; Xue et al., 2025). Because these likelihoods require stochastic transitions, the
sampling ODE is converted to an SDE with the same marginals. Li et al. (2025); He et al. (2026)
improve efficiency by restricting stochastic sampling and optimization to a subset of timesteps.
ELBO-based methods instead treat the final sample as a single action and replace its intractable log-
likelihood log pθ(x0) with the ELBO surrogate −LFM(θ), producing an online reward-weighted
regression objective on forward-noised samples (McAllister et al., 2026; Fan et al., 2025; Xue et al.,
2026). Although this gives a biased estimator of the exact policy gradient, it is empirically effec-
tive. Methods derived from other perspectives reduce to the same objective (Zheng et al., 2026;
Bergmeister et al., 2026); Appendix B gives these reductions in a common notation. By decoupling
optimization from sampling, ELBO-based methods can substantially accelerate reward maximiza-
tion relative to trajectory-based methods (Choi et al., 2026).
Distributional discrepancies as training losses. Several few-step distillation methods match the
output distribution of a student generator to that of a pretrained teacher, through the integral reverse-
KL divergence (Luo et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2024a;b), general f-divergences (Xu et al., 2025), or mo-
ment matching (Salimans et al., 2024). Other works match the data distribution directly. Zhou et al.
(2025) train few-step generators from scratch with an MMD-based moment-matching objective, and
De Bortoli et al. (2025) learn the full conditional distribution of clean data given a noisy state with
scoring rules such as the energy score, rather than regressing its mean. FD-loss (Yang et al., 2026)
post-trains generators by minimizing the Fr´echet distance to real data in a representation space.
A.1
MEASURING FIDELITY AND DIVERSITY
Distribution-level fidelity. FID (Heusel et al., 2017) compares generated and real images through
the Fr´echet distance between Gaussian fits to their Inception-v3 features. Its limitations have moti-
vated several alternatives. Stein et al. (2023) find that Inception features align poorly with human
judgments of modern generators and recommend DINOv2 features instead. KID (Bi´nkowski et al.,
2018) and CMMD (Jayasumana et al., 2024) replace FD with MMD, which drops the Gaussian
assumption and admits an unbiased estimator. Precision and recall (Kynk¨a¨anniemi et al., 2019) sep-
arate fidelity from coverage by estimating the support of each distribution with k-nearest-neighbor
balls in feature space.
Diversity. LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) and DreamSim (Fu et al., 2023) compare images through
deep features calibrated to human similarity judgments. LPIPS mainly reflects low-level differences
such as color and texture, whereas DreamSim also captures layout, pose, and semantic content. The
mean pairwise perceptual distance within a set of samples is a common diversity measure in reward
fine-tuning (Domingo-Enrich et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025d;c). The Vendi Score (Friedman & Dieng,
2023) instead measures the effective number of distinct samples, as the exponential of the entropy
of the eigenvalues of the normalized similarity matrix.

D
EXPERIMENT DETAILS
D.1
EVALUATION METRICS
Drift. We measure drift against pref rather than against a fixed real dataset, which separates the
change induced by fine-tuning from the pretrained model’s own mismatch with real data. Computing
FD per prompt and then averaging measures this change within each conditional distribution, rather
than in the marginal over prompts. Its absolute values are far larger than standard FD values and are
meaningful only relative to one another. We compute avg(FD-64) with Inception-v3 and DINOv2
ViT-L/14, and DreamSim diversity with DINO ViT-B/16 and OpenCLIP ViT-B/32.
Held-out metrics. DFR-FD computes its reward with ConvNeXt-V2, SigLIP2, and CLIP ViT-
L/14 features, and DFR-DS with the DINO ViT-B/16 branch of DreamSim. A metric is not held
out for an instance if it is computed with one of that instance’s encoders or a closely related one,
because the instance optimizes a closely related quantity during training. We therefore strike through
DreamSim’s DINO branch and DINOv2 FD, a later model of the same self-supervised family, for
DFR-DS. DreamSim’s OpenCLIP branch remains held out for DFR-FD: it is a ViT-B/32 trained
on different data and fine-tuned for perceptual similarity, not the CLIP ViT-L/14 that DFR-FD uses.
Struckout entries are not counted as wins or losses. DFR-MMD compares SD3.5-M’s own VAE
latents and uses no pretrained feature encoder, so every metric is held out for it.
29

D.2
EVALUATION PROTOCOL
Checkpoint selection. During training, we evaluate the target reward every 10 steps on the task’s
full test prompt set. This curve is used to select checkpoints: when all three seeds possess a check-
point that reaches the threshold within the compute budget, it is considered successful. The full
tables (Appendix D.6) also report configurations that do not reach a threshold: seeds that reach it
are evaluated at their first checkpoint that reaches it and the others at their peak, taking the earliest
on ties. Steps is the mean number of training steps to the threshold over the seeds that reach it, from
which Appendix D.7 derives the speedups.
Evaluation prompts and sampling. From the GenEval test set (“g”; 2,212 prompts), DrawBench
(“d”; 1,000), and the OCR test set (“o”; 1,018), we draw a fixed random subset of 256 prompts
each. Models fine-tuned on GenEval are evaluated on g and d, and models fine-tuned on OCR on o
and d. For every prompt, every model generates 64 images from the same 64 initial-noise seeds, at
512 × 512 resolution with 20 sampling steps and guidance scale 4.5; multi-reward DiffusionNFT is
sampled at its prescribed scale of 1.0. We run the transformer and text encoders in FP16 and decode
with the VAE in FP32.
Statistics. RAM and DFR entries are the mean ± sample SD over three seeds; existing methods
are single checkpoints and are reported without SD. Two configurations are tied on a metric if their
means, as displayed, differ by at most the larger of their two SDs, and otherwise the one with the
better mean wins. In the ImgRwd and PickScore columns of the full tables (Appendix D.6), red
marks a value significantly below SD3.5-M’s by the same criterion; for single-seed methods, we use
the median seed SD of the RAM rows.
D.3
COMPUTE BUDGET
All runs of a task use the same compute: a fixed wall-clock budget of 288 GPU hours for GenEval
and 144 for OCR. The distributional rewards add little time per training step. Measured on this
hardware, DFR-FD and DFR-DS are within 3% of RAM, and DFR-MMD takes approximately 9%
longer, mostly for the pairwise distances within each group and to the reference samples. Within the
same budget, DFR-MMD therefore completes correspondingly fewer steps. Speedups in Table 1
include this overhead.
D.4
HYPERPARAMETERS
DFR coefficients. Each instance adds its group-centered reward rR to the normalized advantage
with coefficient λ (Algorithm 1), so λ is in units of the task advantage. DFR-MMD uses the energy
kernel with exponent b = 1.75 on SD3.5-M’s normalized VAE latents, with M = 8 reference sam-
ples per prompt from pref. DFR-DS uses the DINO branch of DreamSim with distances normalized
to [0, 1]. DFR-FD combines three feature spaces, each measured in units of its own noise floor, so
its raw scale is not interpretable; we instead set λ = ϕ/sw, where ϕ is the fraction of the advantage
spread that the FD reward contributes and sw is calibrated once per task (Appendix C.6.3). The
tables use λ = 0.55 (MMD), ϕ = 0.07 (FD), and λ = 3 (DS) on GenEval, and λ = 1.5, ϕ = 0.09,
and λ = 4 on OCR.
Choice of β. We add DFR at the largest β for which RAM reaches the highest threshold on every
seed. DFR-DS keeps this β: its diversity reward does not measurably slow reward optimization,
so it adds diversity at no cost in speed rather than acting as further regularization. DFR-MMD and
DFR-FD also pull the policy toward pref; on OCR they no longer reach the threshold on every seed
at β = 5, so we use β = 4.

D.9
TRAINING STABILITY ACROSS β
Training is unstable at both ends of the range of β, in different ways (Figure 4). At β = 1, runs
disagree: the seed SDs of FD at the evaluated checkpoints are 3–10× those at β ∈{2, 3} (Table 5),
and the reward dips by up to 0.037 below its running maximum, but every dip recovers and no seed
collapses within the budget. On OCR, the smallest β = 2 likewise has FD seed SDs 4–6× those at
β ∈{4, 5} (Table 6). At β = 4 of GenEval, training collapses: every seed peaks between steps 170
and 240 and then declines. On OCR, larger β plateaus rather than collapses: RAM peaks at 0.950
at β = 8 and 0.923 at β = 12.
