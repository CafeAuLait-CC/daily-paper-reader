Abstract
Diffusion models have become a leading approach in visual generative modeling,
particularly effective in text-to-image and controllable generation. Their main draw-
back is slow inference as it requires many sampling steps. While recent distillation
methods enable one-step text-to-image generation, extending these techniques to
controllable generation is still challenging. Existing controllable models, such
as ControlNet, rely on dual-branch design that make distillation difficult. In this
work, we adopt a simpler strategy. Instead of adding extra conditional branches, we
finetune a selected subset of layers in the original text-to-image diffusion model to
directly enable controllable generation. This results in DeltaControl, a single-flow,
multi-step diffusion model capable of supporting spatial-condition generation while
matching the performance of dual-branch approaches. Building on this teacher
model, we further apply step-distillation to obtain FlashControl, a one-step model
capable of spatial controllable generation with a single forward pass.

Introduction
Diffusion models [33, 5, 15, 14, 40] have become a strong paradigm for both text-to-image generation
and controllable synthesis. In parallel, recent distillation methods [8, 48, 49, 26, 37, 36, 29] have
reduced diffusion sampling to a small number of steps, and one-step generators are now increasingly
competitive with their multi-step teachers. Extending this progress to controllable generation,
however, is substantially less direct. Existing controllable models such as ControlNet and T2I-
Adapter inject spatial guidance through external control pathways on top of a pretrained text-to-image
backbone [51, 27]. These designs are effective in the standard multi-step setting, but our analysis
suggests that they are poorly matched to one-step distillation. When such a teacher is compressed
into a single forward-pass student, the student must absorb both the base generative process and the
control-specific correction into one computation. In practice, this leads to weak control, unstable
optimization, or blurry results, even when the multi-step teacher itself performs well.
This observation motivates the first hypothesis of our work: for one-step controllable generation to
succeed, control must be integrated into the teacher backbone in a form that remains transferable
under distillation, rather than attached through an auxiliary branch. Based on this view, we introduce
DeltaControl, a single-stream controllable teacher designed explicitly for one-step transfer. Instead
of duplicating large portions of the network, DeltaControl adapts a selected subset of parameters in the
original backbone to encode spatial control. We study this design on both UNet and DiT backbones
and find that tuning feed-forward layers provides the most favorable behavior for controllability and
convergence under a matched parameter budget. Our interpretation is straightforward: in a single-
stream architecture, attention layers largely govern global feature mixing, so perturbing them can
induce broad and unstable changes, whereas feed-forward layers act on already mixed representations
and provide a more stable subspace for injecting control. This yields a controllable teacher that is
compact, single-stream, and compatible with standard distillation. More importantly, it distinguishes
our goal from prior single-stream controllers such as RepControlNet [9] and OminiControl [44]: our
objective is not only efficient controllable generation, but a distillation-friendly teacher architecture
whose control mechanism survives compression into a one-step student.
Building such a teacher is only the first step. The next challenge is to distill it into a one-step generator
without obscuring the effect of teacher architecture. Recent methods such as SwiftBrush [29, 8] and
DMD [48, 49] show that score-based distribution matching can transfer a multi-step diffusion model
to a one-step generator efficiently [49, 8]. In this work, we adopt vanilla DMD [49] as the main
distillation objective because it is a simple and widely used score-based formulation that cleanly
isolates the role of the teacher architecture. We leave stronger adversarial variants, such as DMD2
[48], to the appendix. This choice is deliberate: our main claim is that teacher design is the critical
ingredient for one-step controllable distillation, and we therefore avoid introducing extra adversarial
components in the main setting that could confound this comparison.
Even with a suitable teacher and a simple distillation objective, one-step controllable students are
still fragile. Different controls, such as canny edges, depth maps, segmentation masks, and poses,
have very different structures and scales. If we inject them naively, they can disturb the backbone
features and make training unstable. We address this with AutoAdapter. AutoAdapter first aligns
condition features to the hidden-state statistics, then applies token-wise gating to control how strongly
each token is injected. In our framework, AutoAdapter is not just a small add-on. It is the module
that makes different control types usable within one model and keeps early training stable, when the
student is most sensitive to feature mismatch.
A separate issue appears during one-step distillation itself. During late-stage distillation, the student
starts ignoring variations in its input Gaussian noise, causing different noise samples under the same
prompt-condition pair to produce nearly identical outputs. This hurts both diversity and fine detail.
To address this, we introduce a Latent Variance Preservation objective, which encourages the
output to retain a meaningful response to perturbations in the input noise. The role of this term is
specific to one-step diffusion distillation: it preserves stochastic sensitivity in the student and reduces
the tendency toward deterministic collapse. Combined with pure DMD and AutoAdapter, it gives a
training recipe that is stable enough for one-step controllable generation.
Together, these components form a unified view of the problem. DeltaControl addresses the archi-
tectural mismatch between controllable teachers and one-step students. Single stream teacher with
pure DMD loss keeps the distillation objective tied to the spatial condition. AutoAdapter aligns het-
2


erogeneous controls to the backbone feature space. Latent Variance Preservation keeps the one-step
student responsive to noise. Under this formulation, FlashControl is not a loose collection of existing
modules. It is a pipeline in which each component resolves a specific failure mode that appears when
controllable generation is compressed into a single forward pass.
Our contributions are as follows:
• We show that naive one-step distillation fails for dual-branch controllable models, while
single-flow teachers provide a better foundation for one-step controllable generation.
• We introduce DeltaControl, a single-stream controllable teacher that integrates structural
control into the base backbone by adapting a selected subset of layers. Across both UNet
and DiT backbones, we find that feed-forward tuning provides a favorable balance between
controllability, efficiency, and distillation compatibility.
• We show that a DMD-based distillation objective, together with AutoAdapter-based condi-
tion alignment, provides a stable recipe for one-step controllable generation and preserves
spatial faithfulness under structural controls.
• We introduce a Latent Variance Preservation objective for one-step controllable distilla-
tion, which reduces deterministic collapse by maintaining the student’s sensitivity to input
noise, and we demonstrate real-time one-step controllable synthesis across multiple control
modalities within a single model.
• To the best of our knowledge, FlashControl is among the first one-step controllable dis-
tillation frameworks to explicitly build the controllable teacher as a LoRA-compatible
single-stream backbone adaptation, rather than distilling from a duplicated control branch.

Related Work
Diffusion Models [14, 34] have surpassed GAN [11] across a wide range of tasks, including
controllable generation [51, 27], image editing [1, 25], and personalized synthesis [35]. This progress
began with LDM [34] and was further advanced by the DiT architecture [30]. The recent text-to-
image models, SD3 [10] and FLUX [15, 16], achieve impressive results by adopting Rectified Flow
training [20], and Multi-Modal Transformer (MMDiT) backbone [30].
Multi-step Controllable Diffusion Models. Controllable synthesis guides text-to-image diffusion
with structural inputs (e.g., edges, depth, segmentation, pose). T2I-Adapter [27] trains lightweight
adapters on a frozen backbone, while ControlNet [51] adds a parallel control branch and has inspired
many variants, including ControlNet-XS [50], UniControlNet [52], UniControl [31], and AnyControl
[43]. To reduce tuning cost, CTRLORA [47] adapts small parameter subsets of the control branch.
Instead of using dual-branch, some recent works, such as UniCombine [45] and OminiControl [44],
append the conditional inputs to the noisy latent input and denoise them with a LoRA-finetuned
single-branch DiT. These techniques often leverage strong backbones like FLUX and incur a massive
computation cost growth with respect to the number of conditions. Despite strong controllability,
these methods still rely on multi-step sampling, leading to slow inference.
Diffusion Distillation. Diffusion models are slow at inference due to many denoising steps, motivat-
ing distillation to shorten sampling while preserving quality [36, 21]. Consistency Models and LCM
enable few-step sampling but still show a quality gap at one step [41, 22]. ADD/LADD use adversar-
ial losses for 1-4 step distillation but can be unstable [37, 38], while DMD/DMD2 distill from the
teacher score field for one-/few-step generation [49, 48]. SwiftBrush reduces overhead via image-free
score distillation with VSD [28, 46], and SBv2 improves further with stronger initialization, efficient
LoRA, and a clamped-margin CLIP loss, sometimes surpassing the teacher [8].

ControlNet.
Text prompts provide global semantic guidance, but many applications require spatial
control from a condition map c, such as edges, depth, segmentation, or pose. ControlNet [51]
addresses this by attaching an auxiliary control branch to a pretrained text-to-image backbone. The
control branch processes (xt, t, c) and injects condition-dependent residuals into the frozen base
denoiser, usually through zero-initialized layers. This design preserves the pretrained generative
capability while learning spatial alignment.

FlashControl
4.1
Overview
Our objective is one-step (single forward pass) controllable text-to-image generation via distil-
lation, with stable optimization and high-quality spatial condition model. We hypothesize that
one-step controllable generation is substantially harder than one-step text-only generation,
because the student must preserve both visual fidelity and spatial faithfulness within a single network
4


evaluation. In particular, the common dual-branch design used by ControlNet-style methods is not
well matched to one-step distillation, as discussed later in Section 4.2. To address this problem,
we propose FlashControl, a two-stage teacher→student pipeline for real-time, high-quality and
controllable generation.
Stage 1: distillation-friendly conditional teacher (DeltaControl in Section 4.3). One-step text-
to-image distillation methods typically rely on a single-branch teacher architecture. Motivated by
this observation, we train a single-branch DeltaControl teacher, which enables spatial signal control
by fine-tuning all feedforward layers from the base text-to-image diffusion model. To better handle
heterogeneous control modalities, we introduce AutoAdapter, a lightweight conditioning module.
Stage 2: one-step student (FlashControl) via distillation in Section 4.4. We distill the single-
stream DeltaControl teacher into a one-step generator using Distribution Matching Distillation
(DMD) [49, 48]. In addition, we deliberately drop adversarial distillation, since controllable
generation requires realism under a spatial constraint rather than unconstrained image realism. Finally,
to prevent late-stage collapse in one-step distillation, we introduce Latent Variance Preservation
(LSP), which maintain the output variation and prevents deterministic mapping.
4.2
From Dual-branch to efficient single-stream teacher.
Is dual-branch ControlNet optimal for one-step distillation?
ControlNet [51] is the most com-
mon framework for multi-step controllable image generation. A natural approach for one-step
controllable T2I is therefore to (i) train a ControlNet teacher and (ii) apply a one-step distillation
method (e.g., DMD [48, 49]). We experiment with distilling dual-branch models, including Con-
trolNet and T2I-Adapter, into onestep generators using DMD. However, the results show that this
straightforward pipeline results in low-quality and blurry results, as in Fig. 4 and Table 5, even when
the multi-step teacher itself performs well.
We attribute this failure to an architectural mismatch between dual-branch teacher and single-step
student. ControlNet decomposes controllable generation into two coupled components: a base
denoiser and an auxiliary control branch that injects condition-dependent residuals. In one-step
distillation, the student must simultaneously approximate the base denoising dynamics and the
control-induced correction within a single forward pass. This entangled objective leads to unstable
optimization: the student either collapses to the base model (weak control) or overfits noisy control
residuals (degrading fidelity). This behavior is consistent with recent observations in distilling large
dual-branch architectures, where additional stabilization tricks are required and training may still
collapse (e.g., gradient explosion) [7]. We further verify in Fig. 4 that simply “simplifying” the
objective by freezing either the base denoiser or the control branch does not resolve the issue. Overall,
these results suggest that ControlNet’s dual-branch architecture is not well suited as a teacher for
one-step controllable distillation.
Why DeltaControl? From RepControlNet to a unified single-branch teacher.
This analysis
suggests a simple principle: a strong one-step controllable student should be distilled from a single-
branch controllable teacher, where control is integrated into the backbone rather than produced by an
external branch. RepControlNet [9] is a representative approach in this direction. It reparameterizes
control by replicating parts of the backbone and learning condition-dependent residual transformations
that can be merged into a single network, removing the need for a dual control branch during inference.
We identify several limitations of RepControlNet. First, it replicates parts of the backbone during
training following on a tedious replicate-and-merge strategy, leading to unnecessary increase in
memory and computation cost. Second, it does not investigate which layers should be adapted
to effectively incorporate control signals. Instead, it adopts a dense “replicate-and-tune” strategy
that duplicates and finetunes many layers without analyzing their roles in controllability. Third,
RepControlNet is evaluated only on the UNet backbone, leaving open the question of whether the
approach generalizes to transformer-based architectures such as DiT. Finally, RepControlNet focuses
primarily on a single-condition setting and evaluates only a limited set of control modalities. This
restricts its applicability in scenarios where multiple heterogeneous control signals must be handled
within a unified framework.
In contrast, our method addresses all four limitations. We do not replicate network components but
directly finetune some of them, offer more efficient training and memory usage. We perform an
5


analysis to identify which layers are most effective for adapting control signals, design a strategy
that generalizes across both UNet and transformer backbones, and develop a unified controllable
framework capable of handling diverse control modalities within a single model.
Comparison to other single-branch approaches.
Recent methods, such as OminiControl [44]
and UniCombine [45], append the conditions to the noisy latent input, offering a cleaner DiT-based
single-stream formulation for versatile multi-step control. However, their goal is efficient controllable
generation itself, not the construction of a teacher whose control interface can be faithfully compressed
into a one-step student. In contrast, DeltaControl is designed around this stricter requirement: it
uses selective backbone adaptation to integrate control directly into layers that remain stable under
transfer, and combines this with AutoAdapter so that the student inherits a normalized, reliability-
aware control interface during distillation. As a result, our single-stream design is not merely lighter;
it is more friendly to distill, as will be showed in Section 5.
4.3
Stage 1: DeltaControl - Multi-step Conditional Model
4.3.1
Efficient Finetuning Strategy.
Inspired by RepControlNet, DeltaControl removes the dual-branch architecture and instead fine-tunes
only a selected subset of parameters in the base T2I model. To determine which layers are most
suitable for learning control signals, we conduct a series of experiments on two widely used diffusion
backbones: UNet and DiT. For DiT, which consists of Transformer blocks combining self-attention,
cross-attention, and feed-forward (FFN) layers, we evaluate three finetuning configurations with
the same number of trainable parameters as vanilla ControlNet: (i) attention-only, (ii) FFN-only,
and (iii) a mixed subset of attention and FFN layers. For UNet, which contains both Transformer
and ResNet blocks, we examine three analogous variants: (i) ResNet - selecting a subset of ResNet
blocks with the same number of trainable parameters as ControlNet, (ii) Attn - replacing part of the
selected ResNet blocks with attention layers, and (iii) FFN - replacing part of the ResNet subset with
feed-forward layers. We use Stable Diffusion 2.1 [33] as the UNet-based model and PixArt-α [5] as
the DiT-based model. During training, only the selected subset of parameters is fine-tuned. Control
signals are injected using a simple condition embedding, implemented as a learnable MLP, and we
evaluate controllability using the Canny edge condition.
As shown in Fig. 5, finetuning FFN layers consistently yields the largest improvements in
adapting to new conditioning signals across both architectures, compared to finetuning attention
layers alone. Notably, this strategy consistently outperforms ControlNet in both UNet and DiT
backbones across all training stages. We hypothesize that this effect arises because, in a single-flow
architecture, the self- and cross-attention modules already align textual prompts with image features,
while the FFN layers mainly transform and refine the combined representations. In contrast,
modifying attention layers changes the global mixing patterns of features, where small parameter
updates can lead to disproportionately large and potentially destabilizing effects.
We illustrate the architectural differences between ControlNet and DeltaControl in Fig. 2. Instead of
training an additional control branch, DeltaControl achieves controllability by fine-tuning a small
subset of parameters in the base model, significantly reducing overhead compared to ControlNet
and RepControlNet. As shown in Tab.4, this design requires fewer trainable parameters, leading to
lower GPU memory usage and faster training. At inference time, DeltaControl runs a single-branch
that natively incorporates control signals, eliminating the need for a separate control branch. The
selected parameters can also be adapted with LoRA, preserving the original model’s generative
capability. Overall, this design improves both training and inference efficiency. In addition, we
introduce AutoAdapter, which enables effective controllable generation.
4.3.2
AutoAdapter.
A direct addition of raw control embeddings to the model’s hidden states is fragile in the multi-
condition setting: edge, depth, segmentation, and pose signals differ substantially in scale, sparsity,
and reliability. These differences can cause either vanishing control signals or over-dominant
perturbations. To address this, we introduce AutoAdapter, a lightweight module that converts
incoming control tokens into hidden-state-compatible residuals prior to backbone injection. Its
primary role is twofold: it improves the quality of DeltaControl under heterogeneous conditions and
it provides a stable control interface for the alternating student-teacher updates during distillation.

Latent Sensitivity Preservation (LSP).
While score distillation provides an efficient formulation
for one-step generation, we observe a distinct optimization instability specific to controllable distil-
lation: the student rapidly becomes overly mode-seeking. It overfits to a narrow teacher trajectory,
loses sensitivity to the input Gaussian noise, and collapses to a near-deterministic mapping for any
given prompt-condition pair (y, c). Qualitatively, this manifests as late-stage stylized collapse and
oversmoothing; quantitatively, it produces sharp, disruptive spikes in both student and LoRA losses
during late training.

Conclusion
FlashControl shows that single-step, controllable image generation is possible with a streamlined,
single-branch design. It preserves much of the teacher’s controllability while simplifying both training
and inference. In evaluations, it performs on par with traditional two-branch methods. For future
work, we can investigate simultaneous multi-condition composition (e.g., combining depth and pose
in a single input).

AutoAdapter benefits distillation.
We investigate whether AutoAdapter actively benefits the one-step distillation process, rather than
merely strengthening the Stage-1 teacher. We compare the optimization trajectories of the plain
single-stream baseline, SS + AutoAdapter, and SS + AA + LSP under identical distillation condi-
tions. Visually, AutoAdapter establishes recognizable structure much earlier in training and yields
consistently cleaner conditional outputs Fig. 7. Quantitatively, it produces lower and substantially
smoother loss curves prior to the late-stage instability region Fig. 6. These findings confirm that
AutoAdapter transcends simple Stage-1 quality improvements: by providing a statistically well-
conditioned control interface, it makes the alternating Stage-2 updates inherently easier and more
stable to optimize.
B
LSP benefits distillation.
We examine the training dynamics to isolate the stabilizing effect of LSP. As shown in 6, distillation
without LSP exhibits a pronounced spike in both student and LoRA losses. This quantitative instability
manifests visually in 6, where the ablated model drifts into severe degradation and oversaturationin
training. Conversely, the inclusion of LSP yields flat, stable loss curves and preserves coherent visual
structure throughout the same training range. These findings substantiate our core theoretical claim:
LSP functions strictly as a collapse-prevention mechanism. Rather than acting as a generic diversity
heuristic, it actively preserves the student’s sensitivity to input noise, successfully preventing the
model from degenerating into a near-deterministic mapping once the spatial control signal dominates.

Selective parameter fine-tuning experiment.
In Fig. 5, we compare ControlNet with our Delta-
Control variants on both a U-Net–based model (Stable Diffusion 2.1) and a DiT-based model
(PixArt-α).
PixArt-α has 28 transformer blocks; for each DeltaControl variant:
• Attn: fine-tune all parameters in the self-attention and cross-attention layers of all 28 blocks.
• FFN: fine-tune all feedforward layers in all 28 blocks.
• Attn+FFN: fine-tune a subset of parameters taken from both the Attn and FFN variants,
again across all 28 blocks.
Stable Diffusion 2.1 has 4 down blocks, 1 mid block, and 4 up blocks; for each DeltaControl variant:
• ResNet: fine-tune all convolution layers in the ResNet sub-blocks of all down blocks, the
mid block, and the first two up blocks.
• FFN: fine-tune all convolution layers in the ResNet sub-blocks of all down blocks and the
mid block, plus all feedforward layers in the transformer blocks.
• Attn: fine-tune all convolution layers in the ResNet sub-blocks of all down blocks, the
mid block, and the first up block, plus all self-attention and cross-attention layers in the
transformer blocks.
We choose the number of tuned layers so that the percentage of trainable parameters matches
ControlNet for a fair comparison. All models are trained with a single Canny edge condition: we (i)
encode the condition with a VAE, (ii) map it with a small trainable embedding module, and (iii) add
it to the intermediate latent at the network head in the same way for all variants.

Limitations
FlashControl has several limitations. First, although the final student is fast at inference, the method
still requires a strong pretrained backbone and a two-stage teacher–student training pipeline, which
introduces non-trivial training cost. Second, our experiments focus on 512 × 512 images and six
common spatial controls, so we do not claim direct generalization to higher-resolution synthesis,
video, or specialized domains. Third, FlashControl currently handles one control type at a time. Our
results suggest that training with more condition types under the same budget can slow convergence
and slightly degrade quality, so robust multi-condition composition remains future work. Finally, like
other controllable image generators, fast one-step inference may lower the cost of creating misleading
visual content; responsible release should include usage restrictions, provenance tools, and safety
filters.

AutoAdapter: Detailed Design
In this section, we provide a more detailed explanation of the AutoAdapter module introduced in
Section 4.2 of the main paper. Recall that AutoAdapter is a lightweight head that converts a spatial
control input (e.g., a Canny edge map or a depth map) into token features that can be safely injected
into the single-flow backbone of DeltaControl.
Overall data flow.
Given a single control image cin and backbone hidden states h ∈RB×N×D at
the network head, AutoAdapter proceeds in three high-level stages:
1. Encode the condition image into a latent embedding c0 using the same VAE encoder as the
base diffusion model, then project it to the same dimension as h.
2. Match the distribution of the condition tokens to the hidden-state tokens using the distribution
matching module (whitening and re-scaling, as defined in Eqs. (5)–(7) of the main paper).
3. Modulate each condition token with a token-wise uncertainty gate, and add the gated
condition features to the backbone hidden states.
This design lets the model interpret control inputs in the same feature space as the backbone, while
automatically deciding where and how strongly each control token should affect the current latent.
We provide pseudo-code for the AutoAdapter module in Algorithm 1.
Distribution matching in practice.
We (i) VAE-encode cin to c0, (ii) whiten per-channel over
tokens as in Eq. (5), (iii) feed the whitened tokens, the current hidden-state tokens, and the timestep
embedding ϕ(t) into a small MLP to predict per-channel scale and shift parameters (s, b) as in Eq. (6),
and (iv) re-scale and shift to produce calign as in Eq. (7).
Intuitively, this module performs an AdaIN-style alignment between the condition stream and the
backbone stream. Without this step, directly adding raw condition tokens to h can either drown out
the original features (if the condition is too large) or make the control nearly invisible (if it is too
small). The distribution matching module keeps the condition in a “safe” range where gradients
remain stable across timesteps and conditions.
Token-wise uncertainty gating.
Even after distribution matching, not every condition token should
affect the hidden state equally. For example, a poorly extracted edge map or an occluded region
should be trusted less than a clean, well-aligned region. AutoAdapter therefore applies a token-wise
gate gi to each condition token calign,i before adding it to the corresponding hidden token hi.
The gate gi is computed from three signals:
1. A similarity term between the hidden token and the aligned condition token (high similarity
⇒gate opens).
2. A time-dependent factor time(t) that encourages stronger control at early denoising steps
and weaker control near the final steps.
3. An uncertainty term produced by a small MLP on calign,i, which decreases the gate when the
condition token appears noisy or unreliable.
These three terms are combined into a single scalar gate and squashed by a sigmoid, as described in
Sec. 4.2.2. The gated condition token is then added to the hidden state:
hi ←hi + gi · calign,i.
In regions where the condition aligns well with the current latent (e.g., edges or poses that agree with
the generated structure), gi is close to 1 and the control strongly influences the hidden state. When
the condition is inconsistent or noisy, gi shrinks toward 0, effectively turning the control off in those
tokens.
