# Denoising as Projection: Constrained Optimization with Gradient-Guided Diffusion 

## Runyu Zhang 1∗, Jiawei Zhang 2∗, Gioele Zardini 1, Saurabh Amin 1, and Asuman Ozdaglar 2*†‡

## September 1, 2026 

Abstract 

Diffusion models are increasingly used not only for sampling from learned data distributions, but also for generat-ing samples that optimize task-specific objectives. A common approach is to guide the reverse diffusion process using gradients of an external objective. However, when the data distribution is supported on a structured feasible set, such as a manifold or a constraint set, gradient guidance can move samples away from the learned data geometry. In this paper, we study a simple projected-gradient-guided diffusion update based on the observation that the Stein denoising operator can act as an approximate projection onto the data geometry. The proposed update incorporates the objec-tive gradient inside the denoising step, yielding an inference-time method that uses only a pretrained denoiser and gradient evaluations. We analyze this update as an inexact projected-gradient method for constrained optimization over learned feasible geometries. Our theory covers three settings: linear manifolds, compact convex feasible sets, and compact Riemannian submanifolds. In all these settings, we prove descent and finite-time convergence guaran-tees. Numerical experiments support the theoretical interpretation and illustrate how the proposed update balances objective descent with preservation of the learned geometry. 

# 1 Introduction 

Diffusion models have emerged as a powerful class of generative models, achieving state-of-the-art performance in image synthesis, inverse problems, scientific design, and decision-making tasks [35, 15, 38, 36]. Their success stems in large part from score-based denoising dynamics, which can model complex high-dimensional data distributions. In many applications, however, the goal is not merely to sample realistic data, but to generate samples that are also good with respect to a task-specific objective. Examples include inverse problems [6], trajectory optimization [16], molecular generation [43], control [3], and other settings where samples should remain consistent with a learned data distribution while achieving low cost or high reward. A common strategy for steering diffusion models toward desirable samples is guidance. Classifier guidance and classifier-free guidance modify the reverse diffusion dynamics using auxiliary information so that generated samples better satisfy a target condition [10, 14]. More generally, when a differentiable objective function f : Rn → R is available, one can guide sampling by incorporating the objective gradient into the reverse dynamics. This suggests viewing guided diffusion as a method for solving the constrained optimization problem min x∈X f (x), where the fea-sible set X ⊆ Rn is implicitly represented by the support or high-density region of a learned data distribution. This perspective is particularly relevant when the diffusion model serves as a generative prior for feasible designs, trajec-tories, or decisions, and the objective encodes a task-specific performance criterion. Gradient guidance is attractive in this setting because it can be applied at inference time and requires only evaluations of ∇f . Recent work has begun to study guided diffusion from an optimization perspective [11]. A key difficulty, however, is that directly injecting an gradient into the reverse dynamics may disrupt the feasibility structure encoded by the generative model. This difficulty is fundamentally geometric. Depending on the application, X may be a low-dimensional data manifold, a closed convex constraint set, a set of physically admissible designs, or a family of dynamically feasible and collision-free trajectories. Consequently, naive gradient guidance may reduce the objective value while moving the iterate away from X , thereby degrading sample quality, physical validity, or constraint satisfaction. This issue is particularly important in applications such as diffusion-based robotic planning, where the learned trajectory distribution captures useful behavioral and dynamical priors, but objective guidance can produce infeasible or unsafe trajectories unless an additional feasibility mechanism is introduced [44, 34, 24, 25]. Several lines of work have addressed this issue by modifying the guidance or sampling procedure, but often at the cost of additional sample, computational or modeling complexity. For example, Kharitenko et al. [19] constructs explicit geometric primitives that rely on computing the Jacobian of the score function, which can be more computa-tionally costly; Guo et al. [11] rely on repeated batch generation and objective linearization. Closely related denoiser-as-projection analysis [22] establishes recovery guarantees for linear inverse problems with a quadratic data-fitting ob-jective under an RIP-type condition, rather than general constrained optimization. Beyond these, manifold-aware guid-ance [5, 13, 7, 45], constrained-diffusion methods [4, 23, 18, 12], and reward-alignment approaches [8, 9, 31, 29, 20, 1] address related problems, but may rely on reward-specific fine-tuning, repeated sampling and auxiliary models; de-tailed comparisons are deferred to Appendix A. These existing approaches raise a natural question: can a much simpler update (without outer-loop generation, score-Jacobian evaluation, or model fine-tuning), using only one gradient evaluation and one pretrained Stein denois-ing call per step, already provide optimization guarantees? 

We answer this question by leveraging the fact that the Stein denoising operator can approximate projection onto the learned data geometry. This suggests a natural way to incorporate objective information: we apply the denoiser af-ter the gradient update. Intuitively, the gradient step promotes descent of the objective, while the subsequent denoising step pulls the iterate back toward the feasible geometry. When the denoiser is close to the true projection, the resulting update behaves like an inexact projected-gradient method. 

Contributions. First, we introduce Denoising-Corrected Gradient (DCG) Guidance, a simple inference-time method that applies an objective-gradient step followed by one call to a pretrained denoiser at each reverse diffusion step. DCG operates along a single reverse trajectory and requires neither repeated batch generation or score-model adapta-tion [11], nor differentiation of the denoiser [19]. Second, we show that the Stein posterior-mean denoiser can act as the geometric correction operator underlying DCG. We establish quantitative approximation bounds between the denoiser and the relevant projection operator for linear Gaussian, compact convex sets, and compact smooth submanifolds settings. These results allow us to interpret the reverse diffusion dynamics as a time-varying inexact projected-gradient method. Third, we derive finite-time convergence guarantees that reflect the geometry and objective class. For linear-Gaussian data and strongly convex objectives, both the projected objective error and the normal component contracts geometrically. For compact convex feasible sets, we establish geometric convergence for strongly convex objectives, and an O(1 /√T ) best-iterate bound for the nonconvex objectives. Finally, for compact smooth submanifolds, we 2obtain an O(1 /√T ) best-iterate bound for local convergence. Thus, the analysis covers linear subspaces, possibly nonsmooth convex feasible sets, and smooth manifolds within a unified approximate-projection framework. Table 1 summarizes the theoretical distinction from the closest optimization-oriented approaches. Finally, we provide numerical experiments (in Appendix B) on synthetic constrained optimization, motion plan-ning, and standard diffusion-based reinforcement learning benchmarks from D4RL. Across these settings, DCG achieves a consistently favorable tradeoff between objective value and constraint satisfaction, outperforming standard gradient guidance and matching or improving upon closely related optimization-oriented diffusion baselines. 

Limitations. Our analysis assumes an exact Stein posterior-mean denoiser and that the learned data support faithfully represents the feasible set. Learned-score errors can be incorporated through additional projection-error terms, but a full treatment of model misspecification is left for future work. We also focus on deterministic DDIM dynamics under a variance-exploding diffusion with an exponentially decaying noise schedule. Extending the theory to stochastic samplers, other diffusion parameterizations, and more general noise schedules requires tracking the corresponding time-varying relaxation and denoising errors.

# 7 Conclusion 

We introduced Denoising-Corrected Gradient Guidance, a simple inference-time method that alternates an objective-gradient step with the ordinary denoising operation of a pretrained diffusion model. Our central observation is that the Stein posterior-mean denoiser acts as an approximate projection onto the support of the data distribution. This con-nects DCG directly to inexact projected-gradient optimization. Building on this connection, we established projection-approximation bounds and finite-time optimization guarantees for linear subspaces, compact convex sets, and compact smooth submanifolds. Experiments on synthetic constrained problems, trajectory tracking, and D4RL benchmarks show that DCG consistently improves the balance between objective value and feasibility compared with direct gradi-ent guidance, while remaining competitive with more geometry-intensive baselines. 10 References

# A Related Works 

This appendix provides a more detailed comparison between the our DCG algorithm studied in this paper and related approaches for geometry-preserving guidance, constrained diffusion, reward alignment, and score-based optimization. 

Gradient guidance from an optimization perspective. A closely related approach studies gradient guidance as an optimization procedure while accounting for the structure learned by the diffusion model [11]. Rather than applying the nonlinear objective gradient directly at each noisy state, the method operates through an outer loop. At each iteration, it generates a batch of samples, evaluates the objective gradient at their mean, and uses this gradient to construct a locally linearized objective for the next round of guided generation. The guidance is applied through the model’s predicted clean endpoint, which helps align the update with the learned data structure. With a fixed score model, the resulting sequence of distribution means can be interpreted as optimizing a data-regularized objective. The adaptive variant further fine-tunes the score model using newly generated samples, allowing this regularization to diminish over successive iterations. The main distinction is therefore algorithmic as well as conceptual. Their method handles a nonlinear objective through repeated batch-based linearizations and full rounds of guided generation, with each guided step requiring differentiation through the clean-sample prediction. The adaptive variant additionally requires repeated model updates. In contrast, our method requires no outer-loop linearization, target reward value, batch generation, or differentiation through the denoiser. Accordingly, we analyze the method as an inexact projected-gradient algorithm for constrained optimization over X , rather than as a guided sampling procedure whose distributional mean follows a regularized optimization iteration. 13 Score-based and denoising-based manifold optimization. Landing with the Score is the closest work to our Riemannian-manifold analysis [19]. It considers optimization over a data manifold specified implicitly by a data dis-tribution and shows that a learned score and its Jacobian can approximate geometric operations such as nearest-point and tangent-space projections. It then uses these approximations to construct Denoising Landing Flow and Denoising Riemannian Gradient Descent, with guarantees for approximate manifold adherence and a small Riemannian gradient norm. Despite the shared geometric motivation, the two approaches use the diffusion model differently. Landing with the Score treats a diffusion-trained score network as a source of geometric information for building a standalone Rieman-nian optimizer: the score-derived denoising map approximates projection onto the manifold, while its Jacobian approx-imates projection onto the tangent space. The resulting algorithms explicitly assemble these operations into landing or Riemannian-gradient updates and therefore require score derivatives or Jacobian-vector products. Our method, by contrast, remains a guided-diffusion procedure. It does not separately estimate tangent spaces, construct Riemannian gradients, or differentiate through the denoiser. Our analysis shows that this simple guidance-and-denoising update it-self behaves like a relaxed projected-gradient or retraction-type step when the denoiser approximates the nearest-point projection. This makes the method directly compatible with pretrained diffusion samplers while requiring only ordi-nary denoising evaluations. We also cover compact convex feasible sets in addition to smooth manifolds, connecting the same update to projected-gradient optimization beyond the Riemannian setting. 

Reward alignment. Reward alignment updates a generative model toward a reward-specific distribution. DRaFT [8] backpropagates differentiable rewards through the sampling chain, whereas PRDP [9] uses reward-difference re-gression for black-box rewards. Tilt Matching [31] and Iterative Tilting [29] instead learn reward-tilted velocity or score fields without differentiating the reward through the sampling trajectory. Thus, although their training objectives differ, these methods all require a reward-specific training or distribution-matching stage. DAS [20] uses Sequential Monte Carlo with multiple particles, resampling, and repeated reward evaluations. Adap-tive Diffusion Guidance [1] learns a time- and state-dependent guidance scale by solving a stochastic optimal-control problem, while Meta Flow Maps [32] require precomputing an auxiliary map to estimate reward-tilted value-function gradients. In contrast, our method requires neither auxiliary training nor Monte Carlo estimation, using only one objective-gradient evaluation and one denoising call per step. 

Guidance for inverse problems and manifold constraints. Standard inverse-problem solvers combine reverse dif-fusion with measurement-based corrections [39, 17, 37], but generally do not guarantee manifold preservation. MCG and diffusion posterior sampling apply guidance through the Tweedie estimate bx0(xt) via ∇xt f (bx0(xt)) [5, 6]; this requires differentiating through the score network, similar in spirit to Landing with the Score . MPGD [13] avoids this score-Jacobian computation by guiding directly in the estimated clean space, and uses an auxiliary autoencoder to enforce manifold-compatible updates; its guarantees rely on idealized linear-manifold and perfect-autoencoder as-sumptions. DiffStateGrad [45] instead estimates a low-rank subspace by SVD and projects the guidance onto it, but provides no theoretical guarantee that this estimated subspace coincides with the true data manifold. CFG++ [7] modifies classifier-free guidance through interpolation of conditional and unconditional predictions and unconditional renoising, with its manifold-preservation guarantee focused on the locally linear setting. DMPlug [42] parameterizes the solution as x = R(zT ) for a deterministic DDIM sampler and optimizes the initial noise zT ; however, each up-date requires backpropagation through the unrolled sampler, making the method computationally expensive for long reverse trajectories. 

Denoising as approximate projection. The closest conceptual precedent for interpreting denoising as projection is [30]. That work models a denoiser through a relative projection-error condition and interprets deterministic diffu-sion sampling as approximate gradient descent on the squared distance to the data support. Its convergence analysis therefore concerns the restoration dynamics of the diffusion sampler itself. Our analysis introduces an additional task objective f . We consequently establish constrained optimality and stationarity guarantees, rather than convergence only toward the learned data geometry. 

Constrained and projected diffusion models. A line of work incorporates explicitly specified constraints into dif-fusion models, assuming access to constraint functions or corresponding correction mechanisms. Projected Diffusion Models [4] consider sample-level feasibility constraints and enforce them during inference through explicit projection. 14 Mirror Diffusion Models [26] similarly assume access to the feasible geometry, but handle known convex constraint sets by mapping them to an unconstrained dual space through a mirror map and training diffusion in the transformed coordinates, with feasibility enforced by the inverse map. Related constrained-training approaches [23] instead in-corporate problem-specific constraint violations into diffusion training. Another distinct line of research considers constraints at the distribution level : Constrained Diffusion Models via Dual Training optimizes the generated distri-bution subject to prescribed distributional requirements [18], while Primal-Dual Inference targets entropy-regularized distribution optimization under average constraints using a dual-conditioned score model and inference-time dual up-dates [12]. In all these settings, the constraints themselves are explicitly available. Our setting is different: the feasible set X is represented only implicitly by a pretrained diffusion model. Rather than assuming a constraint function or projection oracle, we show when the fixed Stein denoiser itself approximates projection onto this learned feasible geometry and use it as the correction step in projected-gradient optimization. 

Constraint-aware diffusion models. A growing line of work incorporates constraints into diffusion models, with an important distinction being where and how the constraints enter . Projected Diffusion Models [4] consider ex-plicit, sample-level feasibility constraints and enforce them during inference through explicit projection. Related constrained-training approaches [23] instead inject problem-specific trajectory constraints, such as collision avoidance and dynamics, into diffusion training through constraint-violation penalties. [18] considers a different, distribution-level setting, where the generated distribution is optimized subject to requirements expressed through desired reference distributions using a Lagrangian dual formulation. Primal-Dual Inference [12] further studies distribution optimiza-tion under average constraints, training a dual-conditioned score model and updating the dual variables alongside the samples during reverse diffusion. Most closely related to our setting, DiffOPT [21] also considers an unknown fea-sible set learned from feasible data, but its optimization result concentrates on feasible points that are already strict local minima of the unconstrained objective, and therefore does not cover general constrained optima arising from the geometry or boundary of the feasible set. In contrast, our feasible set X is encoded only implicitly by the pretrained diffusion model , and our analysis covers general constrained optima, including the active-constraint regime where the unconstrained gradient need not vanish. 

Diffusion-based robotic motion planning. An important application of explicit-constraint diffusion methods is robotic motion planning, where diffusion models provide trajectory priors while constraints such as collision avoid-ance, dynamics, and safety can often be evaluated explicitly [16, 2]. Existing methods therefore augment diffusion sampling with control-barrier or control-Lyapunov mechanisms [44, 28], constraint-based search [34], or explicit pro-jection and constrained-optimization steps [24, 25]. These methods use externally specified constraints to restore or certify feasibility. DCG addresses the complementary setting in which feasible trajectory structure is instead learned implicitly from demonstrations: after an objective-gradient step, the pretrained denoiser itself serves as the feasibility-correction operator, without requiring an explicit model or projection of the learned trajectory set.

Figure 1 compares PDG guidance (baseline) and DCG guidance (ours) on three reference trajectories. Here the constraint violation is the ℓ2 norm of the double-integrator feasibility residual ∥Ax − b∥2. In each case, PDG produces a planned trajectory that closely follows the reference, but the corresponding rollout deviates substantially once the controls are executed through the double-integrator dynamics. This mismatch indicates that PDG optimizes tracking in state space without enforcing dynamic consistency, so low tracking error in the planned solution does not translate into reliable closed-loop behavior. In contrast, DCG yields planned trajectories that remain consistent with their rollouts: the executed trajectory nearly coincides with the guided-diffusion plan across all three settings. This comes at the cost of slightly higher tracking error relative to the reference, but it ensures that the recovered solution is dynamically feasible. The convergence plots reinforce this distinction. PDG rapidly drives down the objective, yet its constraint violation remains large throughout denoising, whereas DCG converges to solutions with near-zero constraint violation while approaching the optimal objective. Together, these results show that dynamic consistency is the key advantage of DCG: it recovers plans that can actually be executed, rather than reference-fitting trajectories that violate the system dynamics.

We conjecture that repeatedly using only the smallest-noise denoiser introduces a persistent finite-noise projection bias, causing conver-gence to a biased stationary point. In contrast, DCG achieves the best overall optimality–feasibility balance in both tests. It attains substantially better objective values than DRGD while maintaining much smaller constraint violation than PDG, and its performance is comparatively robust to the choice of guidance scale.

Baselines. We compare DCG with three baselines using the same pretrained diffusion model and DDIM sampling procedure. Monte Carlo denotes unguided diffusion sampling: it measures the tracking performance obtained by drawing directly from the learned trajectory distribution without using the reference cost. PDG applies standard gradient guidance by directly incorporating the tracking-cost gradient into each reverse-diffusion step. It can strongly reduce the cost of the generated state sequence, but does not explicitly account for whether the resulting states and actions remain dynamically consistent. DRGD is the denoising-based Riemannian-gradient method of [19], which uses score or denoising information to construct the geometric operations required for optimization over the learned trajectory manifold. In contrast, DCG directly applies a tracking-gradient step followed by the pretrained denoising map. All methods are evaluated using 100 generated trajectories for each reference. For the qualitative visualization, we show the Monte Carlo sample with the top-4 lowest planned tracking cost among the 100 generated candidates in Figure 3. 

Results. Table 2 reveals a substantial difference between optimizing the planned trajectory and producing a trajec-tory that remains effective after execution. Unguided Monte Carlo sampling generally has low dynamic-feasibility error because it remains close to the training distribution, but it does not reliably track the specified references. PDG achieves very low planned costs, yet much of this apparent improvement disappears when its actions are rolled out through the true dynamics. This discrepancy is also visible in Figure 3: the trajectories planned by PDG closely follow the references, while their corresponding executed trajectories can deviate substantially. DCG provides the strongest overall combination of executable tracking and dynamic consistency. As reported in Table 2, it achieves the lowest rollout cost and dynamic-feasibility error among the guided methods on both random references. The improvement is especially pronounced for Random Trajectory 2, where DCG reduces the rollout cost from 3.8825 for PDG and 1.2894 for DRGD to 0.1846 . In the Circle setting, DCG achieves the lowest dynamic-feasibility error among the guided methods, while its rollout cost is slightly higher than that of DRGD. Thus, DRGD is generally comparable to or slightly worse than DCG, whereas DCG performs more consistently across the three references. The representative samples in Figure 3 further show that DCG produces planned trajectories whose track-ing improvement is largely retained after execution. Together, these results demonstrate that DCG effectively steers trajectories toward the reference while preserving the learned relationship between states and controls. 

## B.4 Diffuser Benchmarks 

To verify that our algorithm generalizes to larger-scale and more complex dynamical systems, we further evaluate DCG on standard MuJoCo offline-control benchmarks using a Diffuser-style planning pipeline. Similar to the unicycle setting, the diffusion model is trained on offline trajectories and generates candidate state–action sequences through reverse denoising, while gradient guidance biases the generated plans toward higher predicted return. The resulting action sequence is then executed in the environment, and performance is evaluated using normalized return. We compare our proposed DCG method with the classical PDG baseline across Hopper-medium-v2, Walker-medium-v2, and HalfCheetah-medium-v2. All results are reported over 150 evaluation episodes. PDG and DCG are evaluated using the same initial states and environment seeds. For each task, we tune the PDG and DCG guidance scale separately. As shown in Table 3, DCG achieves slightly higher normalized returns on all three tasks. We would also like to note that these experiments are intended primarily to validate that DCG can be integrated seamlessly into an existing Diffuser-based control pipeline and transferred to related benchmark tasks. Although the improvements are consistent, they remain modest in magnitude.
