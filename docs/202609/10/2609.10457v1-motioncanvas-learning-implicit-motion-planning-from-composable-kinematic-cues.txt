ABSTRACT
Professional character animation requires both natural motion and precise, versatile
control. For example, it is common for the creators to define the timing of a
specified action, to control the motion range of the character’s arm swing, and the
route the character walks through, like specifying various kinematic motion cues
on a “motion canvas”. This motivates us to propose MOTIONCANVAS, a model
that supports cue-conditioned implicit motion planning to faithfully and coherently
connect all cues, dense or sparse, full or partial, into one full-body motion sequence.
Specifically, MOTIONCANVAS represents heterogeneous kinematic cues on a
shared motion canvas, where position and rotation values are specified across body
joints and time. A shared flow-matching model generates motion conditioned on
this canvas, with optional language and input motion; cue imputation keeps the
specified canvas values fixed in both training and sampling. To learn coherent
completion across different cue sets, we train with a compositional cue sampler
that varies when cues are applied, which positions or rotations are specified, and
how they are combined. Together, these designs enable a single generator to

INTRODUCTION
Human motion generation is becoming a foundation technology for games, film, virtual avatars, and
embodied simulation. In production, however, generative quality matters only when motions also
obey precise creative and technical requirements. Text-to-motion offers semantic expressiveness
but limited numerical control (Tevet et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2024; Jiang et al.,
2023): a caption can describe an action, but does not fix its path coordinates or joint orientations at
designated frames. General-purpose generation must therefore combine a strong motion prior with
precise control over supplied kinematic values.
We organize kinematic control into temporal cues, spatial cues, and their composition. Temporal
cues anchor poses or retained motion segments at chosen frames; spatial cues prescribe global
paths and facing or local joint positions and rotations. Each can apply at isolated instants or over
extended intervals, including portions of an existing performance that should remain unchanged.
These requirements often coexist within one action, as illustrated in Figure 1: a character may need to
follow a route, reach a pose at a designated moment, and maintain a prescribed upper-body motion.
Existing motion-control methods use learned completion, inference-time constraint enforcement, or
both. For temporal structure, CondMDI learns diffusion from varying frame–joint observations for
flexible in-betweening (Cohan et al., 2024). For spatial control, MaskControl couples learned motion-
token regularization with inference-time optimization of joint-position targets (Pinyoanuntapong
et al., 2025); ProjFlow enforces linear spatial constraints through kinematics-aware projection of
a pretrained flow (Watanabe et al., 2026). KIMODO spans these control categories using masked-
feature imputation in a two-stage denoiser, with training organized around predefined constraint
patterns and their mixtures (Rempe et al., 2026). MotionLab further shares generation and editing
through task-modulated rectified flows and source–condition–target mappings (Guo et al., 2025). A
cue’s consequences extend across time and the articulated body, so executing their combinations
requires learning the complete motion that connects them.
A cue set groups the kinematic cues for one motion. These prescribe partial motion states that
the generated action must pass through. General-purpose control must infer how to connect all of
these states into one coherent full-body trajectory. We call this capability cue-conditioned implicit
motion planning: the connecting trajectory is inferred as the motion is generated. The motion canvas
records the cue values and cue layout: which frames, joints, and position or rotation channels are
specified, including root translation. A generation mask distinguishes specified and unspecified
entries (Figure 1). Language describes the intended action, while optional input motion supplies
motion context through separate conditioning channels.
Our compositional cue sampler trains the model across cue combinations that vary in their frames,
joints, and position or rotation channels. Translation and joint-position cues can select individual
Cartesian axes, covering height and planar projections alongside full 3D targets. We call combinations
of different cue types mixed cues.
We learn these full-body connections with one flow-matching model conditioned on the complete
cue set. Cue imputation fixes cue values during training and sampling, with zero target flow velocity.
Boundary and rollout objectives train coherent transitions into and out of cue-prescribed states,
including isolated root waypoints. Forward-kinematic (FK) consistency links predicted positions to
the body decoded from rotations, enabling position cues to guide articulated motion.

Our contributions are threefold:
• A shared canvas unifies temporal and spatial cues, including axis-level position control. Com-
patible full-pose and partial-body cues compose across frames and body parts, with compositional
cue sampling teaching one generator to satisfy them together.
• A cue-preserving flow-matching model learns coherent full-body motion under combined
cues. Cue-constrained flow paths preserve prescribed states, while boundary and rollout objectives
train the shared model to connect them into a complete action.
• One model delivers state-of-the-art controlled-motion quality, mixed-cue adherence, and
text-to-motion generation. These gains span temporal, root, and body-part protocols and their
mixtures; no-cue generation retains language–motion alignment in the same trained model.

RELATED WORK
Motion generation and specialized control.
Task-specific generators address temporal completion
and keyframes (Cohan et al., 2024; Shafir et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2023), trajectories and spatial
targets (Karunratanakul et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2024), instruction editing (Athanasiou et al., 2024; Li
et al., 2025b; Jiang et al., 2025), transfer and style (Raab et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024; Kim et al.,
2025), repair (Mu et al., 2025), and sequential or streaming generation (Barquero et al., 2024; Xiao
et al., 2025; Ling et al., 2026). Their task-specific interfaces motivate a shared completion behavior
across heterogeneous cue layouts.
Unified motion control.
UniMotion (Li et al., 2025a) unifies generation and understanding;
UMO (Cong et al., 2026) uses a motion prior for in-context generation, completion, and edit-
ing, with language-generated trajectories for spatial control. KIMODO (Rempe et al., 2026) uses
joint-level imputation, MotionLab (Guo et al., 2025) shares source–condition–target mappings,
and MotionMaster (Jiang et al., 2026) uses a motion-language model for generation and editing.
MaskControl (Pinyoanuntapong et al., 2025) learns spatiotemporal masks over a fixed anchor set.

Conditional generative paths.
Known-value imputation and masked prediction (Uria et al., 2014;
Germain et al., 2015; Hoogeboom et al., 2022), and channel-wise editing context (Zhang et al., 2023b;
Brooks et al., 2023), are established conditional-generation tools. Motion methods use flow-path

temporal compatibility under mixed cue sets.

METHOD
MOTIONCANVAS realizes cue-conditioned implicit motion planning through a shared canvas and cue
sampling (Section 3.1), cue-preserving flow (Section 3.2), and geometry and transition supervision
(Section 3.3). Together, these components train one model to infer a coherent full-body trajectory
through all prescribed partial states (Figure 2).
3.1
MOTION CANVAS AND CUE COMPOSITION
Motion representation.
We follow the HY-Motion-1.0 representation (Wen et al., 2025), omitting
three redundant pelvis-position channels. For T frames, the state x ∈RT ×198 has rows
xi = [ ri, qi, pi ] ∈R198,
(1)
Here i indexes frames and d scalar channels; ri is root (pelvis) translation, qi contains continuous 6D
rotations of 22 SMPL joints (Zhou et al., 2019; Loper et al., 2015), and pi contains pelvis-relative
positions of 21 non-root joints. Position cues may select individual axes; rotation cues assign all six
channels of a joint.
Motion canvas.
The motion canvas records cue values xcon and encodes the cue layout with a
generation mask M ∈{0, 1}T ×198 (Figure 2(a)). Language and input motion are separate contextual
inputs. Mid = 0 marks a specified entry; Mid = 1 marks an unspecified entry to generate. Thus
1 −M selects specified entries.

FLOW MATCHING FOR COMPOSITIONAL MOTION CONTROL
We train a shared flow-matching model (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023) within the cue-defined
affine set in Eq. (2), preserving cue values while learning full-body completion. Here flow time
t ∈[0, 1] indexes refinement of an entire clip, while i indexes animation frames. For reference motion
x1, we draw Gaussian noise z ∼N(0, I) and t ∼U[0, 1], and construct
ΠM(x; xcon) = M ⊙x + (1 −M) ⊙xcon,
(4)
xt = ΠM((1 −t)z + tx1; x1).
(5)
Cue imputation ΠM projects the state onto this set by copying cue values to specified entries and
leaving the remaining entries unchanged. The initial state is ΠM(z; xcon).

3.3
TRAINING OBJECTIVES
Alongside the flow-matching objective, we supervise predicted motion for kinematic accuracy,
consistency between position and rotation representations, and coherent transitions around supplied
cues. During training, we observe that matching position cues does not ensure coherent connections:
root and joint trajectories can change abruptly around supplied positions. As in motion blending,
compatible postures alone do not ensure compatible movement across a transition (Kovar et al.,
2002).

4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
HumanML3D (Guo et al., 2022) tests temporal, root, and body-part cues (Q1) and their composition
(Q2), with paired captions unless marked no-text. We measure decoded cue error, route and transition
compatibility, and full-motion FID and retrieval (Petrovich et al., 2023). BABEL (Punnakkal et al.,
2021) tests sequential generation, MotionFix (Athanasiou et al., 2024) and PerMo (Kim et al., 2025)
editing, and BrokenAMASS (Mu et al., 2025) repair. Unless a benchmark specifies otherwise, outputs
are resampled to a common 30 fps SMPL-22 evaluation grid.
4.2
TEMPORAL CONTROL AND SEQUENTIAL GENERATION
Temporal cues.
To test Q1 beyond one cue layout, we use prediction from the first frame, motion
in-betweening (MIB) between fixed first and last 10% segments, and completion around 4–12 nonuni-
form full-body keyframes selected separately for each clip, with and without text. Table 1(a) and
Appendix Table 7 score both supplied-frame execution and completed-clip quality. MOTIONCANVAS
combines zero decoded cue error with the lowest FID in every displayed text-conditioned protocol;
with nonuniform keyframes and no text, it preserves the cues exactly but ranks second to CondMDI
in FID. These results cover contiguous, boundary, and irregular frame selections while exposing the
remaining benefit of semantic context (Figure 3(a)).
Sequential generation.
Given ordered BABEL captions, each action after the first is generated with
the preceding output’s final five frames fixed as a prefix. Segment retrieval tests action recognition;
transition-window FID and jerk profiles assess the joins. Table 1(b) gives MOTIONCANVAS the
highest R@1–3, lowest MM-Dist and transition-window FID, and reference-closest peak jerk. These
gains indicate recognizable actions and compatible joins, although the area under the jerk-profile gap
(AUJ) remains 3.1× the lowest baseline (Appendix C.1).
4.3
KINEMATIC CONTROL ACROSS SPATIAL SCOPES
Root trajectories and waypoints.
Given a dense trajectory, the model must generate steps, facing,
and limb coordination that realize the route; given sparse waypoints, it must additionally infer a
smooth intervening path. We therefore report Path ADE over all frames, not only anchors, together
with full-motion metrics (Appendix Table 8). Table 2(a) gives MOTIONCANVAS the best FID and
R@3, 0 cm dense-path ADE, and 3 cm sparse-waypoint ADE, demonstrating exact dense-route
execution and coherent path inference between sparse waypoints.
Body-part control.
We test Q1 across target joints or regions, rotation or position cues, and full or
selected Cartesian axes. Dense cues specify every valid frame; sparse cues use 30-frame intervals.
Table 2(b) reports near-zero error on the displayed rotation controls and 1.22 cm dense lower-body
position error; across these settings, MOTIONCANVAS also achieves the best FID and R@3. The
complete study supports Q1 across these dimensions, although error in the sparse both-wrist XYZ
setting remains higher than MotionLab, MaskControl, and ProjFlow (Figure 3(b); Appendix Table 9).

4.4
MIXED-CUE CONTROL
To test planning under mixed cues (Q2), P1–P4 randomly compose full-pose, root-path, body-region,
and joint-axis position cues with different densities and spatiotemporal distributions. For each test
motion, we sample the cue layout and copy compatible cue values from that motion (Appendix C.4).
Full-motion metrics assess the coordinated action; four family-specific errors measure adherence to
the combined cues. Table 3 shows the best aggregate and per-protocol full-motion metrics and lowest
four cue errors, supporting coherent completion across randomized cue sets. Figure 3(c) visualizes a
mixed cue set.
4.5
TEXT-TO-MOTION GENERATION WITHOUT KINEMATIC CUES
Q3 evaluates ordinary HumanML3D text-to-motion generation with the caption as the only condition,
against the HY-Motion-1.0-Lite initialization and published systems. Appendix Table 5 shows
improved FID (0.0157 to 0.0108) and MM-Dist, with retrieval changing by at most 0.45 percentage
points. Broad control training therefore preserves language–motion alignment rather than trading it
for controllability.
4.6
INPUT-MOTION EDITING AND REPAIR
Editing.
Editing transforms the input motion according to an editing instruction while preserving
other attributes. On 1,013 MotionFix triplets, only the input motion and editing instruction are
supplied. MOTIONCANVAS leads every reported MotionFix metric, reaching 0.0056 FID and 99.08%
M2M R@3 (Figure 3(d); Appendix Table 11). PerMo requests either a new style with action preserved
or a new action with style preserved; attribute and paired-retrieval metrics test the requested change,
retained factor, and target agreement. MOTIONCANVAS leads requested-attribute and paired-motion
scores on both tracks while retaining high complementary-attribute accuracy (Appendix Figures 9
and 10).
Repair.
Repair locates anomalies across joints and frames, then uses MOTIONCANVAS to regenerate
them from retained motion. BrokenAMASS separates repair with known anomaly regions, which
isolates regeneration, from automatic repair, which also localizes anomalies; the latter leads seven of
eight metrics, with acceleration the only exception (Appendices A.6 and C.6).
4.7
USER STUDY
To complement objective metrics, three professional technical artists compared 1,218 anonymously
ordered output pairs across five tasks, rating motion quality, physical artifacts, and repair effectiveness.
These yield 8,757 dimension-wise better/same/worse (G/S/B) judgments, favoring MOTIONCANVAS
in every dimension (Appendix C.7).
4.8
ABLATION STUDY
Training path and cue sampling.
To evaluate the training path and sampler (Q4), variants share
HY-Motion initialization, the 700-hour corpus, and training budget. Full-state flow interpolates all
variables during training and imputes cues at inference; named-task sampling uses fixed cue patterns.
As shown in Table 4(a), cue-preserving training improves trajectory execution (Path ADE: 0.122 to
0.030 m), while compositional cue sampling improves mixed-joint adherence (8.11 to 4.09 cm).
Boundary supervision.
Exact cue matching does not ensure compatible adjacent motion. Adding
objective bundles cumulatively reduces root-path second difference at supplied XZ/XYZ waypoints
from 13.34/13.46 to 1.62/1.96 and improves text-to-motion FID from 0.0127 to 0.0108, demonstrating
improved cue-local compatibility (Table 4(b); Appendix Table 15).

CONCLUSION
We presented MOTIONCANVAS for cue-conditioned implicit motion planning: inferring a coherent
full-body trajectory through the partial states prescribed by heterogeneous cues. A shared canvas
9


and compositional cue sampling enable one flow-matching model to learn these connections across
different cue layouts, while cue imputation preserves the cue values. The same model achieves state-
of-the-art results in controlled-motion quality, mixed-cue adherence, sequential generation, editing,
and repair, while retaining strong text-to-motion performance without kinematic cues. Together, these
results show how a shared motion prior can support diverse authoring requirements, allowing creators
to specify selected aspects of an action while the model infers the complete movement that brings
them together.

ADDITIONAL METHOD DETAILS
A.1
CONDITIONING INPUTS AND MMDIT ARCHITECTURE
The motion context is csrc = xsrc ⊙M for editing and zero otherwise; it conditions unspecified
entries on the input motion. The MMDiT motion input concatenates [xt∥csrc∥M] ∈RT ×594. The
language stream contains either a generation caption or an editing instruction, never both. In all
equations, i indexes motion frames, t denotes flow time, and s indexes solver steps; θ denotes the
model’s learned parameters.
Frozen Qwen3 token features in R4096 and a frozen CLIP sentence feature in R768 are projected to
width 1024. A two-layer, 16-head token refiner processes the language sequence before multimodal
fusion. The 18-layer MMDiT contains six double-stream blocks followed by twelve single-stream
blocks, with width 1024, 16 attention heads, an MLP expansion ratio of four, LayerNorm, RMS-
normalized queries and keys, and zero dropout. Motion attention is restricted to a maximum temporal
offset of 60 frames (2 seconds), so an interior motion token attends to at most 121 motion tokens.
The final head predicts one 198-dimensional flow velocity per frame.
Skeleton and coordinate conventions.
The 201-dimensional HY-Motion representation includes
the pelvis position relative to itself, which is always zero. Omitting this three-channel entry gives
our 198-dimensional state; root translation is retained. SMPL-22 denotes the 22-joint body skeleton
used here, excluding articulated hands. Its kinematic tree is the parent–child hierarchy connecting
joints through bones. Forward kinematics (FK) propagates rotations and bone offsets through this
hierarchy to compute global joint positions (Loper et al., 2015). Local joint rotations are relative
to the parent, whereas global joint positions and orientations use the common world frame. Root
orientation describes pelvis orientation; heading is its facing direction projected onto the horizontal
14


plane, not the direction of travel. The continuous 6D rotation representation stores two 3D vectors and
orthogonalizes them to recover a rotation matrix (Zhou et al., 2019). Pelvis-relative joint positions in
Eq. (9) subtract the pelvis position while retaining the coordinate axes.
A.2
COMPOSITIONAL CUE SAMPLING DISTRIBUTION
Equation (3) defines the three cue-sampling branches. Their mixture probabilities are 0.45 for one
selection of frames and kinematic quantities, 0.25 for a composition of selections, and 0.30 for
a correlated binary map. The composition branch draws R ∈[3, 8] with a decaying prior and
independently resamples frames and quantities for each selection; the selected frames are reused
across selections with probability 0.30. The correlated-map branch combines independent identically
distributed (IID), temporally correlated, joint/axis-correlated, and low-rank components, then restricts
cues to an explicitly sampled set of frames. Every selected frame contains at least one specified
kinematic quantity.

Frame selection.
A selection specifies all valid frames with probability 0.45. Otherwise, its
specified-frame ratio falls in (0, 5%], (5%, 20%], (20%, 50%], (50%, 80%], or (80%, 100%) with

probabilities (0.30, 0.25, 0.18, 0.14, 0.13). The first interval samples the frame count log-uniformly
to include isolated cues; the others sample it uniformly. For this fixed count, the sampler selects a
prefix or suffix, both a prefix and suffix, an interior segment, scattered frames, a correlated subset, or
disconnected segments.

A.7
OPTIMIZATION AND SAMPLING CONFIGURATION
We initialize from the released HY-Motion-1.0-Lite checkpoint (Wen et al., 2025) and further train
on an approximately 700-hour motion corpus covering diverse motion categories and variations.
All benchmark test and evaluation sequences used in this work are excluded from further training.
Training batches contain 30% text-only generation, 60% cue-conditioned generation, 5% MotionFix
editing, and 5% PerMo editing. Half of each editing stream additionally samples kinematic cues
from the reference edited motion. Text is dropped with probability 0.1 for classifier-free guidance;
kinematic-cue dropout is disabled.
26


We use AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) with learning rate 10−4, β = (0.9, 0.99), zero weight
decay, dataloader batch size 20, and gradient clipping at norm 2. A final continuation uses learning
rate 10−5 and global batch size 768 in FP32. Training runs for 2,000 epochs on 64 GPUs over
approximately 14 days. Relative to LMC, the weights are λpos = 50, λvel = 500, λexcess = 0.5,
λfk = 1500, λworldSob = 0.03, λrootSob = 0.01, λrootH1 = 50, λendpoint = 1, and λedge = 0.5.
Rollout weights are λroll-join = 0.05 and λroll-excess = 0.1. Unless stated otherwise, MOTIONCAN-
VAS generations use random seed 42. All reported MOTIONCANVAS inference uses classifier-free
guidance scale 2.5 and 50 Euler steps with cue imputation; repair additionally follows Appendix A.6.

C.1
TEXT-TO-MOTION AND SEQUENTIAL GENERATION
Text-to-motion.
The no-cue setting tests whether joint training for control and editing alters the
inherited language-conditioned generator. Table 5 reports the complete HumanML3D comparison
with the HY-Motion-1.0-Lite initialization and published text-to-motion systems.
Relative to the initialization, every retrieval rank changes by at most 0.0045 and MM-Dist improves
from 27.3560 to 27.2810. Among the displayed systems, MOTIONCANVAS attains the lowest FID
and MM-Dist while maintaining nearly unchanged retrieval and reference-like diversity. Joint training
therefore improves distributional fit without erasing language alignment or motion variety.
Sequential generation.
Temporal completion naturally yields sequential generation when adjacent
action segments share specified boundary frames. We construct the benchmark from the official
BABEL validation annotations. Sequence-level captions and explicit transition labels are not used as
action prompts; neighboring actions separated by an annotated transition meet at its midpoint, while
the next action onset defines boundaries without a transition interval. Adjacent intervals shorter than
30 frames are greedily merged forward, except for a short final interval, which is merged backward.
A fixed caption-rewrite cache turns each merged source-label sequence into one action caption.
30


Retaining episodes with at least two resulting actions yields 1,295 episodes, 7,285 action intervals,
and 5,990 transition boundaries.
For every action after the first, we prepend the preceding output’s final five frames as fixed temporal
cues; after sampling, this five-frame prefix is discarded and only the new continuation is appended.
Each generation uses the 360-frame sequence length used in training, with attention restricted to
its valid frames. All methods are converted to canonical neutral SMPL-22 motion at 30 fps before
evaluation. Subsequence retrieval uses official BABEL action groups, while subsequence and 30-
frame transition-window FID use per-sample ℓ2-normalized joint-position embeddings.

C.2
TEMPORAL CONTROL
The temporal protocols vary how much of a motion is specified and where the cue frames occur.
Prediction supplies the first frame or first 20% of frames; motion in-betweening supplies the first and
last frame or the first and last 10%; nonuniform-keyframe in-betweening supplies a precomputed
set of 4–12 nonuniform full-body keyframes selected separately for each clip, always including its
endpoints. Text-on rows measure how well the model combines semantic intent with partial motion,
whereas no-text rows isolate completion from kinematic cues alone. Every method receives identical
supplied frames and values. Temporal cue error is the mean Euclidean position error on supplied
frames; failure rates threshold each supplied frame at 20 or 50 cm.

The complete table separates exact execution of supplied frames from the quality of the completed
action. For prediction and in-betweening, MOTIONCANVAS combines zero cue error with the best

supplied frames and remains second in FID and MM-Dist. The no-text rows thus isolate what can be
inferred from motion evidence alone without obscuring this protocol-specific tradeoff.

C.6
MOTION REPAIR
Motion repair couples anomaly localization with regeneration: anomalous motion is localized
across joints and frames, then regenerated using the remaining valid motion as kinematic cues.
BrokenAMASS provides 299 valid 100-frame clean–corrupted pairs at 20 fps and evaluates the two
stages separately and together.
In repair with known anomaly regions, the known anomaly regions mark local rotations whose clean–
corrupted geodesic discrepancy exceeds 10−4 rad and root translations whose Euclidean discrepancy
exceeds 10−4 m. The clean reference determines only which entries are anomalous; regeneration
uses the corrupted clip and the resulting generation mask, thereby isolating regeneration quality.
In automatic repair, anomalies in joint motion and root translation are localized from the corrupted
clip itself before regeneration, evaluating the complete localization–repair pipeline. Appendix A.6
defines localization, mask expansion, and exact restoration.
For MOTIONCANVAS, rotations are resampled from 20 to 30 fps by spherical interpolation and root
translations by linear interpolation on endpoint-inclusive grids.
A 30 fps mask entry is the Boolean union of the two 20 fps endpoints contributing to it. Outputs are
resampled to the original 100-frame grid, and reliable values are restored before evaluation.
StableMotion (Mu et al., 2025) receives an anomaly mask at its native frame granularity.

LIMITATIONS
MOTIONCANVAS is currently trained for 22-joint body motion and does not model articulated hands
or facial expression, limiting expressive detail and applications that require fine-grained character
performance. Rotation cues are preserved exactly because they directly constrain the rotation channels
defining the decoded body. Position cues instead constrain redundant Cartesian channels and can
retain residual error after forward-kinematic decoding, where their consistency with rotation and
root translation is learned rather than hard-enforced. Post-hoc inverse kinematics, which adjusts
joint rotations to satisfy position targets, could enforce feasible targets but would add an engineered
correction stage outside the model; achieving exact decoded position control within the generative
process is a more principled direction. Finally, current spatial control is expressed through root
trajectories and body-part position cues. This provides a first step toward precise human–object
interaction (HOI) and human–scene interaction; both require explicit object and scene geometry,
contact constraints, and physical-feasibility reasoning.
