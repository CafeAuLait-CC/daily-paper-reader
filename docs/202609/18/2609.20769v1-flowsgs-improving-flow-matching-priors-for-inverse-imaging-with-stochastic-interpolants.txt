FlowSGS: Improving Flow Matching Priors for
Inverse Imaging with Stochastic Interpolants
Tianao Li1,3, Xinhui Qian2, Emma Alexander1,3
1Department of Computer Science, 2Department of Statistics and Data Science, Northwestern University

Abstract
Flow matching has emerged as the state-of-the-art generative model and has been
used for plug-and-play (PnP) priors to solve inverse problems in computational
imaging. However, existing flow-based inverse solvers assume linear forward mod-
els and/or make simplifying approximations in posterior sampling. To circumvent
these problems, we introduce FlowSGS, a flow-based posterior sampling method
using Split Gibbs Sampling (SGS) to decompose the posterior into a likelihood step
and a prior step. Specifically, we sample from the likelihood step using Langevin
dynamics and leverage the Stochastic Interpolants (SI) framework to integrate a
pretrained flow model into the prior step. We provide a form for the prior step
that uses SI’s reverse-time SDE, and show connections to previous PnP methods.
Moreover, with the aid of the flow prior’s straight probability paths and a novel
timestep correction technique for the reverse-time SDE, FlowSGS requires fewer
network evaluations in its prior step than plug-and-play diffusion samplers. Our
experiments show state-of-the-art performance on a range of inverse problems. For
the first time, we provide an experiment on a nonlinear inverse problem (Fourier
phase retrieval) for flow-based inverse solvers.

Introduction
Computational imaging systems aim to reconstruct an image x ∈Rn encoded in incomplete and
noisy measurements y = A(x) + n ∈Rm, where A(·) is the forward model and n represents noise.
These inverse problems are often ill-posed due to information loss, leading to uncertainty in the
reconstructed image ˆx. To fully explore the solution space, it is often desired to sample from the
Bayesian posterior
ˆx ∼p(x|y) ∝p(y|x)p(x),
(1)
where p(y|x) is the data likelihood and p(x) is the prior. This is particularly important for scientific
and medical imaging, where high-stakes decisions are being made in downstream applications.
Deep generative models have significantly improved posterior sampling methods by implicitly
capturing complex priors. Score-based diffusion models [50, 27, 52] learn the score function of the
target distribution and transform samples of a base distribution (often a standard normal distribution) to
samples of the target distribution by solving the reverse-time SDE of the diffusion process. Diffusion
models are capable of learning complex data distributions of images and videos, and have been widely
used to learn prior distributions p(x) as plug-and-play (PnP) priors. Most diffusion samplers draw
posterior samples by conditional sampling on the measurements y, either by taking gradient steps
towards higher data likelihood (e.g., DPS [14] and many others [29, 33, 26, 41]), or projection onto
the measurement space (e.g., [51, 16, 15]). PnP-DM [58] and several other works [68, 59, 17, 13]
leverage variable splitting to split the sampling into a likelihood step and a prior step, where a
pretrained diffusion model is incorporated as a plug-and-play prior. Inversebench [67] provides a

Flow matching is an emerging generative technique that improves upon diffusion models by construct-
ing straighter probability paths, which provide more efficient sampling, easier training, and higher
quality data generation [37, 38]. Although there already exist a number of flow-based inverse solvers,
they rely on mathematical approximations [47, 6, 64, 60, 42, 45, 34, 5] or assume a linear forward
model [47, 64, 60, 42, 5]. Specifically, PnP-Flow [42] iterates between a data-fidelity gradient step,
an interpolation step, and a denoising step that uses a flow network as a time-dependent denoiser.
FlowChef [45] uses gradient-free vector-field steering to push the probability-flow ODE trajectory
toward measurement-consistent points. FlowDPS [34] derives the flow version of Tweedie’s formula,
and estimates both the clean image and noise component to simultaneously maintain data-consistency
and generative quality. Other work [18] turns flow networks into a principled prior and learns the
posterior via variational inference. Importantly, none of these models has been demonstrated on a
nonlinear inverse problem.
Stochastic Interpolants (SI) provide a recent unifying framework that reveals the deep connection
between diffusion sampling and flow matching [2]. We leverage their connection between score
functions and velocity fields to present FlowSGS, a more principled posterior sampler using flow
matching as a plug-and-play prior. Specifically, our variable splitting technique avoids the major
simplifying assumptions and approximations of previous flow matching samplers. We split the
posterior sampling into a likelihood step and a prior step via split Gibbs sampling (SGS), and solve
the two steps iteratively with Langevin dynamics and a pretrained flow network. In contrast to prior
flow-based methods that rely on likelihood gradients [34], gradient-free trajectory steering [45],
or variational approximation of the posterior [18], our SGS-based splitting provides a principled
sampling method that targets the actual Bayesian posterior. With the aid of a novel timestep correction
technique, our integration of flow priors provides faster sampling with fewer steps needed in the prior
step than PnP-DM [58], which aligns with the theory that straighter probability paths used in flow
priors lead to better sampling. A high-level overview of FlowSGS is shown in Figure 1.
Our experiments in Section 4 show state-of-the-art image reconstruction compared to both diffusion-
and flow-based methods. We demonstrate accurate posterior sampling for a toy example with
known posterior, show high image quality on linear (motion deblurring, Gaussian deblurring, super-
resolution, compressed-sensing MRI) and nonlinear (Fourier phase retrieval) tasks.

2.2
Split Gibbs sampling
Variable splitting has been widely applied to inverse problem solving, even before the era of deep
generative priors. Algorithms such as Half-Quadratic Splitting (HQS) [11, 24], and the Alternating
Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) [9, 44] split the regularized MAP optimization into a
likelihood step and a denoising step, where a pretrained denoiser can be plugged in [56, 10, 63, 30].
Unrolled methods further turn these plug-and-play solvers into a neural network, and allow task-
specific training of the plugin denoisers [43]. However, these methods only produce a point estimate,
rather than drawing random samples from the Bayesian posterior.
Split Gibbs Sampling (SGS) [57] provides a solution for this and has already seen applications in
posterior sampling with classic priors [46], pretrained denoisers [7, 20], or diffusion priors [59, 58, 13].
SGS decomposes Bayesian posterior sampling in (1) into a likelihood step and a prior step, and
alternates between sampling from these two steps until convergence. Specifically, in the k-th iteration,

3
Method
FlowSGS uses Split Gibbs Sampling (SGS) to alternate between a likelihood step and a prior step
coupled by augmented Lagrangian parameters {ρk}. We use Langevin dynamics to sample from the
likelihood step because it applies to both linear and nonlinear forward models, and use an exponential
decay annealing strategy for the coupling parameter ρk = max(ρ0αk, ρmin). Among existing
methods, this is most similar to PnP-DM [58], which leverages the same problem decomposition but
uses a diffusion model in its prior step.
Prior step
We present four significant interventions in the prior step, expressed as lines 4-7
in Algorithm 1, which accommodate the replacement of diffusion models with more efficient and
accurate sampling via flow matching. Line 4 computes the correct starting timestep tk for the prior
step in the k-th iteration, adapted to the current noise level ρk and the interpolant schedule of any
pretrained flow model, see Theorem 1. Line 5 scales the output of the likelihood step zk such that
its noise level matches the noise σtk in the interpolant at tk. Line 6 samples from the prior step
by solving the reverse-time SDE with the Euler-Maruyama method [35]. Line 7 shows the SI’s
reverse-time SDE using only the velocity field, to our knowledge, used here for the first time in
posterior sampling for inverse problems. Corollary 1 specifies how our general framework can
be reduced to PnP-DM with a specific choice of diffusion coefficient. See more implementation
details of our method in Section C and analysis of convergence in Corollary 2. Our theory-driven
improvements are shown to be empirically effective in our benchmark experiments in Section 4.3.
Our ablation studies in Section 4.5 confirm that each of our four interventions is necessary.
Timestep correction
At a high level, these interventions lead to improvements over diffusion-based
methods, both in image quality and in the number of discretization steps needed in the prior step SDE
solver. As expected, FlowSGS’s flow priors can use fewer sampling steps and generate better results
than PnP-DM’s diffusion priors (see Section 4.5). Note that this makes the SDE discretization error
much more significant for flow matching than diffusion models. To mitigate the SDE’s discretization
error, we propose a timestep correction strategy to be applied before solving the SDE in Line 6
of Algorithm 1, illustrated in Figure 5a. We empirically validated that our timestep correction
significantly boosts FlowSGS’s reconstruction quality when the number of discretization steps is
smaller than 16.
Likelihood step
The likelihood step is also significant compared to existing flow-based poste-
rior samplers. We are the first flow-based method to incorporate measurement likelihood via SGS.
This change enables key applications in inverse imaging: more accurate posterior estimation (Sec-
tion 4.2) and nonlinear inverse problems (Section 4.4) with bimodal posteriors. Many methods
rely on simplifying approximations: some use linear projections between measurement and re-
covery space [47, 64, 60, 42, 5], while others incorporate the likelihood gradient as guidance for
the reverse-time SDE/ODE [34] or use gradient-free vector-field steering to enforce measurement
consistency [45]. In contrast, our method is a sampling method that directly targets the actual
Bayesian posterior. By avoiding simplifying assumptions, our likelihood step requires more computa-
tion (Langevin sampling rather than a single gradient step), but provides a more accurate posterior

3.1
Prior step with flow matching
The key observation that drives the design of FlowSGS’s prior step is the fact that sampling from the
SGS prior step (8) is equivalent to running SI’s reverse-time SDE (3) at the specific starting point
related to the coupling parameter ρk, which we summarize below in Theorem 1.
Theorem 1 (Prior step equivalence to SI reverse-time SDE). Suppose (αt, σt) defines an interpolant
process with gaussian probability path in (2), and αt∗> 0 and t∗satisfies σt∗/αt∗= ρk, running
the reverse-time SDE in (3) from xt∗= αt∗zk+1 to t = 0 produces samples from the SGS prior step
π(x|zk+1) in (8).
See proof of Theorem 1 in Section A.2. While PnP-DM [58] shows a similar equivalence between
its prior step and the diffusion’s reverse-time SDE, our Theorem 1 generalizes to any Gaussian
probability path defined by (2) under the SI framework. In fact, we later show in Section 3.2 that
PnP-DM’s prior step is a special case of our prior step. The insights drawn from Theorem 1 directly
lead to the timestep conversion, scaling, and reverse-SDE solving in Lines 4-7 of Algorithm 1, which
we will show to be effective in our ablation studies.
3.2
Relation to Existing PnP Methods
While in this paper we assume a flow prior, another way of using the score–velocity conversion in (4)
into the reverse-time SDE in (3) is to replace the velocity field v(·; ·) with the score function s(·; ·),
leading to a FlowSGS prior step using a diffusion model. We show in the following theorem that
FlowSGS’s diffusion-based prior step with a specific choice of the diffusion coefficient wt results
in the prior step of PnP-DM [58]. In other words, FlowSGS provides a general framework that can
incorporate either a diffusion or flow prior, with arbitrary choices of using a score or velocity model,
the interpolant process, and diffusion coefficients, and PnP-DM is a special case of FlowSGS under
specific design choices.
Corollary 1 (PnP-DM as a special case of FlowSGS). Define λt ≜−γt/αt = (αt ˙σt −˙αtσt)/αt. At
the sampling stage, FlowSGS’s timestep conversion and reverse-time SDE (3) reduce to PnP-DM (see
(7) in [58]) under specific conditions: the EDM diffusion coefficients αt = sE(t), σt = sE(t)σE(t),
the score–velocity conversion, and the KL-optimal diffusion coefficient wt = 2λtσt.
See proof of Corollary 1 in Section A.3. In fact, such a connection is no surprise, since the
stochastic interpolant framework also includes diffusion models in its mathematical formulations.
Our contribution lies in specifying the exact form of this relationship.
FlowSGS’s structural similarity to PnP-DM allows us to extend its convergence guarantee to FlowSGS
under the SI parameterization, which we summarize in the following corollary.
Corollary 2 (Convergence analysis). Suppose the interpolant schedule (αt, σt) satisfies that σt/αt
is strictly increasing on (0, 1], and the diffusion coefficient wt is bounded away from zero on the
interval used by the prior step. Then, under the L2-accurate condition on the pretrained flow
network, Algorithm 1 with constant coupling ρk ≡ρ enjoys the same non-asymptotic convergence
guarantee as PnP-DM [58, Theorem 3.1]: the average Fisher information between the non-stationary
process driven by the velocity network and the stationary process driven by the true velocity field
decays at a rate of O(1/K) in the number of SGS iterations K, up to a floor set by the velocity
network approximation error.
See detailed proof of Corollary 2 in Section A.4. Compared to PnP-DM, the assumptions become
more flexible: PnP-DM inherits its diffusion-coefficient floor from the EDM forward process (which
can vanish at the endpoints for iDDPM/EDM, requiring the workaround in [58, Appendix A.3]),
whereas in FlowSGS, the diffusion coefficient wt is a free sampling-time choice, so the floor condition
is a constraint on the user’s choice rather than on the model. The monotonicity condition on σt/αt is
the SI analog of PnP-DM’s Assumption A.1 and is satisfied by all standard schedules we consider.

4
Experiments
4.1
Baselines
We compare our method with three diffusion-based samplers: DPS [14], PnP-DM [58], DAPS [62],
as well as three flow-based samplers: PnP-Flow [42], FlowChef [45], and FlowDPS [34]. We pretrain
diffusion/flow models using the same NCSN++ [52] architecture for fair comparisons (see details
in Section C.1). All flow-based methods use the linear interpolant schedule [38] by default. For each
task, we tune the hyperparameters of all methods for the best performance.
4.2
Toy problem
To validate that FlowSGS can accurately sample from the Bayesian posterior, we create a toy
compressed sensing problem with a Gaussian likelihood and a Gaussian prior, such that the posterior
is an analytically available Gaussian distribution. See problem setups in Section B.1. We draw 128
posterior samples and compute the mean and pixel-wise standard deviation of the Gaussian posterior,
and compute the PSNR and SSIM of the mean image with respect to the ground truth image, the
KL-divergence of the Gaussian posterior with respect to the true posterior, shown in Figure 2. We
show that FlowSGS and PnP-DM (both being sampling methods) produce the most accurate posterior
in terms of both mean image quality and KL-divergence. Note that PnP-Flow, although not claimed
as a posterior sampler [42], also produces an accurate posterior. In contrast, DPS, FlowChef, and
FlowDPS significantly overestimate the uncertainty.
4.3
Linear inverse problems
We benchmark the baselines and FlowSGS (with different interpolant schedules) over a variety of
linear inverse problems (motion/Gaussian deblurring, super-resolution, and compressed sensing MRI)
using the FFHQ dataset [32] and the fastMRI knee dataset [61] resized to 256×256. See problem
setups in Section B.2. We pretrain diffusion/flow models on these datasets (checkpoints available).
In Tables 1 and 5, we report metrics averaged over 100 test samples not used during training. We draw
8 samples for each method and report the mean image. Visual samples are shown in Figure 3. We
show that FlowSGS is outperforming the baselines across a variety of inverse problems. Among the
interpolant schedules, the linear schedule provides the best overall results, while VP-SDE provides
the worst results. This aligns with the fact that the linear and GVP schedule used in flow matching

Using latent space models
We note that, in addition to nonlinear inverse problems, the generality
of our method allows latent-space flow priors, which we consider briefly here. Figure 10 shows
example comparisons on the motion deblurring and super-resolution problems to PnP-Flow [42],
FlowChef [45], and FlowDPS [34], using the off-the-shelf Stable Diffusion 3.5 [19] medium model.
See Section B.3 for experimental details. Our method is slower due to repeatedly taking the gradient
through the decoder in the Langevin steps (see Section C.2). This suggests future work using a hybrid
pixel/latent space Langevin sampling scheme (running in pixel space initially and then moving to
latent space in later iterations), as mentioned as a possibility in [62].

Ablation studies
We perform ablation studies on the key design choices in our flow-based prior step (Lines 4-7
in Algorithm 1) as well as the interpolant schedules (αt, σt) and diffusion coefficients wt.
Timestep conversion, scaling, and timestep correction
In Figure 5a, we illustrate the robust
timestep correction strategy we designed to reduce discretization error in the prior step SDE solver
(Line 6 in Algorithm 1). Specifically, instead of starting at the closest discrete timestep to tk as in
PnP-DM [58], we use an adaptive stepsize in the first Euler step and start from the correct time tk.
In Figure 5b and Table 7, we show FlowSGS results on motion deblurring with missing components
in Algorithm 1: correct timestep conversion (Line 4), scaling (Line 5), time step correction (Line
6), and verify that they collectively contribute to our method. In Figure 5c, we observe that as the
discretization step decreases, PnP-DM (blue) is significantly affected by discretization error, while
our timestep correction allows FlowSGS to maintain a more stable performance (purple). This aligns
with the theory that the linear interpolant has straighter probability paths and is more robust to
discretization errors [36, 38, 40] than diffusion models. See visual samples in Figure 11. As a result,
we use a 32-step discretization (marked by a star) in the prior step, while PnP-DM needs 100 steps.
Interpolant schedules and diffusion coefficients
In Table 8, we compare FlowSGS results gener-
ated using three schedules (VP-SDE, linear, and GVP) and four diffusion coefficients (see summary
in Section A.1) on motion deblurring and Fourier phase retrieval. We find that the linear interpolant
combined with the KL-optimal diffusion coefficient wKL gives the best results. This is in accordance
with the fact that wKL minimizes the upper bound on the KL divergence between the true and learned
data distribution [40, 2], leading to more accurate sampling. Despite wKL’s empirical success, it is
worth mentioning that wKL is not regular at t = 0 and t = 1 [40], therefore in practice we solve the
SDE in [ϵ, 1 −ϵ] with ϵ = 1 × 10−5.
5
Conclusion
We present modifications to SGS that accommodate SI-based generative models, including potential
future approaches. In doing so, we improve existing flow-based methods with better integration of
measurement likelihood and more efficient prior-step sampling than similar diffusion-based methods.
We characterize our relationship to a previous method in both theory (comparing FlowSGS to
PnP-DM in Corollaries 1 and 2) and empirical results. Despite our SOTA performance, we note
current limitations: sampling cost is elevated compared to other flow methods, though this could be
improved with one-step flow models, e.g., Mean Flow [25] and Meta Flow Maps [48]. We currently
only consider non-blind inverse problems, and could extend into semi-blind or blind settings by
incorporating our flow-based prior step into EM frameworks, e.g., PRISM [28]. Finally, Langevin
dynamics are not efficient with latent space models, which suggests potential for hybrid sampling.

Rather than reproduce the full Fokker–Planck calculation, we show that FlowSGS satisfies all the
structural conditions used in PnP-DM’s [58] Theorem 3.1, so that the same proof applies after a
parameter substitution. We highlight three aspects and then describe the substitution.
Identical SGS skeleton.
The likelihood step of FlowSGS (Algorithm 1, line 3) is identical to
PnP-DM’s: both target the same conditional distribution π(z|x) ∝exp[−f(z; y) −∥x −z∥2/2ρ2]
via Langevin dynamics. PnP-DM’s Brownian-bridge interpretation ([58, Proposition A.2]) and the
resulting KL/Fisher contraction (their Lemma 2 of [55]) therefore carry over to FlowSGS.
Prior step has the canonical drift–diffusion form.
PnP-DM’s key technical lemma ([58,
Lemma A.4]) is a generic Fokker–Planck argument that bounds the time-derivative of the KL
divergence between any pair of diffusion processes that share the same diffusion coefficient c(t) and
differ only in their drifts. It makes no assumption about how the drift is constructed.
The FlowSGS prior-step SDE (5) is exactly such a process: its drift is an affine combination of the
velocity v(x, t) and the state x, and its diffusion coefficient is √wt. The non-stationary process
(driven by the pretrained vθ) and the stationary process (driven by the true v) share the same
c(t) = √wt, which is the only structural requirement of the lemma. Hence PnP-DM’s lemma applies
to FlowSGS’s prior step verbatim, with the squared drift gap proportional to ∥vθ −v∥2
2 instead of
∥sθ −s∥2
2.
SI-equivalent assumptions.
PnP-DM’s Theorem 3.1 relies on two assumptions:
• (Assumption A.1) σE(t) is strictly increasing, so there is a unique t⋆with σE(t⋆) = ρ.
Under the SI parameter correspondence σE(t) = σt/αt (Corollary 1), this becomes the
requirement that the noise-to-signal ratio σt/αt is strictly increasing – precisely the well-
posedness condition of our Theorem 1, and satisfied by all schedules in Table 2.

• (Diffusion-coefficient floor) The infimum δ := inf[0,t⋆] sE(t)√2 ˙σEσE of PnP-DM’s reverse-
SDE diffusion coefficient is strictly positive. Under the same correspondence this infimum
becomes inf[0,t⋆]

where the assumptions become looser: PnP-DM’s δ is fixed by the EDM forward process
and can vanish at the endpoints (e.g. for VP/iDDPM), forcing the workaround in [58,
Appendix A.3]; FlowSGS lets the user pick wt freely at sampling time, so the floor is a
constraint on the user’s choice rather than on the model.
The score-accuracy assumption of PnP-DM translates to an L2-accuracy condition on the velocity
field under πτ, which is equivalent to the score condition via the velocity–score relation ((4) in the
main paper) up to bounded scalar factors.
Reduction.
Combining the three remarks, every step of the proof of Theorem 3.1 in [58, Ap-
pendix A.2] goes through: the likelihood-step contraction is unchanged; the prior-step contraction
follows from [58, Lemma A.4] applied to the canonical-form SDE described in Remark 2, with PnP-
DM’s v(t) replaced by √wt and the score-error term replaced by the corresponding velocity-error
term; the telescoping over K outer iterations is identical. The resulting non-asymptotic bound has the
same shape as PnP-DM’s: an O(1/K) contraction from initialization governed by min(ρ, δ)2, plus a
velocity-approximation floor governed by δ2.

Implementation details
C.1
Network architectures
In our toy problem and benchmarking experiments with pixel-space models ( Sections 4.2 and 4.3),
we pretrain our own diffusion and flow models using the NCSN++ architecture [52] implemented
by Inversebench [67]. All models are trained for 500 epochs with the Adam optimizer on each
dataset, and use an exponential moving average (EMA) for better performance. For the diffusion
models, we use the VP-SDE as the diffusion schedule, while for the flow models, we train with the
VP-SDE, linear, and GVP schedules (see summary in Table 2) and compare them in our ablation
studies (see Tables 1 and 8). Within every task, all diffusion/flow models are trained on the same
training set and share the same architecture and optimization parameters for fair comparisons. Our
model checkpoints trained on CelebA, FFHQ, and fastMRI knee are available on our project page.
We use the off-the-shelf Stable Diffusion 3.5 Medium [19] as our latent-space flow prior.

Only two hyperparameters materially affect reconstruction quality. The first is ρmin, which sets
the stationary coupling between the likelihood and prior steps and hence the starting time tk of the
final prior step that refines the output; LPIPS degrades from 0.188 to 0.259 as ρmin grows to 1.0,
since a large terminal coupling leaves the prior step starting too far up the interpolant to commit to
a measurement-consistent solution. The second is τ, the assumed measurement noise level, which
plays the same role as the measurement weight in DPS [14], FlowChef [45], FlowDPS [34], and

degrades on both sides. By contrast, FlowSGS is robust to the starting level ρ0 and the decay rate α,
each of which varies LPIPS by less than 0.008 across more than an order of magnitude. We note that
the Langevin step size η improves monotonically over the range tested, indicating that our default is
conservative rather than tuned to an interior optimum. For context on the parameter count, principled
posterior samplers such as PnP-DM [58] and DAPS [62] carry a comparable number; several of
ours arise in the Langevin likelihood step and could be eliminated for linear forward models by a
proximal likelihood sampler [58], which we retain Langevin in place of for generality across linear
and nonlinear operators.

E
Broader Impacts
We demonstrate that this method is effective in a medical imaging application (accelerated single-coil
MRI) and believe it could broadly impact scientific and medical imaging in a positive way. However,
inverse imaging enables reconstruction of scenes that may have been considered private before such
methods existed. While we do not directly test on such applications, we do show reconstructions
of faces, which suggests that this method might be used to identify people in situations that would
previously have been anonymous. Additionally, we use 2 NVIDIA H100 GPUs for pretraining, and a
single H100 GPU for sampling.
