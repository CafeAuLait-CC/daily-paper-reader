## Abstract 

Text-driven 3D human-object interaction (HOI) genera-tion requires synthesizing whole-body human and object motions that stay coupled through contact. Yet existing generators either entangle the core variables that govern an interaction—object pose, contacting regions, and limb posture—with dozens of subordinate joints, or plan only end-effectors or coarse trajectories. No stage of genera-tion holds the interaction in a form that is both settled and observed, which leaves contact to external guidance, post-hoc refiners, or internal guidance without a surface mea-surement. We present CHORD , a two-stage rectified-flow framework that settles the interaction in a compact plan and observes that plan against the object surface while it is being generated. First, DRAFT uses twelve part tokens per temporal patch to jointly plan the 6-DoF object trajec-tory, contact states, and the end and middle joints of ev-ery kinematic chain. At each flow step, DRAFT decodes the expected interaction from the closed-form clean estimate, measures it against the object surface, and routes this feed-back to the responsible part token with zero extra network evaluations. Second, FULL keeps this plan unchanged and infills the remaining body and finger joints. On InterAct, 

CHORD reduces FID by 33% and lifts hand contact F1 from 0.730 to 0.834, while its feedback improves contact on unseen objects and transfers plug-and-play to existing HOI generators. 

1arXiv:2611.28682v1 [cs.CV] 21 Nov 2026 1. Introduction 

Generating 3D human-object interactions (HOI) from nat-ural language is a core capability for character animation, virtual reality and embodied AI [24, 30, 49, 54]. Com-pared with text-to-motion synthesis [46, 51] or grasping of static objects [44, 45], text-driven HOI is more demand-ing: it must jointly synthesize a 6-DoF object trajectory and whole-body human motion, including articulated fin-gers, that stay coupled through contact throughout the in-teraction. Consider the instruction “lift the bat high with both hands and swing it” (Fig. 1, right). The text leaves three forms of coordination implicit. First, body parts divide the labor : the hands grip the handle, the torso rotates into the swing, and the legs stay planted to brace the motion. Sec-ond, body and object move as one : the bat must track the hands frame by frame rather than drift beside them. Third, 

contact persists : once the hands close around the handle, they must stay on it without hovering, slipping or penetrat-ing. Recent methods have made notable progress toward these requirements along two axes. The first is how the human and the object are coordinated. Single-stage gen-erators synthesize both together, by coupling a human and an object branch through cross-attention [38], by learning motion jointly with contact [10, 54], or by denoising body, hand and object token groups at separate noise levels [49]. Coarse-to-fine pipelines first produce a sparse guide and then synthesize the detailed motion conditioned on it: hand positions predicted from the object motion [29] or coarse human and object trajectories [24]. The second axis is how contact errors are corrected. Most methods correct them outside the generator. During sampling, guidance steers the sample toward predicted contacts, contact constraints or a learned interaction field [10, 27, 30, 38, 54], or a separate physics-informed predictor or relation model corrects each denoised estimate [52, 57]. After sampling, the motion is refined by contact optimization or a dedicated refinement network [4, 14, 34, 65]. A recent alternative keeps the guid-ance inside the generator: the body, hands and object are denoised at different paces, so that the parts that are already clean guide those that are still noisy [49]. Despite these advances, body and object still drift apart during the interaction because no stage of generation holds the interaction in a form that is both settled and observed. It is not settled because the variables that decide it—how the object moves, which body regions touch it, and how each limb is postured—have no dedicated locus. Single-stage generators mix them with every other degree of free-dom, while coarse-to-fine pipelines plan only end-effectors or coarse trajectories and leave limb posture open. It is not observed because the generator receives no measurement of where its evolving prediction stands relative to the ob-ject surface, which leaves contact to corrections outside the generator that add inference cost, or to internal guidance that lacks such a measurement. To this end, we present CHORD , a part-structured rectified-flow framework that decides the interaction in a plan and checks that plan against the object surface while it is being generated (Fig. 2). To settle the interaction, CHORD decides it in a dedicated planning stage. Once the object pose, the pelvis, and the end and middle joints of every kinematic chain are fixed, the remaining body joints are tightly constrained by skeletal geometry. Our first stage, D RAFT , therefore represents each temporal patch with twelve part tokens, one for the object and one per planned joint, that jointly generate the object trajectory, these joints and their contact states. Our second stage, FULL , takes this plan as fixed and infills the remaining body joints and the fingers. To observe it, CHORD closes the contact loop inside the learned velocity field of D RAFT . At every flow step, the closed-form clean estimate of the rectified flow [33, 35] exposes the interaction the plan currently expects to pro-duce. We measure this expected interaction against the ob-ject surface and return the measurement to the part token responsible for it, so the plan is revised while it is being formed, without extra network evaluations, as the measure-ment is computed from the clean estimate the velocity field already produces. By deciding the interaction in one plan and checking it as it forms, CHORD keeps body and object coupled throughout the interaction. On the InterAct benchmark [54], CHORD outperforms prior text-to-HOI generators in motion realism and contact accuracy. The feedback keeps improving contact on objects unseen in training. We further demonstrate its versatility as a plug-and-play module, which improves contact accuracy and reduces floating objects when integrated into existing HOI generators [49, 54]. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose CHORD, a two-stage rectified-flow frame-work that decides the interaction in a part-structured plan and then completes the remaining body and finger joints. • We introduce in-generation contact feedback, which mea-sures the expected interaction against the object surface at every flow step and corrects contact without gradient guidance or extra network evaluations. • Extensive experiments show that CHORD achieves state-of-the-art motion realism and contact accuracy, and that its feedback transfers to unseen objects and to existing generators. 

## 2. Related Work 

Human-object interaction generation. Early HOI syn-thesis addressed reaching and grasping static objects [23, 44, 45, 50] or forecasting short interactions from past mo-2tion [52]. Full-body manipulation datasets [2, 21, 29, 54] shifted attention to generating human and object motion jointly from text, alongside zero-shot approaches that com-pose pretrained priors [3, 8, 31, 36, 53] and physics-based approaches that realize interactions in simulation [16, 48, 55, 56]. Kinematic methods further cover intent condi-tioning, fine-grained semantics, hand-object or multi-body interaction, static human-object configurations, unseen ob-jects and autoregressive or masked generation [6, 12, 13, 17, 22, 32, 39, 59, 64]. Among text-driven generators, HOI-Diff [38], CG-HOI [10] and Text2HOI [4] couple human and object generation with learned contact or affordance predictors, and LIGHT [49] denoises the body, hands and object asynchronously. 

Hierarchical and part-aware generation. To coordinate body and object motion, hierarchical methods split inter-action synthesis into stages. OMOMO [29] predicts hand trajectories from a given object motion before generating the full body, CHOIS [30] conditions on sparse object way-points and DecHOI [24] generates coarse human and object trajectories prior to detailed motion. However, the interme-diate representation is either supplied at test time or limited to trajectories and end-effectors that leave limb posture un-constrained. Part-aware conditioning has been explored in object-free motion generation, through part-level descrip-tions [28, 63], prompt decomposition [43], part embeddings derived from a global sentence vector [19] or coordinated part-wise generators [66], and in HOI through learnable chain-level queries, shared across the frames of a sequence, that gather the text and object geometry [62]. In contrast, the plan of CHORD is generated from text alone, covers the object trajectory, contact states and the end and middle joints of every kinematic chain, and is copied unchanged into the final motion. Each of its part tokens queries the words of the instruction at every temporal patch, so each part attends to the words that concern it as the motion un-folds. 

Contact correction. Existing pipelines typically correct contact outside the generator. Sampling is steered through classifier or training-free guidance [1, 7, 9, 18, 25, 41, 60] with affordance or contact objectives [10, 38, 54], neu-ral interaction fields [27] or physics-informed or relation-based corrections during denoising [52, 57, 61]. Others ap-ply post-hoc contact optimization [14, 65], optimization of the diffusion noise [26, 42] or a separate refinement net-work [34]. These corrections add gradient steps, optimiza-tion or a second network at inference. A recent alterna-tive keeps the guidance inside the generator through asyn-chronous denoising [49], which removes the external clas-sifier but does not measure the prediction against the object surface. In contrast, CHORD measures the expected inter-action against the object surface at every flow step and feeds the measurement back into the responsible part token, so the correction becomes part of the velocity field.

3.2. Part-Structured Planning and Completion 

Interaction plan. The plan should fix what defines the in-teraction and leave to F ULL what skeletal geometry already determines. It therefore holds the object pose, which locates the interaction surface, the pelvis with its root orientation, which places the body, and the end joint together with the middle joint of five kinematic chains: hands and elbows, feet and knees, head and chest. The end joints set where the body reaches and touches; the middle joints fix the limb posture that the end joints leave open and that hand-first plans [29] leave undetermined. Every remaining body joint then moves rigidly with a planned joint or lies on a short bone chain between two of them. FULL completes the motion by infilling (Fig. 2e). The token inputs combine the planned state with the surface relations and contact probabilities predicted by DRAFT ; the finger token additionally receives the object surface points nearest to each planned hand. 

3.3. In-Generation Contact Feedback 

Because F ULL leaves the planned object trajectory and joints unchanged, whether the body reaches the object is decided in D RAFT . A generator that encodes object geome-try only as a static condition, however, has no explicit mea-surement of whether the interaction it currently expects to produce reaches the surface. CHORD instead checks the expected interaction while it is being decided: at every flow step, the interaction the plan currently expects is measured against the object surface, and the measurement is returned to the part token that will revise it (Fig. 2d).

## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Dataset. We evaluate on InterAct [54], a benchmark of whole-body human-object interactions with text descrip-tions and object meshes. We follow its official training and test splits. 

Metrics and protocol. We measure alignment and real-ism via retrieval precision (R-Precision, top-1/2/3), FID, multimodal distance (MM Dist) and diversity [15]; physi-cal plausibility via the foot-skating ratio (FSR, the share of frames with foot sliding) and object penetration (Pene, the mean depth of body joints inside the object); and interaction accuracy via the contact ratio (Contact, the share of frames in contact) and frame-wise hand contact precision, recall and F1 against the ground truth. All methods are scored with the pretrained evaluator of LIGHT [49]. In ablations, F1 H denotes hand contact F1 ( i.e . CF 1) and R@3 256 top-3 R-Precision at batch size 256. 

Baselines. We compare with HOI-Diff [38], CHOIS [30], Text2HOI [4], InterAct [54], ROG [57] and LIGHT [49]. All are trained on InterAct with text, object geometry and body shape as the only inputs, CHOIS without waypoints, and scored with the same evaluator. 

Implementation details. Object surfaces are sampled with 4096 points, and clips have at most 300 frames at 30 fps. The instruction is encoded by a frozen 

Qwen2.5-1.5B [58], and DRAFT uses classifier-free guidance scale 0.5. Our model and every ablation variant are trained with the same recipe for 170k updates, and their results are means over ten sampling seeds. Sampling a clip takes N = 32 DRAFT and 8 F ULL Euler steps, 72 network evaluations under classifier-free guidance, without gradient guidance or an auxiliary network. 

4.2. Comparison with Prior Methods 

Quantitative comparison. As shown in Tab. 1, CHORD achieves the lowest FID, MM Dist and penetration, the best top-2 and top-3 R-Precision and the diversity closest to the ground truth. It also has the highest contact precision, recall and F1. Both designs contribute to these gains, as the abla-tions in Secs. 4.3 and 4.4 show. On each of the six subsets, CHORD also has the best contact F1, R-Precision and MM Dist against InterAct and LIGHT. 

Qualitative comparison. Figure 3 compares representa-tive sequences with the released InterAct [54] and LIGHT models. The baselines move the object without touching it, leave it floating between the hands or let the body pass through it. In our sequences, the hands stay on the object while it moves. 

Unseen objects. To evaluate generalization to unseen shapes, we follow the protocol of DecHOI [24] and replace the object in OMOMO [29] test clips with 17 meshes from 3D-FUTURE [11] of the same categories, none of which is seen in training, while keeping the captions (Tab. 2). CHORD follows the instruction more closely than the re-leased LIGHT model, makes more hand contact, leaves the object floating in fewer frames, breaks contact less often and keeps the hands closer to the moving object. Disabling the feedback at inference lowers contact and raises floating and contact breaks on these shapes.

## 5. Conclusion 

We presented CHORD, a text-driven HOI generator in which planning and contact correction form one design. CHORD decides the interaction where it can be checked, and checks it where it is decided. The part tokens give every contact region its own place in the plan, surface feedback re-vises that place at every flow step, and the completion stage keeps what the plan has settled. CHORD outperforms prior methods in realism and contact accuracy on InterAct, and its feedback carries over to unseen objects and to existing generators. 

Limitations. The feedback acts on the planned joints, and the fingers are completed around them without a loop of their own; closing the loop at the finger level is a natural extension. CHORD also models a single rigid object in a clip of pre-specified, bounded length ( L ≤ 300 frames); articulated objects and longer sequences are left for future work.
