Abstract
An embodied speaker’s expressiveness depends not only on
what is said, but also on how the voice sounds and how the
body moves. However, existing joint speech–motion gener-
ation models are largely driven implicitly by transcript se-
mantics or intermediate speech representations, offering lit-
tle explicit control over motion hierarchy, body parts, or vo-
cal style. To bridge this gap, we construct MotionSpeech, a
structured benchmark for controllable joint speech–motion
generation that includes transcripts, voice-style descrip-
tions, and motion conditions at two hierarchical levels.
Building on this benchmark, we propose DuoFlow, a dual-
stream latent flow transformer that jointly denoises speech
and motion under a shared noise level and a unified
second-based temporal coordinate. Modality-specific resid-
ual connectors inject structured conditions into the cor-
responding streams, layer-wise bidirectional cross-modal
attention coordinates their generation, and a monotonic
phoneme pathway combines normalized progress anchors
with CTC supervision to strengthen speech temporal mod-
eling. DuoFlow provides a unified interface for fine-grained
motion and vocal-style control and demonstrates the ability
to synthesize high-quality, synchronized, and instruction-
consistent speech–motion sequences. Project page.

1. Introduction
An embodied speaker communicates through speech and
movement at the same time: an utterance may be accom-
panied by a rhythmic beat, an illustrative hand gesture, or a
subtle postural shift [31, 40]. In many applications the ver-
bal content is fixed in advance, as in a script, a voice-over, or
an avatar’s dialogue. The central question then shifts from
what is said to how it is delivered: how the utterance should
sound, and how the body should move as it is spoken.
Because conversational gesture varies considerably across
speakers and contexts [15, 50], a single plausible realiza-
tion is insufficient when a particular delivery is intended,
and user intent can be difficult to reconcile with fine-grained
motion and timbre. In practice, users can readily express an
overall gestural intent but cannot be expected to enumerate
the temporally ordered actions of individual body parts. A
useful control interface should therefore cover hierarchical
levels of granularity, expanding concise high-level guidance
into detailed, coherent motion while also following fine-
grained instructions when they are available.
Existing systems generally lack such an interface be-
cause control remains fragmented across two largely sepa-
rate paradigms. On the speech side, instruction-following
text-to-speech can follow vocal instructions but does not
synthesize body motion [11]. On the motion side, prompt-
based full-body control, caption-guided gestures, and joint-
level spatial constraints have substantially advanced motion
controllability [4, 12, 41], but still use speech that has al-
ready been produced as the conditioning input. Cascading a
speech generator with a motion generator combines the two
outputs but retains a one-way interface: the motion stage
receives completed audio, so a motion instruction supplied
at that stage cannot shape the speech upstream. Early and
recent joint speech–gesture studies have demonstrated the
feasibility of joint generation [3, 18, 19, 36, 37, 45]; joint
speech–face generation [27, 30] and dialogue systems sup-
porting discrete body-action directives [53] provide related
precedents.
However, these interfaces still do not allow
users to control both motion and vocal expression through
instructions.
Turning joint generation into controllable generation
raises three challenges. First, supervision for such an in-
terface does not yet exist. Existing co-speech corpora pro-
vide audio, transcripts, motion, and in some cases seman-
tic or emotion labels [31, 33], while caption-guided motion
work adds textual gesture supervision [12]; however, none
aligns the same speech–motion target with hierarchical mo-
tion descriptions and a voice description. Second, semantic
condition guidance is complex: speech and motion differ in
temporal resolution, so their conditions must be injected ac-
curately, and the model must bridge the gap between coarse
user intent and fine-grained motion control. Third, evalu-
ation remains incomplete: existing work [26, 33, 39] does
not directly evaluate whether jointly generated speech and
motion follow their respective voice and motion instruc-
tions, motivating a unified benchmark for quality evalua-
tion.
To this end, we construct MotionSpeech and propose
DuoFlow (Figure 1). MotionSpeech builds on existing pub-
lic datasets [1, 33] and refines four high-quality annota-
tion channels: a coarse motion description (L1), a fine-
grained description of body-part actions and their tempo-
ral order (L2), a voice description (AUDCAP), and a tran-
script. On this basis, we propose DuoFlow, a dual-stream
latent flow transformer. The model denoises motion and
audio latents in parallel under a shared noise level and a
unified time coordinate measured in seconds. Bidirectional
cross-modal attention in every block allows the two streams
to exchange information, while modality-specific residual
connectors adapt the structured condition for each stream.
A dedicated monotonic phoneme-alignment pathway adds
a normalized progress anchor to the phoneme context and
uses phoneme-CTC supervision to make intermediate audio
states follow the transcript order without forced phoneme
boundaries. Progressive audio-only pretraining followed by
joint speech–motion training gives the model strong gener-
alization capability.
Our contributions are threefold.
(i) We present Mo-
tionSpeech, a four-channel structured benchmark for fine-
grained controllable joint generation, comprising 34,950
clips (approximately 125.2 hours).
(ii) We propose
DuoFlow, the first joint speech–motion generation frame-
work to support hierarchical motion and voice-condition
control. (iii) We establish a unified evaluation protocol for
generation quality, cross-modal synchronization, and text–
motion consistency. Experiments show that DuoFlow is the
only one with a native unified interface that simultaneously
supports end-to-end joint generation, fine-grained motion
control, and free-form vocal-style control, while maintain-
ing strong motion quality, speech–motion coordination, and
instruction consistency.
2. Related Work
2.1. Co-speech motion generation.
Given speech, co-speech models synthesize body move-
ment: early cross-modal translation [15], trimodal text–
audio–speaker conditioning with the FGD metric [50], and
large annotated corpora with semantic baselines [31, 33].
Architectures advanced from regression to diffusion, state-
space, and flow models [33, 35, 47]. Recent systems add
caption- or example-level semantics [12, 34, 43, 54]. How-
ever, these methods all treat speech as a conditioning input
and do not support joint speech–motion generation.
2


2.2. Joint speech–motion generation.
Match-TTSG [37] pioneered joint flow synthesis of speech
and upper-body gesture in one process; FastTalker [19]
reaches real-time joint generation by reusing speech fea-
tures; Gelina [18] unifies the two modalities with inter-
leaved discrete tokens and style cloning; Motion-Omni [36]
emits full-body motion natively from dialogue hidden states
with an open evaluation protocol; RapVerse [6] targets vo-
cals and whole-body dance; ViBES [53] lets a dialogue
agent accept discrete body-action directives; DyaPlex [38]
targets streaming duplex conversation. Adjacent joint mod-
els cover faces, audio-video, or agent stacks [5, 9, 10, 22,
24, 27, 52]. Connectionist temporal classification (CTC)
marginalizes monotonic paths between frame sequences
and unsegmented label sequences [17]; we use it only as an
auxiliary training signal on latent audio states, rather than
as an autoregressive decoder or an inference-time forced
aligner.
These systems establish that joint generation is
possible and increasingly efficient, but their interfaces ei-
ther expose no user-facing motion semantics at all (Match-
TTSG, FastTalker, Gelina, Motion-Omni) or trigger dis-
crete behaviors instead of span- and part-level instructions
(ViBES).
2.3. Controllable multimodal generation.
On the speech side, CosyVoice 2 [11] provides stream-
ing zero-shot cloning and instruction-following speech, and
MGM-Omni [44] stabilizes long-horizon personalized tim-
bre; both control a single modality.
LTX-2 [20] gener-
ates synchronized audio-video with a two-stream DiT and
modality-CFG, and unified music–motion models [48, 51]
align rhythmic latents across modalities; pixel-space audio-
video and music-driven synchronization, however, do not
provide semantically structured 3D motion amenable to
part- and span-level instructions. Streaming systems opti-
mize latency rather than controllability [23, 55]. DuoFlow
follows this line of research by adopting joint latent flow
generation as its mechanism and constructing a unified con-
trol interface.
3. The MotionSpeech Benchmark
3.1. Data and Hierarchical Annotations
MotionSpeech couples full-body conversational motion
with multi-scale hierarchical spoken-language annotations.
It selects and integrates samples from two complemen-
tary sources of natural talking–gesturing behavior:
(i)
BEAT2 [33], a studio-capture corpus pairing SMPL-X
body motion with expressive English speech, and (ii) Seam-
less Interaction [1], an improvised conversational corpus
of spontaneous multi-speaker dialogues with rich prosodic
variation.

4.2. Motion VAE
We adapt the MotionStreamer causal codec [46] to en-
code full-body motion into continuous latents. Each 30-Hz
SMPL-X frame contains root translation and 55 joint ro-
tations in 6D, yielding 333 features. After normalization,
independent body, left-hand, and right-hand VAE branches
encode the corresponding channel groups into 128, 64, and
64 latent channels. Causal convolutions compress time by
a factor of three; concatenating the branches gives a 256-
dimensional, 10-Hz motion sequence. The codec is trained
with calibrated reconstruction and KL losses, with extra em-
phasis on root translation and rotation. We freeze it during
flow training, use posterior samples as motion targets, and
decode the generated latents back into SMPL-X motion.

4.4. Coupled Speech–Motion Flow Transformer
Native-rate dual streams.
The motion and audio la-
tents are projected into two separate Transformer streams.
Within each block, each stream first models its own tem-
poral context and reads its modality-adapted text condi-
tion. The streams then exchange information bidirection-
ally through audio-to-motion and motion-to-audio cross-
attention, with timestep-conditioned gates regulating the in-
teraction strength throughout denoising.
A common temporal coordinate.
For the 10-Hz mo-
tion latent sequence, token i corresponds to the interval
[i/fm, (i + 1)/fm), where fm = 10 Hz. For audio, the
patchifier maps each latent index back to a waveform-time
interval according to the sampling rate, mel-spectrogram
hop, temporal downsampling of the audio VAE, and causal
offset, forming a nominal 25-Hz latent clock. Given the dif-
ferent native token rates of speech and motion, we use the
midpoint of each interval to construct temporal rotary po-
sitional embeddings for within-modality self-attention and
bidirectional cross-modal attention.
For the same paired
motion and audio sample, we also share the flow time σ,
placing the two streams at the same denoising stage. The
shared clock, shared noise level, and bidirectional attention
together enable the model to learn synchronization from
paired data.

Training regimes. The model adopts a progressive two-
stage training regime. Stage 1 disables the motion stream
and cross-modal interaction, and trains the speech and pro-
nunciation pathways on speech-only data using audio flow
matching and phoneme-CTC supervision. Stage 2 activates
both streams and their bidirectional interaction, optimizing
both flow-matching losses and the phoneme-CTC regular-
izer on the expanded MotionSpeech conditioning instances.
To preserve the speech branch’s generalization ability, we
sample 10% of Stage 2 training batches from the Stage 1
speech-pretraining dataset.
Inference. Given a transcript, a voice description, and one
L1 or L2 motion instruction, we form cℓand encode it with
the text encoder to obtain the semantic condition. The tran-
script is also converted into a phoneme sequence through
grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) conversion and encoded by
the frozen phoneme encoder to provide the audio stream’s
phoneme context. For a target duration, we set the latent
lengths at the native motion and audio rates and initialize
both streams with independent Gaussian noise. The cou-
pled transformer jointly updates the two latent sequences
over a shared noise schedule from σ = 1 to 0 using 30 Eu-
ler steps with classifier-free guidance. Finally, the frozen
motion decoder reconstructs 30-Hz SMPL-X motion, while
the frozen audio VAE decoder and vocoder reconstruct a
16-kHz waveform matched to the target duration.

phoneme-progress anchor remains active during inference;
the auxiliary CTC heads are removed.
5. Experiments
5.1. Setup
Data.
MotionSpeech contains 34,950 12.9-second seg-
ments (approximately 125.2 hours),
including 17,907
BEAT2 clips and 17,043 Seamless Interaction clips. The
fixed split contains 27,957 training, 3,501 validation, and
3,492 test segments.
Baselines.
CosyVoice 2 [11] followed by MambaT-
alk [47] forms the cascade baseline.
Match-TTSG [37]
jointly synthesizes speech and gesture through mono-
tonic phoneme alignment and conditional flow matching.
Motion-Omni [36] is an end-to-end spoken-dialogue model
that jointly outputs speech and full-body motion.
Metrics. We use seven headline metrics. For speech,
WER is computed with Whisper large-v3-turbo [42], while
SpkSim is the cosine similarity between WavLM-base-plus-
SV [7] embeddings of generated and reference speech and
serves as a proxy for reference-speaker timbre similarity.
For motion, Fréchet Gesture Distance (FGD) uses the of-
ficial EMAGE/PantoMatrix AESKConv features [33], and
foot skating is the percentage of contact frames whose hori-
zontal foot speed exceeds 0.10 m/s. BC Gap is the absolute
mean-BC difference between generated and reference mo-
tions, both paired with reference speech. L1 and L2 Global
PairAcc use the same frozen CLaM-H text–motion evalua-
tor [8, 29].
Implementation details. DuoFlow uses eight coupled
Transformer blocks for both motion and audio, with width
1,024, 32 attention heads, and head dimension 32 in each
stream. Frozen modality codecs provide 256-channel mo-
tion and 128-channel audio latents. A frozen Gemma-3 en-
coder [14] supplies multi-level text features through cross-
attention, while residual connectors adapt them to the two
streams. A frozen AudioLDM2 phoneme encoder [32], the
progress anchor, and auxiliary CTC heads at all eight au-
dio blocks provide pronunciation and temporal supervision.
Stage 1 trains the audio stream on the Emilia [21] subset
for 100,000 steps, and Stage 2 jointly trains both streams
on MotionSpeech for 200,000 steps. Both stages use eight
A100 GPUs, 8-bit AdamW, a per-GPU batch size of 4,
2,000 warm-up steps, and cosine scheduling. The Stage 1
learning rate is 2×10−4; Stage 2 uses 2×10−4 for the motion
branch and 1×10−5 for the audio branch. Inference uses 30
Euler steps.

6. Conclusion
We presented MotionSpeech, a four-channel benchmark
that aligns a transcript and vocal-style description with
coarse- and fine-grained motion intents, together with
DuoFlow, a dual-stream latent flow framework for control-
lable joint speech–motion generation. The framework co-
ordinates native-rate speech and motion latents through a
shared noise level, a common second-based temporal co-
ordinate, modality-specific residual connectors, and layer-
wise bidirectional interaction. It strengthens speech tem-
poral awareness through a monotonic phoneme-alignment
pathway that combines a learned normalized progress an-
chor with training-only CTC supervision. The results show
that an end-to-end model can produce intelligible speech,
realistic and synchronized full-body motion, consistency
with both levels of motion instruction, and controllable vo-
cal delivery. This work establishes a unified interface for
multimodal motion and speech control, while improving
physical contact fidelity and extending the framework to
streaming generation remain future directions.
