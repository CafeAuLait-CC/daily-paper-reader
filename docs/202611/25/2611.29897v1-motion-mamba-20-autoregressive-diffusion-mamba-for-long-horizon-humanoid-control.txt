Motion Mamba 2.0: Autoregressive Diffusion
Mamba for Long-Horizon Humanoid Control
Yue Huang∗, Tianshan Zhang∗, Zeyu Zhang∗†, Jayeon Kang, Akide Liu,
Sungjoon Choi, Dacheng Tao, Fellow, IEEE, Hao Tang‡
Abstract—Long-horizon, real-time controllable motion generation is essential for interactive animation and humanoid robotics, where an
agent must respond to changing instructions and spatial goals while moving. Existing autoregressive diffusion methods generate motion
incrementally, but bounded attention caches limit access to distant history, while repeated denoising introduces substantial inference
overhead. We present Motion Mamba 2.0, a Mamba-based root–body diffusion architecture that integrates local attention, resumable
recurrent states, and persistent content memory (PCM) for long-horizon controllable motion generation. PCM transforms compressed
motion history from Mamba recurrent states, together with state changes and text-conditioned features, into dynamically updated content
slots accessible to local attention. To enable efficient inference, we further develop an execution scheme tailored to this architecture,
combining CUDA Graph replay, fused kernels, and execution-flow simplification while preserving the canonical history update. Evaluations
on retargeted HiPHI demonstrate accurate terminal control over rollouts of up to 39.52 s, while short-horizon comparisons on BONES
Seed and retargeted HumanML3D show that the model also retains strong spatial control and text–motion matching. On an RTX 4090, the
optimized execution reduces median complete-window latency from 1685.35 to 276.14 ms, achieving a median paired speedup of 6.138×
while preserving identical outputs and committed states in the tested rollouts. Moreover, we conduct extensive real-world evaluation on
the Unitree G1, demonstrating sequential text-guided action composition, expressive motion, and interactive locomotion through keyboard
and mouse control. Code: https://github.com/AIGeeksGroup/MotionMamba2.0. Website: https://aigeeksgroup.github.io/MotionMamba2.0.

Diffusion models produce diverse text-conditioned hu-
man motion [1], [2], [3]. Recent methods extend this for-
mulation through temporal composition, autoregressive
prediction, and direct spatial conditioning [4], [5], [6], [7].
Temporal composition allows multiple descriptions to define
a longer sequence, while causal latent models and hier-
archical motion tokens support incremental synthesis [8],
[9]. Spatially constrained methods further specify where
selected joints or the character should move [7], [10], [11],
[12]. In particular, generating one window at a time provides
a practical interface for online instructions. However, two
difficulties remain closely connected. A bounded attention
cache discards older motion features, whereas an unbounded
cache increases storage and computation as the sequence
grows. Moreover, repeated denoising of short windows
can incur substantial execution overhead even when each
individual model evaluation is small. An interactive system
therefore requires both a suitable representation of motion
history and an efficient implementation of its repeated
updates. These requirements concern different aspects of
the problem: reducing the number of sampling steps does
not extend the history available to the model, and a compact
historical state alone does not eliminate denoising overhead.
State-space models provide an alternative to storing every
historical token by summarizing a sequence in a recurrent
state [13], [14]. Their use in motion generation has shown
the value of efficient temporal modeling [15], [16]. Recent
studies also combine Mamba with autoregressive diffusion
or use recurrent temporal representations for sustained and
interactive motion [17], [18], [19]. Nevertheless, a compressed
recurrent state and an attention cache expose history differ-
ently: the former carries evolving context, while the latter
permits direct retrieval of explicit features. Simply placing
both branches in a network does not make the recurrent
history directly accessible to attention. This distinction
motivates a learned interface between accumulated recurrent
motion state and attention-accessible content, rather than
relying on either mechanism alone. Diffusion introduces an
additional requirement: successive noisy candidates describe
alternatives for the same output window, not successive
moments of the accepted motion stream. Persisting those
intermediate candidates would mix sampling iterations with
physical time. We therefore treat the boundary between
prediction and history commitment as part of the model’s
streaming formulation.
We propose Motion Mamba 2.0, an autoregressive dif-
fusion model for long-horizon, real-time controllable G1
motion. The model follows a root–body decomposition [7]:
explicit root predictions determine local trajectory features
for body generation. Within both denoisers, local attention
is combined with a resumable Mamba branch. Our per-
sistent content memory (PCM) learns to map compressed
Mamba state summaries, successive state changes, and
text-conditioned features into dynamically updated slots
accessible to local attention. The slots complement recent
motion features in the bounded cache and aim to sup-
port terminal spatial constraint satisfaction and command-
conditioned generation over extended rollouts; they are not
designed to reproduce arbitrary early instructions verbatim.
Recurrent states, cached features, and slots are committed
only after a clean motion window is accepted; intermediate
denoising candidates read the same immutable history
snapshot. The fixed-size recurrent state, cache, and PCM
slots bound stored context without guaranteeing retention
of every detail from arbitrarily distant motion. The accepted-
history separation also guides efficient inference. Read-
only denoising avoids materializing unused state proposals,
selective fusion reduces intermediate memory traffic, and
CUDA Graphs reduce repeated host dispatch [20], [21]. These
changes preserve root-to-body ordering and the canonical
clean-history update. Our objective is to accelerate the
same sampling computation, complementing methods that

A preliminary version of this work appeared in our
ECCV 2024 conference paper, Motion Mamba [15]. The
present manuscript substantially extends the conference
version in both methodology and experimental evaluation
in the following six aspects. (1) We reformulate the original
text-conditioned latent diffusion framework into an auto-
regressive root–body diffusion architecture for long-horizon
humanoid motion generation. Instead of synthesizing a
motion sequence as a single clip, the new framework gener-
ates successive 52-frame windows while accommodating
changing text instructions and time-indexed spatial con-
straints. (2) We redesign the temporal modeling architecture
beyond the original Hierarchical Temporal Mamba (HTM)
and Bidirectional Spatial Mamba (BSM) blocks. The new
denoisers integrate resumable Mamba-3 recurrence with
local attention and persistent content memory (PCM).
PCM converts accepted recurrent-state summaries and
their changes into gated content slots, providing attention-
accessible representations of compressed motion history
alongside the sliding cache. (3) We introduce an accepted-
only history update mechanism for autoregressive diffusion.
Intermediate noisy predictions read an immutable historical
snapshot, whereas the attention cache, recurrent states, and
PCM slots are updated together only after a clean motion
window is accepted. This separates diffusion sampling
iterations from the temporal evolution of generated motion.
(4) We develop a hardware-aware inference pipeline that
combines execution-flow simplification, custom fused kernel
and CUDA Graph replay across three guidance branches. (5)
We extend the evaluation from the original HumanML3D
and KIT-ML text-to-motion benchmarks to a unified G1
motion setting involving retargeted HiPHI, BONES Seed
and retargeted HumanML3D. The new protocol evaluates
spatial controllability, instruction matching, motion quality,
and inference efficiency over autoregressive rollouts of up
to 39.52 s. (6) We further extend our evaluation to physics-
based simulation and real-world deployment on a Unitree
G1 humanoid, assessing multi-stage text-guided actions
alongside interactive spatial control via keyboard and mouse.

RELATED WORK
2.1
Efficient Long-Horizon Motion Generation
Text-conditioned diffusion models learn expressive motion
distributions from paired language and motion data [1],
[2], [3]. Their representations and sampling procedures
determine both the cost of synthesis and how motion can
be extended in time. Discrete motion representations offer
a complementary formulation, with autoregressive token
prediction in T2M-GPT [27] and masked token completion
in MMM [28]. Longer sequences can be constructed by
composing action-conditioned segments, extending textual
context, or refining overlapping motion intervals [4], [29],
[30], [31], [32], [33]. Past-conditioned diffusion and temporal
motion fields further address continuity beyond isolated
clips [34], [35], [36]. In particular, Lagrangian Motion Fields
represent longer sequences through compact supermotions,
whereas FlowMDM blends positional encodings to improve
transitions between described segments [4], [36]. These
approaches make temporal composition more flexible, but an
interactive application additionally requires new conditions
to affect a stream that has already begun.
Autoregressive diffusion and accelerated sampling sup-
port incremental synthesis with lower per-update cost [5], [6],
[22], [23], [37]. Recent streaming methods introduce causal
representations, historical context, or spatial conditioning
to accommodate continuous generation [7], [8], [38], [39].
MOGO instead uses a hierarchical causal Transformer over
quantized motion tokens for real-time, extended genera-
tion [9]. These developments show that motion represen-
tation, temporal factorization, and sampling efficiency offer
complementary routes to reducing generation cost.
Controllability introduces a further requirement: trajec-
tory and joint targets must influence future motion while
preserving the current action. Guided Motion Diffusion
incorporates spatial constraints through representation de-
sign and diffusion guidance [10], while OmniControl com-
bines spatial and realism guidance to constrain different
joints at different times [11]. COMET studies extended
joint targets for long-term generation, while ARDY exposes
text and flexible kinematic constraints through a hybrid

representation [7], [12]. We build on ARDY’s explicit-root
and latent-body representation to study how recurrent state,
bounded attention, and persistent content can be integrated
into its denoising process. The accompanying execution

the architecture’s sampling and history-update semantics.
For a controlled comparison, all methods use independently
trained G1 adaptations with the same 100,000-update budget;
none uses its original pretrained release.
2.2
State-Space Models for Motion
Structured state-space models connect recurrent, convolu-
tional, and continuous-time sequence processing [40], [41],
[42], [43]. Mamba introduces input-dependent state updates,
and subsequent models extend selective scanning to visual
and multidimensional data [13], [44], [45], [46]. Mamba-2 con-
nects structured state-space models with attention through
state-space duality [47]. Mamba-3 improves the underlying
sequence model through state-space discretization and rotary
dynamics [14]. We use this scan as a temporal modeling
component; its recurrence is not a contribution of this work.
In motion generation, state-space models have been
applied to temporal and spatial dependencies, hybrid archi-
tectures, and text-conditioned synthesis [15], [16], [18], [48].
Memory-oriented variants also consider longer sequences
and the retention of earlier context [49], [50]. MADM
combines Mamba with autoregressive diffusion for text-to-
motion generation, while Dyadic Mamba and InterMamba
extend state-space modeling to interactions between peo-
ple [17], [19], [51].
Cross-segment memory has also been studied in sequence
models: Transformer-XL reuses earlier hidden representa-
tions through segment-level recurrence [52], while Recurrent
Memory Transformer propagates dedicated memory tokens
between segments [53]. Our focus is the interface between
compressed recurrent motion state and attention during
autoregressive diffusion, where intermediate noisy candi-
dates and accepted motion history require separate update
rules. PCM converts accepted state summaries, state changes,
and text conditions into dynamically updated content slots,
while a bounded attention cache preserves recent motion
features. These slots evolve with accepted motion rather than
remaining static, providing dynamically updated motion
context without explicitly storing arbitrary past tasks for
exact recall.
2.3
Humanoid Motion Tracking and Control
Humanoid control combines motion planning with the
physical constraints of balance, actuation, and contact [54].
In physics-based character animation, DeepMimic learns
control policies that imitate example motions while pursuing
task objectives [55]. Learning-based systems have advanced
whole-body teleoperation, human-motion imitation, and
expressive tracking [56], [57], [58], [59]. Retargeting methods
such as GMR [60] align human recordings with a robot em-
bodiment, but kinematic agreement alone does not establish
dynamic feasibility. Large-scale trackers such as SONIC [61]
provide an execution layer for diverse motion references.
This distinction between kinematic generation and physical
tracking is important: accurate reference poses do not by
themselves guarantee balance or appropriate foot contact
after execution.
Motion generation and physical tracking can also be
coupled within a control framework. CLoSD uses a diffusion
planner with simulated feedback for multi-task character
control [62], while OMG studies multimodal motion gener-
ation for humanoids [63]. In contrast, our work focuses on
generating continuous kinematic references from changing
instructions and constraints. These references are executed
by an existing tracking system, allowing us to evaluate
generation accuracy and inference cost separately from
the qualitative simulation and hardware demonstrations of
physical execution.

DATASETS AND RETARGETING
Learning a common motion generator from human and
robot recordings requires a consistent embodiment, temporal
resolution, and feature representation. We combine BONES
Seed [25], HumanML3D [26], and HiPHI [24] in a 29-DoF
G1 representation with 414 physical features at 25 Hz. The
resulting training pool contains 156,819 motion records and
588.92 stored hours, including admitted mirrored records.
Fig. 3 summarizes the corpus, quantifies lower-body target
alignment, and illustrates stored retargeted motions.
3.1
Dataset Construction
BONES Seed provides robot motion references, while Hu-
manML3D and HiPHI contribute human motion recordings
and their associated descriptions. For HiPHI, we select only
the high-precision human-motion subset and exclude the
object-interaction subset. We retarget the selected human
body motions to G1; object trajectories and object-interaction
supervision are not part of the resulting motion-generation
dataset. The training pool comprises 124,658 BONES Seed
records, 14,612 HumanML3D records, and 17,549 HiPHI
records. We retain the source captions, mirror identities,
and transformation provenance with each exported record.
The validation and test manifests contain 9,998 and 12,831
additional records, respectively, and are excluded from the
training pool. Counts include admitted mirrors; duration
measures stored frames rather than independent capture
time. The complete split statistics are provided in App. A.
3.2
Motion Retargeting
BONES Seed motions retain their official G1 trajectories
through resampling and representation conversion. For Hu-
manML3D and HiPHI, we use source-specific adapters built
on General Motion Retargeting (GMR) [60]. HumanML3D
motions are reconstructed from AMASS [64] parameters
using the SMPL-H body-and-hand model [65], whereas
HiPHI motions are reconstructed using BVH forward kine-
matics. Rest-pose transforms and pelvis-height scaling align
the human skeletons with the common robot asset. Root
translations and local rotations are resampled to 25 Hz before
retargeting. The solver combines position and orientation ob-
jectives under joint limits, followed by temporal refinement
and bounded ground and rate corrections. App. A details
the asset checks and conversion procedure.

3.3
Retargeting Assessment
We assess mapped-target alignment over all 18,525 admitted
HumanML3D and 19,503 admitted HiPHI records across
the training, validation, and test splits. For each clip, we
compute the 95th-percentile distance between a robot body
origin and its scaled, offset human target; paired ankles
use the worse side, and clips receive equal weight. Fig. 3(b)
shows the ankle and pelvis distributions for the complete
admitted cohorts, without selecting low-error clips. The cross-
clip median ankle residuals are 1.49 cm on HumanML3D
and 0.93 cm on HiPHI; the corresponding cross-clip 95th
percentiles are 3.37 and 2.39 cm. Median pelvis residuals
are 0.99 and 0.56 cm, respectively. These statistics quantify
alignment to the mapped lower-body objectives.
The stored examples in Fig. 3(c) illustrate leg extension,
stepping, and coordinated arm motion. Such pose-level
evidence and mapped-target position residuals describe
different aspects of retargeting: a target offset alone does
not establish loss of the depicted action, and recognizable
poses do not establish precise hand contact. Separately,
conversion between the stored motion formats and the
414-D representation has a maximum round-trip position
discrepancy of 1.31 × 10−6 m over the admitted human-
source records.

THE PROPOSED METHOD
As shown in Fig. 4, Motion Mamba 2.0 combines auto-
regressive root–body diffusion with local attention, resum-
able Mamba-3 states, and persistent content memory (PCM).
Local attention retrieves recent motion, while PCM supplies
attention-readable content derived from the accumulated
recurrent state. The local cache remains bounded, while
Mamba states and PCM slots carry forward information from
accepted motion windows. All historical states are frozen
during denoising and committed only after a clean motion
window is accepted.

4.3
Persistent Content Memory
The recurrent state and attention cache play complementary

4.4
State-Preserving Efficient Inference
Repeated diffusion denoising incurs overhead from kernel
launches, intermediate memory traffic, and unnecessary
state materialization. We address these costs through state-
preserving execution optimizations while maintaining root–
body dependencies and accepted-only history updates.
Read-only execution. During denoising, we avoid material-
izing state proposals and diagnostics that are not consumed
by either the prediction or a later commit. All recurrent
computation contributing to predicted motion is retained.
The canonical clean-history pass still constructs and commits
the complete accepted state.
Fusion Kernel. We fuse Mamba transition preprocessing,
including the decay activation, decay floor, step-size trans-
formation, and product ∆iAi, into a single traversal. In-
termediate values remain in FP32 registers before outputs
are written in the scan’s required layout. Compatible scale–
shift and gated-residual operations are also fused in the
execution variants that pass the numerical checks. The
attention operator and recurrent scan are retained. Exact
preprocessing and precision boundaries are given in App. B.
CUDA Graph replay. The root and body DiT sequence stacks
use separate CUDA Graphs. Within each stack, the text-
conditioned, spatially conditioned, and unconditional CFG
branches execute serially and are captured together. Input
projections, conditioning preparation, root-output process-
ing, root-to-local conversion, and body-input construction
remain outside these stack-level graphs. Stable input and
state buffers are refreshed before replay. Sampling updates,
random-number handling, recentering, and accepted-state
commits also remain outside capture. Graphs are rebuilt
when tensor layouts or parameter versions become incom-
patible; unsupported calls use the reference path.

4.5
Learning Objective
We optimize clean-window prediction with four terms. The
hybrid term Lhyb applies Smooth L1 to explicit root patches
and body latents. The decoded-body term Lbody applies L1
to reconstructed articulation. The goal term Lgoal applies L1
only to observed coordinates inside the generated window;
later goals remain conditioning inputs until their target
frames enter the output window. The consistency term LFK
measures Euclidean disagreement between joint positions
obtained from decoded rotations through forward kinematics
and those represented directly by decoded position features.
Each term is normalized over its applicable elements. Their
coefficients are denoted by λhyb, λbody, λgoal, and λFK:
L = λhybLhyb + λbodyLbody + λgoalLgoal + λFKLFK.
(15)
The codec is frozen during denoiser training. Histories, gen-
eration regions, and future observations use explicit validity
masks; outputs outside the generation region are excluded
from the supervised reduction. The common budget used
for architecture comparisons is specified in Sec. 5.

5.1
Experimental Setups
Training Procedure.
Motion Mamba 2.0 is jointly trained
with Mamba-3 and persistent content memory (PCM) en-
abled from initialization. The G1 motion codec remains
frozen, while cached text features provide conditioning.
Training minimizes the four-term objective in Eq. (15). We
use AdamW for 100,000 updates with a global batch size of
512 throughout training. The learning rate increases linearly
from 10−6 at update 1 to 5×10−5 at update 500, followed by
cosine decay to 10−5 at update 100,000. Gradients are clipped
to a maximum norm of 1.0. The generation length is fixed at
52 frames. History/future lengths (H, F) are sampled from
(0, 0), (52, 0), (196, 0), (0, 196), (52, 144), and (144, 52), with
respective frequencies of 2, 5, 4, 2, 4, 3 per 20 updates. The
maximum sampled training history is therefore 196 frames
(7.84 s).
Training samples are drawn from BONES Seed, retargeted
HumanML3D, and retargeted HiPHI in a 70:15:15 ratio.
Each sample contains a 52-frame generation window with
historical motion conditions constructed from ground-truth
sequences and sampled future constraints; training does not
autoregressively roll out generated histories. Text and spatial
condition dropout are both set to 0.1, with an empty spatial-
condition probability of 0.2. For fair comparison, all baselines
and architectural variants are independently trained on the
same G1 corpus under the matched 100,000-update budget
and batch-size schedule. All evaluations use the resulting
G1-adapted checkpoints rather than the original pretrained
releases. Further details are provided in App. C.
Evaluation Protocol.
Motion is generated at 25 Hz in 52-
frame windows (2.08 s). Retargeted HiPHI [24] is evaluated
over 1, 4, 9, and 19 windows (2.08–39.52 s), while BONES
Seed [25] and retargeted HumanML3D [26] use 1 and
4 windows. For each horizon and method, 128 held-out
initialization families and three noise seeds yield 384 rollouts
under paired evaluation conditions. HiPHI also shares initial
histories, spatial targets, and noise seeds across horizons,
while instruction schedules and terminal deadlines differ.
Each trial specifies a root target and one wrist or ankle target,
available from rollout onset with a terminal deadline; only
the active text instruction is provided during generation.
Sampling uses 10-step deterministic DDIM (η = 0) [77] with
uniformly spaced timesteps and text/spatial guidance scales
of (2, 2). Further details are provided in App. C.
Evaluation Metrics.
Root CE measures horizontal root-
position error in the world X–Z plane, while End CE
measures the 3D positional error of the constrained wrist
or ankle at the terminal deadline, both in centimeters. Com-
mand matching (Cmd.) evaluates coarse root-motion state
agreement. Text–motion alignment is evaluated by R@3 using
independently trained, frozen evaluators for each benchmark.
HiPHI uses 32 generated candidates, whereas BONES Seed
and HumanML3D use one matched generated candidate and
31 fixed real-motion distractors. R@3 is therefore comparable
within, but not across benchmarks. L2-FID compares distri-
butions of L2-normalized 256-dimensional motion features
from phase-paired 52-frame windows, including those in
extended rollouts. Latency is measured per accepted window
at batch size one. Metric aggregation and reference protocols
are detailed in App. C.

5.4
Inference Efficiency
We evaluate whether the proposed execution optimizations
reduce generation cost while preserving the complete PCM
model. All execution variants use identical frozen weights.
This study separates execution-flow simplification, local
A/DT kernel fusion, and CUDA Graph replay; it does not
change the denoising schedule or memory architecture.
Measurement protocol.
Experiments use one RTX 4090,
batch size one, ten denoising steps, three classifier-free
guidance branches with scales (2, 2), and four windows of
history. Attention remains FP32, the existing BF16 boundaries
inside Mamba are retained, and TF32 is disabled. Each timing
includes the complete accepted 52-frame window, from state
restoration through decoding and CPU snapshot/export.
Text embeddings are preloaded; loading, warmup, and graph
capture are excluded. We collect 30 matched window pairs in
each of five independent processes. Paired variants start from
identical session and random states. Confidence intervals
use 10,000 process-block bootstrap resamples. The timing
boundary and numerical checks are detailed in App. B.4.
Complete-window performance.
As shown in Tab. 7,
the complete scheme reduces p50 latency from 1685.35 to
276.14 ms and p95 from 2471.65 to 345.82 ms, reductions of
83.6% and 86.0%, respectively. The median paired speedup
is 6.138×, with a 95% confidence interval of [6.094, 6.290].
For a 2.08-s motion window, the optimized p50 corresponds
to a real-time factor of 0.133. This measures steady-state
generation, rather than cold-start or command-to-visible-
motion latency.

REAL-WORLD EVALUATION
While Sec. 5.2 assesses long-horizon spatial constraints
and command agreement in generated motion references,
Sec. 5.4 evaluates the cost of producing each motion win-
dow. We next examine whether these references support
continuous and responsive execution on a physical Unitree
G1. The experiments measure sequential text-guided action
composition, online spatial control, and physical response
in the integrated generation-and-tracking system. Fig. 8
presents representative robot executions alongside their Viser
references.
Evaluation setting. We conduct 20 trials each for sequential
action composition (T1) and expressive motion (T2), and 30
trials each for keyboard-controlled (T3) and mouse-controlled
(T4) locomotion. All conditions use the same motion gener-
ator, retargeting pipeline, and whole-body controller, with
paired initial states, random seeds, and command sequences.
Full-task success requires completion of all prescribed stages
without a fall, protective stop, or human intervention. We
additionally evaluate stage completion, command transitions,
tracking accuracy, stance slip, and response latency. The
model generates 52-frame windows at 25 Hz with four
windows of recent context. Detailed evaluation criteria are
provided in App. D.
Deployment architecture. The deployment comprises a
remote RTX 4090 server for text encoding and motion
generation, an intermediate workstation for communication
and interaction, and the onboard G1 controller for mo-
tion tracking. Generated motion references are transmitted
through the workstation via ZeroMQ and SSH tunneling at a
50-Hz transport rate, distinct from the model’s 25-Hz motion
representation. The SONIC controller tracks the received
references without executing the motion generator onboard.
Interactive timing. We distinguish handoff latency from
physical-response latency. Handoff latency is measured from
system receipt of a text or spatial command to completion
of the corresponding motion reference on the GPU server.
It includes motion generation, decoding, accepted-state
updates, and text encoding when a new text instruction
requires it, while excluding network transmission and robot-
side processing. Physical-response latency starts at the same
command receipt event and ends at the onset of sustained
motion satisfying the command tolerance on the G1, addition-
ally accounting for communication, buffering, and physical
execution delays. These measurements are distinct from the
cached-text inference timings in Sec. 5.4.
6.1
Motion Tracking with SONIC
We use SONIC [61] to track the generated motion references
in MuJoCo. Fig. 9 shows four instruction sequences, includ-
ing locomotion reversal, directional turning, and transitions
between articulated actions and walking. For each sequence,
the upper row shows physics-based execution in MuJoCo,
where the SONIC controller tracks the generated motion
reference and the resulting simulated motion is rendered.
The lower row shows a kinematic visualization of the same
reference in Viser: forward kinematics is used to update
the robot mesh, which is then rendered without controller
tracking or physics simulation. The two rows are shown at
matching timestamps to compare the simulated execution
with the reference motion. These examples qualitatively illus-
trate the transfer of generated motion sequences to a tracking
controller under changing instructions, complementing the
quantitative physical evaluation below.
6.2
Sequential and Expressive Motion
The T1 example contains changes in both global displacement
and articulated pose. Forward progression is followed by a
visible bow at 11.61 s and a raised-arm gesture at 28.83 s, with
intermediate changes in body orientation (Fig. 8, panel (a)).
T2 exhibits a different combination of arm elevation, torso
orientation, and lower-body configuration, extending to the
32.48 s sample (Fig. 8, panel (b)). These examples show that
the physical repertoire extends beyond a repeated walking
cycle. The robot and reference preserve recognizable action
structure, while differences in trunk inclination and foot
placement remain visible.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Our current framework focuses on controllable humanoid
motion generation and does not yet address object ma-
nipulation. Future work will focus on whole-body loco-
manipulation, coordinating locomotion with object manipu-
lation.

APPENDIX B
MODEL AND INFERENCE IMPLEMENTATION
B.1
Layer State and PCM Updates
Root and body layers use condition-modulated LayerNorm
before the sequence mixer and feed-forward sublayer. At-
tention and Mamba receive the same mixer features. The
Mamba state carries the accumulated phase, recurrent matrix,
preceding rotated state-input vector, and preceding value
vector. Phase and matrix accumulation use FP32; the latter
vectors follow the BF16 scan boundary. A session reset clears
these tensors, the K/V cache, PCM slots, and preceding
summaries together.
The PCM projector averages the recurrent matrix over the
state dimension, flattens head and value axes, and applies a
linear map and LayerNorm. Successive accepted summaries
define the state difference. The masked mean of valid text
embeddings supplies the write condition, independently
of diffusion time and heading. A linear projection and
LayerNorm form the text features; a linear conditioner
produces scale and shift parameters for the normalized state
difference. The summary, modulated difference, and text
features construct the candidate content in Eq. (11). A linear
decoder and featurewise LayerNorm produce slot proposals,
while a linear sigmoid writer also reads the previous slots
to predict the write gate in Eq. (12). The write-rate cap is
η = 0.25. Gates are broadcast over features, and slots are
updated once per accepted window. During denoising, all
guidance branches read the same committed cache, Mamba
state, slots, and summary.
Training-time context. The complete denoiser, including
PCM, is present from the beginning of training and is
optimized under the clean-window generation objective; no
separate PCM initialization or fine-tuning stage is used. Each
training example predicts a single 52-frame generation win-
dow conditioned on sampled clean ground-truth historical
motion, rather than on an autoregressively generated prefix.
Training does not unroll multiple generated windows or
perform cross-window truncated backpropagation through
time. During inference, by contrast, persistent states are up-
dated from accepted generated windows. The zero-initialized
PCM readout scale in Eq. (13) suppresses the additional
attention contribution at initialization; it does not imply
that PCM is inserted after a separately trained Native stage.
During joint training, the root-to-local conditioning path is
detached before body prediction. Body losses therefore do
not backpropagate through this conversion into the root
denoiser, which remains supervised by its own prediction
losses.

parameter versions become incompatible; unsupported calls
use the reference path.
B.4
Complete-Window Timing and Equivalence
The fixed-checkpoint study follows the hardware, preci-
sion, sampling, and five-process paired protocol in Sec. 5.4.
Complete-window timing includes state restoration, sam-
pling, accepted-state commitment, decoding, and CPU snap-
shot/export; text encoding, loading, warmup, and capture
are excluded. Speedup is the median paired ratio, with a
95% interval from 10,000 process-block bootstrap resamples.
Four seeded 100-window rollouts verify bitwise-identical
outputs and committed states. This cohort is independent of
the model-comparison timings.

C.1
Common Evaluation Conditions
All methods follow the paired protocol in Sec. 5.1: 128 held-
out initialization source families and three noise seeds per
family yield 384 rollouts per duration. Initializations and

future ground-truth motion is provided.
For HiPHI, the same 128 initialization families, initial

shared across horizons. These initialization families are
distinct from the phase-specific motion-reference families and
distinct-caption retrieval-query sets described below. Root
CE and End CE are averaged over all 384 terminal rollout
measurements. Cmd. is computed as the mean frame-level
coarse root-state agreement within each 52-frame window,
averaged over windows within each rollout and then over
all 384 rollouts. R@3 and L2-FID follow the window-level
aggregation described below.
Although initialization cases are paired across horizons,
the instruction-phase composition and window weights dif-

C.2
Training and Baseline Adaptations
Training configuration. Motion Mamba 2.0 is trained jointly
with Mamba-3 and PCM enabled from initialization. The
generator starts from randomly initialized parameters (initial-
ization seed 123), rather than an official pretrained generator
checkpoint; the shared G1 motion codec is frozen and text
features are cached. The model is trained for 100,000 opti-
mizer updates using AdamW with (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.999),
ϵ = 10−8, weight decay 0.01, and global gradient-norm
clipping at 1.0. The learning-rate schedule uses 500 warmup
updates from 10−6 to 5 × 10−5, followed by cosine decay to
10−5 over the 100,000-update schedule. The released training
recipe uses global batch size 512 throughout all 100,000
optimizer updates, totaling 51.2 million sample exposures.
There is no separate PCM pretraining or late-stage PCM
fine-tuning.
Training samples draw from BONES Seed, retargeted
HumanML3D, and retargeted HiPHI with probabilities 0.70,
0.15, and 0.15. The generation window contains 52 frames.
History/future-conditioning lengths (H, F) take the values
(0, 0), (52, 0), (196, 0), (0, 196), (52, 144), and (144, 52) with
respective probabilities 0.10, 0.25, 0.20, 0.10, 0.20, and 0.15.
Text dropout and spatial-constraint dropout are each 0.1,
and the probability of empty spatial conditioning is 0.2.
The supported spatial conditions are root trajectories, root
waypoints, full keyframes, joint positions, and joint rotations.
The largest sampled historical prefix is 196 frames (7.84 s),
shorter than the four-window 208-frame local cache. Thus,
this training protocol does not directly supervise retrieval
from model-generated histories beyond the local attention
horizon; longer-horizon behavior is evaluated through auto-
regressive inference.
Diffusion training uses clean-window (x0) prediction
with a uniformly sampled base diffusion timestep and Gaus-
sian forward noise. The base noise schedule is cosine, with
offset s = 0.008 and maximum β = 0.999; the number of
base diffusion steps is read from the loaded G1 configuration,
not specified by the ten-step DDIM inference schedule. The
four terms in Eq. (15) have unit coefficients. The hybrid
reconstruction term uses Smooth L1 with βSmoothL1 = 1 over
the 148-dimensional hybrid representation; decoded-body
and observed-goal terms use normalized L1, while forward-
kinematics consistency measures joint-position disagreement
in meters. The hybrid root/body subcomponents are diagnos-
tic reductions, not separately weighted losses. These settings
describe the common G1 diffusion/x0 objective and should
not be read as the objectives for every model family.
Controlled baseline adaptations. All compared methods
and architectural variants are independently trained on the
same G1 corpus under 100,000 optimizer updates and the
matched global batch-size schedule, using newly trained
100k checkpoints rather than their original released gener-
ator weights. Their objectives remain architecture-specific:
the shared G1 diffusion adaptations use the clean-motion
objective above, autoregressive action-code adaptations use
token cross-entropy, and MoLingo retains its masked-flow
velocity objective. These distinctions preserve the training
formulation of each model family instead of imposing
a diffusion objective on non-diffusion methods. The G1
adaptation and evaluation interface applies paired physical
constraints and matched data splits to all compared methods.
Minimal uses FP32 PyTorch SDPA with CUTLASS
memory-efficient attention and no fused-kernel or CUDA
Graph acceleration. Native configurations use their admitted
accelerated execution paths. Consequently, architectural
tables primarily compare quality and control; their latency
columns describe the complete evaluated implementations.
The fixed-weight component study in Sec. 5.4 separately
measures execution acceleration.
C.3
BONES Seed and HumanML3D Retrieval and Refer-
ence Distribution
BONES Seed and retargeted HumanML3D use separately
trained, frozen evaluators. For each query, R@3 ranks one
text-matched generated motion against 31 fixed real-motion

The HumanML3D evaluator uses three fixed candidate pools

accepted motions shorter than 10 s, limiting evaluation to
2.08 and 8.32 s. Under this protocol, R@3 measures reference-
anchored retrieval rather than retrieval among 32 generated
motions.
