Abstract
Foundation models (FMs) have advanced visual under-
standing and generation by providing semantically rich rep-
resentations. However, this role of FMs remains underex-
plored in human motion. Large motion datasets offer di-
verse movements at scale, but often lack action labels for
systematically evaluating learned representations on un-
derstanding tasks. Skeleton datasets enable such evalua-
tion through established recognition protocols, yet are typ-
ically smaller and focus on predefined actions captured
in controlled environments.
To combine these strengths,
we present MoSk, to the best of our knowledge, the first
Motion-Skeleton foundation model to integrate motion and
skeleton datasets.
MoSk learns shared representations
through self-supervised masked motion modeling. We in-
corporate joint-name embeddings to provide a common
anatomical reference across various skeleton formats. A
cross-attention decoder reconstructs masked motion from
the encoder’s features, with joint-name embeddings spec-
ifying the target joints.
Experiments demonstrate strong
action-recognition performance across diverse settings, in-
cluding linear evaluation and fine-tuning. MoSk also sub-
stantially improves motion generation across multiple back-
bones through representation alignment, reducing FID on
MotionMillion by up to 9.75 for unconditional generation
and 3.83 for text-to-motion generation.

including classification, segmentation, and object detec-
tion [20, 36].

Combining the strengths of motion data and skeleton
data, we introduce MoSk, a unified motion-skeleton foun-
dation model for human motion understanding and genera-
tion. To the best of our knowledge, MoSk is the first motion-
skeleton foundation model to learn shared representations
from both motion and skeleton datasets. We standardize
heterogeneous sequences into a common format of 3D joint
positions over time, integrating both sources into a single
training corpus. A single motion encoder is trained on this
corpus through self-supervised masked modeling [20].
Within this common format of 3D joint positions,
datasets retain their original joint sets, which differ in joint
counts and anatomical definitions. Inspired by SOfA [10],
we incorporate joint-name embeddings into motion encod-
ing to provide a shared anatomical reference across skele-
ton formats. In masked motion modeling, parts of each se-
quence are masked, and the encoder extracts features from
the remaining motion. A cross-attention decoder then uses
these features to reconstruct the masked motion, with joint-
name embeddings specifying the target joints. This masked
modeling objective encourages the encoder to learn seman-
tically meaningful motion representations that capture spa-
tial dependencies among joints and how their movements
change over time. The motion encoder trained through this
process serves as MoSk, providing a common foundation of
motion knowledge for both understanding and generation.
We conduct extensive experiments on both high-level ac-
tion understanding and low-level motion generation to ex-
amine MoSk’s transferability.
For skeleton-based action
recognition, a classification head is trained on representa-
tions extracted by MoSk. For motion generation, we align
the features of different generative backbones with MoSk
representations during training [60]. Each backbone is com-
pared with its counterpart trained without MoSk to assess
the benefit of representation guidance across model archi-
tectures.
MoSk’s representations are evaluated as direct
inputs for action recognition and as training supervision
for motion synthesis. MoSk outperforms dataset-specific
specialists on multiple benchmarks under linear evaluation
and fine-tuning, and also transfers to unseen skeleton for-
mats. In linear evaluation, it reaches 94.0% accuracy on
NTU RGB+D 60 (X-View) and 69.4% on PKU-MMD II.
MoSk guidance reduces FID in 15 of 16 settings across four
datasets and two backbones, by up to 5.67 (unconditional)
and 2.08 (text-to-motion) on RoMo.
Our contributions are threefold:
• We introduce MoSk, the first motion-skeleton foundation
model that learns shared representations from motion and
skeleton data through self-supervised training, and pro-
vides a single trained encoder for both motion under-
standing and generation.
• Motion and skeleton datasets are integrated into a unified
training corpus, increasing the scale and diversity of data
available for human motion representation learning.
• Extensive experiments demonstrate that MoSk outper-
forms dataset-specific specialists on multiple action-
recognition benchmarks and substantially improves mo-
tion generation across different generative backbones.
2. Related Work
2.1. Visual Representation Learning
Self-supervised representation learning. Self-supervised
learning (SSL) derives supervision from the data itself,
enabling representation learning without task-specific la-
bels. Contrastive learning learns visual representations by
bringing augmented views of the same image closer while
distinguishing different images [7, 19].
Masked autoen-
coding provides another learning objective, reconstructing
missing regions in images or videos from partial observa-
tions [20, 48]. Self-distillation approaches learn transfer-
able visual features by matching teacher and student outputs
across different views, with large-scale training further im-
proving their generalization [6, 36, 42]. Feature prediction
approaches learn semantic and temporal structures by pre-
dicting representations of missing image regions or video
segments from visible context [3, 4]. Together, these ap-
proaches provide a foundation for learning general visual
representations without task-specific annotations.
Visual representations for generation. Pretrained visual
representations support generative modeling through two
main directions: (i) alignment-based approaches use pre-
trained features to supervise generative features, as in Rep-
resentation Alignment (REPA) [60] and REPA-E [22], or
tokenizer latents, as in VA-VAE [59], improving training
efficiency and generation quality; (ii) another direction di-
rectly incorporates pretrained representation encoders into
autoencoding frameworks and trains generative models in
the resulting latent spaces [5, 65]. These studies demon-
strate the usefulness of visual representations both as train-
ing supervision and as generative latent spaces.

2.2. Human Motion Representation Learning
Skeleton representations for action understanding. Self-
supervised skeleton representation learning captures spa-
tial and temporal motion structures for action understand-
ing. Contrastive learning approaches encourage consistent
representations across augmented sequences and different
skeletal views [24, 28]. Masked prediction approaches learn
from incomplete observations by recovering joint motion
or predicting latent features [1, 11, 34]. Broader pretrain-
ing frameworks further explore representations that transfer
across multiple human motion understanding tasks [53, 69].
Motion representations for generation.
Motion gen-
eration methods learn motion representations to support
text-conditioned synthesis [8, 18, 23, 63].
Another line
of work connects motion representations with language
through cross-modal alignment and paired pretraining, im-
proving semantic consistency in text-conditioned motion
generation [27, 47].
Our MoSk learns representations
from both skeleton action and motion-generation datasets
through self-supervised learning, without action labels or
motion captions. These representations support both action
understanding and motion generation.
3. Method
MoSk is a motion-skeleton foundation model that learns
transferable representations from heterogeneous motion
and skeleton datasets through self-supervised learning.
Fig. 2 provides an overview of MoSk. First, input sequences
from various motion and skeleton datasets are standardized
to a common geometric and temporal format while retaining
each dataset’s joint set (Sec. 3.1). The encoder learns mo-
tion structure through masked motion modeling with joint-
name embeddings in input tokens and a learnable decoder
query (Sec. 3.2). The trained encoder provides represen-
tations for action recognition and feature-level supervision
for motion generation (Sec. 3.3).
3.1. Unifying Skeleton and Motion Data
We combine skeleton and motion datasets into a unified
training corpus. Training uses only the input sequences,
without action labels or motion captions. For an input se-
quence Sk from the k-th dataset, the corresponding prepro-
cessing operator Ak converts Sk from its original format
into a common 3D joint-coordinate format:
X = Ak(Sk) ∈RT ×Jk×Cin,
Cin = 3,
(1)
where Jk denotes the joint count of the k-th dataset. Specif-
ically, Ak normalizes the initial translation, heading, and
scale in a common coordinate system with the y-axis point-
ing upward, the ground plane at y = 0, and the initial fac-
ing direction along +z. It also samples T = 64 frames
from the input sequence Sk. This preprocessing standard-
izes the coordinate system and sequence length while pre-
serving each dataset’s original joint set, allowing skeleton
and motion datasets to be used together for training.

3.3. Understanding and Generation
MoSk (EM) consists of the trained encoder Eϕ and its in-
put embedding module, providing the integrated motion and
skeleton representations for understanding and generation.
Action understanding. For skeleton-based action recogni-
tion, we convert an input sequence Sk from the k-th dataset
into X = Ak(Sk) and predict action scores by using a linear
classification head as:
by = Linear(sg[EM(X)]) ,
(12)
where sg denotes stop-gradient and is omitted during semi-
supervised and full fine-tuning. For action label y, the clas-
sification head is trained with cross-entropy loss as:
LCE = −log [softmax(by)]y .
(13)
The classification head is trained using the target bench-
mark’s action labels, and the encoder remains frozen during
linear evaluation.
Motion generation. For motion generation, we use MoSk
as a frozen representation teacher following REPA’s rep-
resentation alignment approach [60]. This approach uses
representations of clean motion to supervise the generative
model’s intermediate features during denoising. Given a
clean motion sequence S0 from the k-th dataset, we obtain
X0 = Ak(S0) and extract teacher features as:
F = sg[EM(X0)].
(14)
Let Xτ be the noisy motion at generative time τ and c de-
note the conditioning information, with c = ∅for uncon-
ditional generation. We align the backbone’s intermediate
features hθ(Xτ, τ; c) with F through a trainable projection
by minimizing the alignment loss as:
LREPA = E[ℓalign(MLP(hθ(Xτ, τ; c)), F)] ,
(15)
where ℓalign denotes the feature alignment loss. The overall
training objective is
L = Lflow + λLREPA,
(16)
where Lflow is the backbone’s original generative loss and
the weighting hyperparameter is empirically set to λ = 0.5.
MoSk provides feature-level guidance only during training,
introducing no additional inference cost.

4.2. Implementation Details
Masking and augmentations.
Joint dropout is applied
with probability 0.5 and removes a randomly sampled frac-
tion of joints (0–40%) across all frames. Token masking
uses either uniform random masking or SLiM’s strategy,
each with probability 0.5. Including joint dropout, the over-
all masking ratio is 90% of the original skeletal-temporal
tokens. Data augmentation follows SLiM, with details in
the Appendix. Clips are resampled to T = 64 frames with
speed factors in [1/1.25, 1.25].
Architecture. We use a ViT encoder with 24 Transformer
blocks, a hidden dimension of 1,024, and 16 attention
heads. The decoder uses 4 Transformer blocks with a hid-
den dimension of 384 and 8 attention heads. A frozen CLIP
ViT-B/32 text encoder provides joint-name embeddings.
Training setup.
All encoder variants are trained for 75
epochs (93,750 optimizer steps), including an initial 4-
epoch linear warmup. Each epoch consists of 1,250 op-
timizer steps. We use AdamW [32] with a learning rate
of 4 × 10−4, (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.95), and a weight decay
of 0.05. Training uses bfloat16 (bf16) with gradient-norm
clipping at 3.0. For generation downstream tasks, the loss
weight is empirically set to λ = 0.5 for all experiments.

4.5. Ablation Study
We vary the sampling ratio between skeleton and mo-
tion data, and we ablate the masked-modeling components.
Generation ablations use MotionJiT. Component ablations
use MoSk-S, a smaller encoder variant described alongside
an encoder-scale ablation in the Appendix.
Data sampling ratio. Tab. 6 varies the probability pK of
sampling skeleton data. Compared with skeleton-only train-
ing, adding motion data at pK = 80% improves recog-
nition on NTU-60, NTU-120, PKU-MMD II, and ETRI-
Activity3D. Motion-only training yields the lowest mean
FID in both generation settings. We use pK = 80% to bal-
ance recognition and generation performance.
Training components. Tab. 7 shows that using anatomy
and side embeddings in both the encoder and decoder gives
the highest average accuracy, with a modest gain over omit-
ting text embeddings.
Random joint dropout improves
transfer to unseen formats, and sampling between random
and semantic tube masking improves average accuracy over
either strategy alone. Predicting frame-to-frame displace-
ment instead of raw coordinates has the largest effect, im-
proving average accuracy by 5.9%-point.
5. Conclusion
We introduced MoSk, the first motion-skeleton foundation
model that learns shared representations from motion and
skeleton data through self-supervised training. MoSk out-
performs the compared sensor-specific methods on all four
NTU fine-tuning protocols and transfers effectively to un-
seen skeleton formats. MoSk also improves both uncondi-
tional and text-to-motion generation, reducing FID in 15 of
16 settings across four datasets and two backbones.

A. Transfer to 2D Skeletons
Evaluation setting. MoSk’s self-supervised training uses
only 3D skeleton and motion sequences. We evaluate MoSk
for 2D skeleton-based action recognition on NTU-60 and
NTU-120, using 17 COCO keypoints estimated from RGB
frames by HRNet [44]. This 2D 17-joint format is unseen
during MoSk’s 3D training. We apply the same 2D coor-
dinate transformation to all frames within a clip and repre-
sent each joint by its coordinates (x, y, 0) and its anatomical
name. The encoder and a linear classifier are fine-tuned to-
gether on the labeled training clips of each split. We report
top-1 accuracy (%).
Results. Tab. 9 shows that MoSk outperforms the represen-
tation learning baselines in the second block across all four
evaluation protocols, with the largest gain of 2.1%-point on
NTU-120 X-Set. It also exceeds the fully supervised refer-
ences on three of the four protocols. The combined corpus
of motion and skeleton data consistently outperforms either
source alone, demonstrating MoSk’s ability to transfer rep-
resentations learned from 3D data effectively to an unseen
2D 17-joint skeleton format.

C. NTU Dataset Splits
Fig. 4 illustrates how we construct the NTU portion of
MoSk’s training corpus. Since NTU-60 is a subset of NTU-
120, we use only NTU-120 to avoid duplicate clips. We
retain clips in the intersection of the NTU-120 X-Sub and
X-Set training splits and exclude NTU-60 X-View test clips.
This yields 18,757 training clips, with verified absence of
overlap with the official test sets of all four NTU evaluation
protocols. SOfA [10] handles the NTU-60 X-View overlap
by removing clips from the test set to form X-View∗. MoSk
instead removes overlapping clips from training, preserving
the full official X-View test set.

We store each clip offline as a T × M × J × 3 tensor of
joint positions in meters, retaining the source joint layout
and body size. T denotes the number of frames, M ≤2
the number of people, and J the number of joints. Clips are
stored at their original frame rates and resampled to 20 fps
during data loading. Figs. 8–10 illustrate the processed joint
sequences across datasets.
Feature conversion for motion datasets. Motion datasets
including HumanML3D, KIT-ML, MotionMillion, and
RoMo provide per-frame features that need conversion to
3D joint positions using their reference implementations.

F. Implementation Details
F.1. Masking and Augmentations
We sample two 64-frame training clips from each source
sequence, with part of the original motion included in
both clips. Both clips share the same left–right flip deci-
sion, while playback speeds, bone-length scaling factors,
and small 3D rotations are sampled independently for each
clip.
Playback speeds follow a log-uniform distribution
over [1/1.25, 1.25]. Every four consecutive frames form
one temporal patch per joint, giving 16 patches per joint
in each clip. Joint dropout is applied with probability 0.5
per training batch, removing a random subset containing
0–40% of the joints from every frame.
Each clip then
uses either random masking or semantic tube masking from
SLiM [11], with equal probability. Random masking selects
individual tokens uniformly at random, whereas semantic
tube masking selects connected joints of the trunk or a limb
over consecutive temporal patches. Joint dropout and token
masking together hide approximately 90% of the original
non-padding tokens, with at least one token remaining vis-
ible. The decoder reconstructs the complete original joint
set, including joints removed by dropout.

the preprocessing operator Ak to obtain X = Ak(Sk). We
average the final output tokens of the MoSk encoder EM to

F.3. Motion Generation
Generator inputs.
MotionJiT [25] and MotionPiT [61]
use the datasets’ motion features: 263-D for HumanML3D,
251-D for KIT-ML, and 272-D for MotionMillion and
RoMo. Each feature channel is standardized using training-
set statistics.
MotionJiT groups four consecutive frames
into one token, whereas MotionPiT assigns one token to
each frame.
Both models predict clean motion features
from noisy inputs. For text-to-motion generation, frozen
CLIP [39] text encoders provide caption embeddings.
MoSk representation alignment. The 3D joint positions
of each clean training clip are resampled to 20 fps. Each
non-overlapping 64-frame segment undergoes the coordi-
nate alignment described in Sec. E before being encoded
by frozen MoSk. MoSk processes all valid tokens and pro-
duces one output token per joint for every four input frames.
Averaging the final-layer tokens across joints gives 16 tem-
poral target features per segment. When the generator in-
puts use a frame rate other than 20 fps, we linearly inter-
polate neighboring MoSk features to obtain a target at the
center time of each four-frame generator group.
Each MoSk target feature channel is standardized over
the valid temporal patches within its training clip.
Hid-
den states from the fourth generator block are mapped to
the MoSk feature space by a trainable projector. For Mo-
tionPiT, the four frame tokens in each group are averaged
before projection. The projected features are aligned with
the MoSk targets through LREPA, with alignment weight
λ = 0.5 in Eq. 16. MoSk and the projector are used only
during generator training.
Evaluation. HumanML3D and KIT-ML are evaluated us-
ing the dataset-specific text-motion evaluators of Guo et
al. [17]. For both MotionMillion and RoMo, we use the
released MotionMillion evaluator [13].

G. Limitations
Training corpus. Our corpus ablations compare training
on motion data, skeleton data, and their combination, with-
out isolating the contribution of individual datasets such as
NTU or RoMo. The extent to which each dataset benefits
action recognition and motion generation therefore remains
unclear. Training on individual datasets or excluding one
dataset at a time would help quantify these effects.
Encoder scale. Our default encoder, MoSk-L, has 304M
parameters, making fine-tuning demanding in both com-
putation and memory.
This limits its practicality under
constrained resources. Although smaller variants, MoSk-
S and MoSk-M, remain effective for REPA alignment, re-
ducing encoder size can lower action recognition accuracy
(Tab. 10).
Reducing the cost of downstream adaptation
while preserving performance across tasks remains a direc-
tion for future work.
