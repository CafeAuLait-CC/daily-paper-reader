Abstract
As generative models tackle larger and more complex
datasets, learned codecs enable efficient generation through
compact latent representations, but may lose details and
cannot guarantee exact preservation of given conditions.
Generating directly in the original data space avoids errors
introduced by the codec. Recent advances in image gen-
eration demonstrate that pixel diffusion can produce high-
quality samples at practical computational costs. Inspired
by these advances, we conduct a preliminary study of pixel
diffusion, demonstrating its potential for adaptation to mo-
tion generation.
Building on these findings, we present
RawMo, a framework that brings text-to-motion genera-
tion to raw motion space.
Our Motion Diffusion Trans-
former (MiT) models local skeletal structures and whole-
body dynamics, while refining motion across frames to im-
prove temporal consistency. Working directly in raw motion
space further allows our high-noise text-motion alignment
loss (HiNCE) to improve text consistency without interme-
diate motion decoding. RawMo supports multiple motion-
conditioning tasks with a single conditional model while
preserving given motion conditions exactly. Experiments
on HumanML3D and KIT-ML show the best text alignment
among compared methods and competitive motion quality,
with ablations confirming the benefits of MiT and HiNCE.

that reduce computation. An encoder compresses data into
latent codes, and a decoder converts generated latent codes

Adapting
pixel diffusion to motion requires modeling body-part re-
lationships and coordinated movement. Our Motion Diffu-
sion Transformer (MiT) extends PixelDiT’s dual-level de-

tency. During RawMo training, our high-noise text-motion

the early denoising steps. Fig. 2(b) illustrates how MiT and

Fig. 1 shows HumanML3D results. RawMo achieves the
strongest text alignment among compared methods and zero

reveal how motion structure and supervision timing shape
the balance between motion quality and text consistency.

2. Related Work
2.1. Pixel-space image diffusion
Pixel-space diffusion generates images directly without a
learned codec. Early diffusion models performed iterative
denoising in pixel space, but processing high-resolution im-
ages incurred substantial computational costs [5, 11]. La-
tent diffusion reduced these costs by moving denoising into
compressed representations, introducing dependence on a
learned encoder and decoder [29].
Recent work revis-
its pixel-space generation through suitable prediction tar-
gets, loss spaces, and efficient architectures [2, 18, 40].
JiT [18] separates prediction targets from loss spaces, show-
ing that clean-image prediction with a velocity-space objec-
tive enables plain Transformers to model high-dimensional
pixel patches. Architectural developments further separate
global modeling from local detail synthesis [2, 40]. Pix-
elDiT [40] combines patch-level semantic modeling with
Pixel Transformer (PiT) blocks for fine-grained pixel up-
2


dates, while complementary designs use lightweight local
refinement [2]. These developments revisit the prediction
task and computational structure of direct generation, moti-
vating raw-motion generation that accounts for body struc-
ture and temporal continuity.
2.2. Text-to-motion generation
Text-to-motion models generate human motion sequences
that follow textual descriptions.
Early diffusion-based
methods denoise motion sequences directly in motion
space [32, 46, 47], with clean-motion prediction enabling
geometric supervision on the generated sequence [32]. To
reduce generation costs, many approaches adopt compact
learned motion representations. Continuous methods per-
form diffusion or flow matching in an autoencoder’s latent
space [1, 17, 39], while discrete methods combine vector
quantization with autoregressive or masked token predic-
tion [9, 24, 41, 44]. Skeleton-aware latent designs further
capture relationships among joints, frames, and words [12].
Recent work also explores flow matching directly in motion
space, with representation-aware noise scaling [38]. Our
RawMo revisits direct motion generation through body-
region modeling, frame-level refinement, and high-noise
text-motion supervision informed by recent pixel diffusion.
2.3. Motion-conditional generation
Motion-conditional generation synthesizes motion under
partial observations, such as joint trajectories, keyframes,
or selected body parts. Existing approaches use inference-
time inpainting or spatial guidance [15, 32], compose diffu-
sion priors for joint-level control [30], or use learned control
branches [4, 37]. Other methods refine structured motion
latents through trajectory-based optimization [33], while
observation-conditioned denoising learns completion from
randomly sampled frame and joint observations [3]. Unified
frameworks further combine generation and editing through
a common formulation of source motion, conditions, and
target motion [10]. Prior work incorporates partial motion
observations during denoising [32] and uses sampling-time
projection to enforce exact linear spatial constraints [35].
Our RawMo preserves given motion conditions exactly us-
ing a single conditional model with a global skip.

4. RawMo
Building on the findings in Sec. 3, RawMo generates
text-conditioned motion directly in raw motion space us-
ing clean-motion prediction and a velocity-space objec-
tive. Fig. 3 provides an overview of RawMo. The Mo-
tion Diffusion Transformer (MiT) combines body-region
and frame representations in a text-conditioned backbone,
followed by joint-level reconstruction through a refinement
head (Sec. 4.1). HiNCE provides text-alignment supervi-
sion for clean-motion predictions at high-noise timesteps
(Sec. 4.2). A global skip preserves given motion conditions
exactly in each clean-motion prediction (Sec. 4.3).
4.1. Motion Diffusion Transformer (MiT)
MiT implements the denoising network fθ through a text-
conditioned backbone Bθ and a refinement head Hθ, where
θ denotes the trainable parameters. For a motion sequence
x ∈RT ×D with T frames and D motion features per frame
(D = 263 on HumanML3D; 251 on KIT-ML) [8], we con-
struct the noisy input xτ at timestep τ using Eq. 1. The
network receives a caption y as the text condition.
MiT constructs three token types from xτ: body-region
tokens, frame tokens, and raw tokens.
Body-region and
frame tokens enter the text-conditioned backbone Bθ, while
raw tokens take a separate path to the refinement head Hθ.
Text-conditioned backbone. Each body-region token rep-
resents one of K = 12 body regions over s = 4 consecu-
tive frames, providing local skeletal context within a short
temporal window. Each frame token represents all D fea-
tures of a single frame, retaining a sequence of whole-body
states for modeling temporal changes. The tokenization op-
erators Pregion and Pframe group the corresponding motion
features and linearly project them into initial region tokens
r0 and frame tokens f0, respectively, as:
r0 = Pregion(xτ) ∈R(T/s)K×C,
f0 = Pframe(xτ) ∈RT ×C,
(4)
where C denotes the backbone token dimension. Region
and frame tokens form the input motion stream [r0; f0],
where [·; ·] denotes token concatenation.
A frozen CLIP text encoder [28] encodes the caption y
into a global sentence-level embedding tg ∈RCtext and lo-
cal token-level embeddings tl ∈RL×Ctext, where Ctext is
the dimension and L is the token count. The global em-
bedding summarizes the entire caption, while the local em-
beddings provide detailed text context for joint attention
with motion. We combine the sentence-level embedding
and timestep into the global conditioning vector c as:
c = ψτ(embτ) + ψg(tg) ∈RC,
(5)
where embτ is the sinusoidal embedding of timestep τ, and
ψτ and ψg are separate multilayer perceptrons that map
4


their inputs to C-dimensional features.
The global con-
ditioning vector modulates each backbone block through
adaptive normalization, providing timestep and text context.
Following FLUX [16], the backbone processes motion
and text through dual-stream blocks followed by single-
stream blocks. Dual-stream blocks maintain separate pa-
rameters for the motion stream [r0; f0] and the local text
stream tl, while attention over both streams allows in-
formation exchange between motion and text.
Single-
stream blocks then process the concatenated motion and
text streams with shared parameters. After the final block,
we retain only the updated frame features f, defining the
backbone output as:
f = Bθ([r0; f0; ψl(tl)]; c) ∈RT ×C,
(6)
where ψl projects local text embeddings to dimension C.
The output frame features f incorporate body-region and
local text information, providing context for the refinement
head Hθ. The backbone uses rotary positional embeddings
(RoPE) [31] to encode the temporal position of each mo-
tion token. Region tokens use the center of their s-frame
interval, while frame tokens use their frame indices.
Refinement head.
Reconstructing each temporal patch
as a separate group of frames can introduce periodic ar-
tifacts at patch boundaries. Our refinement head Hθ ad-
dresses this issue by coordinating joint-level reconstruction
across frames through temporal attention.
We adapt the
Pixel Transformer (PiT) blocks of PixelDiT [40] to motion,
grouping joint features by frame. Along the raw-token path,
a joint-wise projection Pjoint embeds the noisy motion fea-
tures of each joint at each frame into raw tokens u0 as:
u0 = Pjoint(xτ) ∈RT ×J×Ch,
(7)
where J denotes the number of joints and Ch denotes the
head token dimension. The backbone frame features f are
combined with the global conditioning vector c to obtain
frame-wise conditioning cf as:
cf = c ⊕f ∈RT ×C,
(8)
where ⊕denotes element-wise addition after repeating c
across the T frames. Each frame’s conditioning vector mod-
ulates its joint features through adaptive normalization, sup-
plying Hθ with timestep, text, and motion context.
Within each head block, a squeeze operation compacts
the J joint features of each frame into one vector. Temporal
self-attention is then applied across the resulting T frame
vectors, which are subsequently expanded back into joint
features. A final linear projection maps the refined features
back to the original motion channels, producing the clean-
motion prediction ˆx as:
ˆx = fθ(xτ, τ; y) = Hθ(u0; cf) ∈RT ×D.
(9)
Grouping and conditioning operate per frame, while tempo-
ral attention coordinates updates across time.
4.2. Text Alignment at High Noise
We use our high-noise text-motion alignment loss (HiNCE)
to improve the text consistency of clean-motion predictions.
Before MiT training, we jointly pretrain a motion encoder
EM and a text encoder ET on paired training data. Through
a CLIP-style InfoNCE objective [22, 28], the two encoders
learn a shared embedding space for measuring how well a
motion expresses its caption. Fig. 3 (top left) illustrates this
pretraining stage.
During MiT training, EM and ET remain frozen and
serve as a fixed semantic reference for HiNCE. Working
in raw motion space allows HiNCE to supervise clean-
motion predictions directly, passing semantic feedback to
MiT without an intermediate motion decoder.
Let d(ˆx, y) denote a text-motion distance score between
a predicted motion ˆx and caption y, defined as the nega-
tive similarity score computed from EM(ˆx) and ET (y). To
compute HiNCE over a batch of N motion-caption pairs,
we sample a timestep τi for the i-th pair. The correspond-
ing clean-motion prediction ˆxi is paired with caption yi.
We apply alignment supervision at high-noise timesteps to
encourage the described action during the early denoising
stages. Since smaller τ corresponds to higher noise, we se-
lect the subset I = {i : τi ≤0.25}. For a nonempty I,
HiNCE is defined as:

conditional model with the observed motion xc ∈RT ×D
and a binary mask m ∈{0, 1}T ×D as additional input
channels. Unobserved entries in xc are zero-filled, and m
is 1 at observed entries and 0 elsewhere.
A global skip keeps the given motion unchanged and

is defined as:

5. Experiments
5.1. Datasets and Evaluation Metrics
Datasets. We evaluate RawMo on HumanML3D [8] and
KIT Motion-Language (KIT-ML) [27]. HumanML3D con-
tains 14,616 motion sequences and 44,970 descriptions (22
joints, 20 fps), while KIT-ML contains 3,911 sequences and
6,278 descriptions (21 joints, 12.5 fps). We follow the stan-
dard training, validation, and test splits [8].
Text-to-motion evaluation.
We report R-Precision
(R@1/2/3) for text-motion retrieval, Fr´echet Inception Dis-
tance (FID) for comparing generated and real motion dis-
tributions, and Multimodal Distance (MMDist) for the av-
erage embedding distance between paired motions and cap-
tions. On HumanML3D, we use both the standard 263-D
evaluator of Guo et al. [8] and the 67-D evaluator intro-
duced by MARDM [21]. The latter retains root motion and
local joint positions. We use both evaluators to verify our
performance across different motion representations. KIT-
ML uses only the 251-D evaluator of Guo et al.
Motion-conditioned evaluation.
We follow OmniCon-
trol [37] for pelvis control, reporting FID, R@3, Diversity
(Div.), and foot skating ratio (Skate). Diversity is better
when closer to the real-data reference, while Skate mea-
sures foot sliding during ground contact. Trajectory error
(Traj.) is the fraction of trajectories with any controlled-
frame error above 50 cm, while location error (Loc.) is the
fraction of controlled frames with errors above this thresh-
old. Average error (Err.) is the mean 3-D positional error
(m) at controlled frames. We also report FID, R@3, and
Div. under the 67-D evaluator.

(0.9, 0.95), and weight decay 0.01. The warmup–stable–

5.4. Motion-Conditioned Generation
Baselines.
Baselines cover inference-time control using
motion priors [15, 30, 32], learned control mechanisms [4,
37], and optimization or projection for constraint enforce-
ment [33, 35], alongside recent motion-control frame-
works [20, 26, 34].
Pelvis control. Pelvis control requires the generated full-
body motion to follow pelvis positions specified at selected
frames. We preserve height with the global skip and adjust
root velocities during sampling to match horizontal targets
(see Appendix). Table 4 reports results on HumanML3D
following the protocol of OmniControl [37], using a single
conditional RawMo model across all observation densities.
RawMo achieves 0 Traj., 0 Loc., and 0 Err., demonstrating
exact preservation of the specified pelvis positions. Com-
pared with MaskControl [26] under the 263-D evaluator of
Guo et al. [8], RawMo reduces FID from 0.061 to 0.040 and
improves R@3 from 0.809 to 0.849. Under the 67-D eval-
uator [21], RawMo also achieves the lowest FID (0.055)
and highest R@3 (0.835). These results demonstrate exact
spatial control alongside improved motion quality and text
alignment under both evaluators. Fig. 4 shows qualitative
results of our RawMo on text-to-motion generation and di-
verse motion-conditioning tasks.

6. Conclusion
We presented RawMo, bringing text-to-motion generation
back to raw motion. MiT captures skeletal structure and
temporal dynamics, while HiNCE improves text alignment
through clean-motion supervision at high noise. Experi-
ments on HumanML3D and KIT-ML demonstrate the po-
tential of direct raw-motion generation for high-quality,
text-consistent motion with exact condition preservation.

Datasets
We extend the HumanML3D and KIT-ML experiments to
two large-scale motion datasets, MotionMillion [7] and
RoMo [45], following their official splits. On both datasets,
RawMo directly generates the provided 272-D motion fea-

Each baseline is retrained separately on
each dataset using its official implementation and training
schedule for each stage. Checkpoints and, where applica-
ble, guidance scales are selected based on the lowest vali-

Table 7 reports results on both datasets. On MotionMil-
lion, RawMo ranks first on all five metrics, reducing FID
from 19.79 (MotionStreamer [36]) to 9.38 and MM-Dist
from 23.58 (CMDM [39]) to 21.18. On RoMo, RawMo

B. Motion-Conditioned Generation on Hu-
manML3D
We extend the main paper’s pelvis-control comparison to
the other eight motion-conditioning tasks in Fig. 4. Om-
niControl [37], MaskControl [26], and ACMDM [20] are
fine-tuned from their released control checkpoints on the
same mixture of nine tasks using their official training
code. These baselines receive the specified joint positions
in global coordinates.
Table 8 shows that RawMo achieves the highest R@3
and zero error at the conditioned joint positions on all nine
tasks. RawMo also achieves the lowest FID on eight tasks
and ranks second on sparse keyframes, with FID 0.033
compared with 0.024 for ACMDM. Averaged across tasks,
RawMo obtains FID 0.033 and R@3 0.837; the second-best
values are 0.044 for ACMDM and 0.813 for MaskControl,
respectively.
C. Ablations on KIT-ML
Table 9 extends the main paper’s HumanML3D ablations to
KIT-ML [27]. Removing the refinement head Hθ produces
the largest degradation in mean FID, from 0.118 to 0.177.
Without frame tokens f0, FID worsens to 0.150, compared
with 0.124 without body-region tokens r0.
The full KIT-ML model applies HiNCE at τ ≤0.25 with
weight λ = 0.02. Removing HiNCE lowers R@1 from
0.488 to 0.477. Applying HiNCE at all timesteps improves
R@1 to 0.495 but worsens FID to 0.132. Increasing back-
bone depth to 16 gives the lowest mean FID (0.115), with
57.5M parameters compared with 49.9M for the full model.
Overall, both datasets show similar trends when remov-
ing motion-modeling components or changing the HiNCE
timestep range.

Motion feature representation.
On HumanML3D and
KIT-ML, RawMo directly generates the motion features in-
troduced by Guo et al. [8]. For a skeleton with J joints,
each frame contains the root angular velocity about the ver-
tical axis, two planar root-velocity components, root height,
the positions and 6-D rotations of the J −1 non-root joints,
the velocities of all J joints, and four foot-contact indica-
tors. The non-root joint positions are expressed in the root’s
heading frame relative to its projection onto the ground
plane. This representation has D = 12J −1 channels per
frame: 263 for HumanML3D with J = 22 and 251 for
KIT-ML with J = 21. We standardize each channel using
training-split statistics. Root heading and planar position
are recovered by integrating the root velocities.
Network architecture. Table 12 summarizes the architec-
ture, training, and sampling settings. The backbone Bθ uses
dual-stream and single-stream blocks with an MLP ratio of
4, temporal RoPE [31], sigmoid gating on attention outputs,
and RMSNorm before and after each branch. The global
conditioning vector modulates each block through adaptive
normalization. Each body-region token pools three to five
joints over four frames and receives a learned region em-
bedding.
The 12 regions cover the pelvis, spine, chest,
head, and bilateral hips, legs, shoulders, and arms. Adja-
cent regions share a junction joint. Each frame token is
formed by projecting all D features through a rank-128 bot-

HiNCE implementation. HiNCE uses a motion encoder
and a text encoder. The motion encoder projects standard-
ized motion features to 256 dimensions, reduces temporal
resolution by a factor of four using two strided 1-D con-
volutions, and applies a four-layer, four-head Transformer.
The text encoder applies a two-layer Transformer to frozen
CLIP token features. Both encoders use mean pooling and
output unit-normalized 512-D embeddings.
We jointly train the encoders with symmetric In-
foNCE [22] and a learned temperature initialized to 0.07.
Training uses a batch size of 256 and a learning rate of
10−4, with 1,000 warm-up steps followed by cosine decay.
The training budget is 12k steps on HumanML3D and 6k
on KIT-ML. Checkpoints are selected by validation R@3,
choosing step 3k for HumanML3D and step 1k for KIT-
ML. Following TMR [23], we mask near-duplicate neg-
atives when the cosine similarity between the CLIP end-
of-text features of the negative and positive captions ex-
ceeds 0.9. During text-to-motion generator training, both
encoders remain frozen. The HiNCE loss is applied at high-
noise timesteps τ ≤0.25, excluding queries with dropped
text prompts and masking the same near-duplicate nega-
tives. To account for the motion encoder’s temporal stride,
we average the losses for the four input offsets 0, 1, 2, 3.
Motion-conditioning inputs. The conditional model re-
ceives the given motion features xc, their feature mask
m, and six root-conditioning channels.
The six root-
conditioning channels comprise two position channels, two
heading channels, and two availability masks. The position
channels encode the root’s horizontal position in global co-
ordinates as (x, z)/(2 m). The heading channels encode the
root heading angle θ as (cos θ, sin θ). One availability mask
indicates whether the root position is given, and the other in-
dicates whether the root heading is given. Zero-initialized
linear projections inject these conditions into the joint fea-
tures used for region pooling, the frame tokens, and the raw
joint tokens.

conditioned generation tasks. The temporal training tasks
correspond to Fig. 4(b,d,f,g) of the main paper: (b) the
first frame; (d) periodic keyframes with a stride of 2–32

Sampling. We apply classifier-free guidance to the flow ve-
locity and integrate from noise at τ = 0 to clean motion
at τ = 1. The sampling grid is obtained by applying the
same resolution shift used for training to uniformly spaced
noise fractions from 1 to 0.001. Across N sampling in-
tervals, Heun’s method [14] followed by a final Euler step
requires 2N −1 network evaluations. Text-to-motion guid-
ance scales are selected on the validation split.
H. Limitations
Zero-shot motion control. RawMo performs motion con-
trol using a conditional model trained separately from
the dedicated text-to-motion model. Enabling the text-to-
motion model to follow motion conditions without addi-
tional training remains an open problem.
Sampling latency. RawMo generates motion through itera-
tive denoising. As reported in Table 11, generating a single
HumanML3D clip on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 takes a me-
dian of 0.594 s. Future work could focus on reducing the
number of sampling steps while preserving motion quality,
with the goal of lowering generation latency and improving
responsiveness in interactive applications.
