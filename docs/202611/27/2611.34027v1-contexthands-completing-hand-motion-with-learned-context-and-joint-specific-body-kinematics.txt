Abstract
Detailed hand motion contributes to the realism and ex-
pressiveness of generated human motion. Completing these
details from body motion and language requires connecting
the context of an action to the articulation of the hands. We
introduce ContextHands, which completes hand motion from
an observed body sequence and text descriptions assigned to
hands and time intervals. Our approach learns probabilistic
hand features from the descriptions and body motion, using
auxiliary hand prediction to connect these features to hand
articulation. A body encoder preserves joint and body-part
features across multiple temporal scales, allowing each hand
joint to query these features for joint-specific body informa-
tion. The diffusion generator combines this joint-specific
body information with the learned hand context. Both en-
coders and the generator are trained jointly, so the final
generation objective also shapes the context features. Ex-
periments on the OakInk2 dataset show that ContextHands
generates more accurate hand motion and achieves lower
Fr´echet Hand Distance than existing hand-motion and gen-
eral human-motion generation methods adapted to the task.
In qualitative examples of grasping and tool use, Contex-
tHands more closely reproduces the detailed hand poses
in the reference motion. We will release our code and pre-
trained models to support further research.

Detailed hand motion is important for expressive and real-
istic human animation. Hands convey intent through ges-

tures and play a central role in everyday object manipulation
[21, 33, 49]. When a character points, holds a handle, or
uses a tool, wrist orientation and finger configuration help
specify how the action is performed. Convincing animation
therefore requires hand poses appropriate to the action, to-
gether with natural changes in articulation over time [28, 49].
Hand-motion completion generates these hand details to
accompany an already available body sequence.
Motion-generation datasets and representations often pro-
vide limited hand detail [49]. Hand-focused research ad-
dresses this gap with dedicated data, language annotations,
and generative models [48, 49]. Body-conditioned methods
exploit correlations between body and hand motion [21, 28],
but the observed body does not uniquely determine the hands:
similar arm movements can accompany different finger con-
figurations, and the two hands may move differently [28].
Language provides complementary information about ac-
tions, body-part configurations, and spatial relations [8, 48].
Together, body motion and language provide context for the
hand motion that accompanies an action.
This context can differ between hands and change over
time: one hand may remain open while the other closes and
later opens again. Descriptions therefore need not apply
to both hands throughout a clip. OakInk2 [42] provides
descriptions of manipulation events with hand-specific tem-
poral annotations. We use these existing descriptions and
their hand–time assignments as inputs for completion from
body motion. The central challenge is to translate the context
of each hand into detailed articulation while retaining access
to the body cues relevant to individual joints.
BOTH2Hands [48] learns separate body-to-hand and text-
to-hand generators, then blends their predictions. Prior-First,
Condition-Second [33] learns semantic adapters on a frozen
body–hand prior with structured attention. These approaches
combine the outputs of separately trained generators or adapt
an existing motion prior. ContextHands learns body-and-text
features for hand completion through two complementary
objectives: hand prediction links the features to articulation,
while joint training with the generator shapes them for the
final completion task. A Hand Context Encoder learns these
features from the assigned descriptions and body motion. We
model the features probabilistically because body motion and
language still permit different hand articulations [24, 34].
At each frame, the context feature is shared across the
joints of a hand. To let individual wrist and finger joints also
draw on different body cues, we retain a separate path for
joint-specific body conditioning. The Joint-Query Kinematic
Encoder preserves joint and anatomical-part features across
multiple temporal scales; each hand joint queries them for its
own condition. A single Hand Diffusion Generator combines
these conditions with the shared hand context to generate
rotations while keeping the observed body motion, including
wrist positions, fixed.
We evaluate ContextHands on OakInk2 and examine both
conditioning paths and joint versus staged training through
ablations. Our contributions are:
• A Hand Context Encoder learns probabilistic hand fea-
tures from body motion and text, guided by auxiliary hand
prediction and joint training with the generator.
• A Joint-Query Kinematic Encoder provides each wrist
and finger joint with body conditions drawn from joint,
anatomical-part, and temporal-scale features, complement-
ing the context shared within each hand.
• On OakInk2, ContextHands achieves the lowest joint-
position and wrist-rotation errors and the lowest FHD
among five existing methods adapted to the same comple-
tion task.
2. Related Work
Hand motion generation.
Body-conditioned methods ex-
ploit the relationship between body movement and hand
articulation. Body2Hands [21] predicts conversational ges-
tures from upper-body motion, while BHD [28] uses sepa-
rate hand branches and spatial and temporal memories to
model body–hand relationships. Language provides addi-
tional cues, but methods differ in how they combine these
conditions. BOTH2Hands [48] first trains body- and text-
conditioned generators separately, then blends their pre-
dictions through cross-attention.
Prior-First, Condition-
Second [33] instead learns semantic adapters on a frozen
autoregressive body–hand prior with kinematic-chain atten-
tion. Its kinematic attention uses queries derived from noisy
hand states. Our hand-joint queries are computed from the
observed body motion before denoising, while the body-and-
text context is learned jointly with the generator. Among ded-
icated text-to-hand models, HandMDM [3] uses descriptions
derived from sign-language data, whereas CLUTCH [37]
learns a hand-motion tokenizer and language model from
in-the-wild motion–text pairs. HandX [49] distinguishes
left-hand, right-hand, and inter-hand descriptions and also
supports wrist-trajectory constraints. These methods com-
plement joint body, hand, and facial motion generation from
speech [18, 40] or text [20]. Our task instead keeps the
observed body motion fixed.
Motion representations and generation.
Motion genera-
tion from text uses diffusion models [5, 36, 44, 46] as well
as autoregressive or masked prediction of discrete motion
tokens [10, 14, 27, 43]. A related question is how learned rep-
resentations connect language to motion. TEMOS [24] links
text and motion latent distributions through a shared motion
decoder, whereas MotionCLIP [35] aligns a motion autoen-
coder with the CLIP embedding space [29]. TMR [25] com-
bines motion reconstruction with contrastive text–motion
alignment to learn representations; MoLingo [11] uses frame-
level language supervision to learn motion representations.

Hand-specific supervision can also guide text-driven gen-
eration: TSHaMo [6] transfers guidance from a teacher with
auxiliary hand inputs during training to a student that uses
only text at inference. We instead supervise posterior context
features with auxiliary hand prediction. A shared decoder
also produces the prior features that condition our generator
at inference.
Controllable motion and interaction synthesis.
Control
beyond a sequence-level description can specify where or
when motion occurs. Spatial constraints guide diffusion in
GMD [15] and OmniControl [39], while FineMoGen [45]
uses spatiotemporal descriptions. KinMo [47] structures
language–motion alignment around global actions, kine-
matic groups, and their interactions. Complementary work
composes motion across time or body parts [1, 2, 26]. In-
teraction synthesis models relationships between the two
hands or between hands and objects. InterHandGen [16]
and HandDiffuse [17] model two-hand interactions. Text-
conditioned object interaction includes full-body synthesis
in IMoS [9] as well as hand-focused generation. For the
latter, DiffH2O [7] separates grasping from subsequent ma-
nipulation, while Text2HOI [4] predicts contact from text
and object geometry before generating motion. OakInk2’s
Task-aware Motion Fulfillment (TaMF) baseline [42] pairs
task descriptions with hand-specific interaction segments and
generates hand motion from object trajectories and geometry.
ContextHands instead completes hand motion from body
motion and provided descriptions, without object geometry
at inference or explicit contact modeling.
3. Method
3.1. Task definition
Given body motion B over T frames and descriptions T ,
we generate wrist and finger rotations while keeping the
body motion, including wrist positions, fixed. Assignments
A specify the hand and time interval for each description.
These inputs are available during training and inference;
target hand motion is available only during training.
Following Prior-First [33], we separate the given body
kinematics from the rotations to be generated. We model
p(H | B, T , A), where H ∈RT ×2×16×6 contains one wrist
and 15 finger-joint rotations per hand in continuous 6D
form [50]. These are local rotations relative to each joint’s
parent in SMPL-X [23]; wrist rotations are relative to the
forearm. We mask wrist-rotation features and exclude finger
rotations from the body inputs. Forward kinematics com-
bines the generated rotations with the given body motion to
obtain hand geometry.
3.2. Method overview
ContextHands combines context shared within each hand
with body cues specific to its individual joints (Fig. 2). The
Hand Context Encoder (HCE) learns framewise features
Ut,h ∈R96 for hand h, guided by auxiliary hand prediction.
The Joint-Query Kinematic Encoder (JQKE) reads body
information separately for each wrist and finger joint to
obtain C(B). A single Hand Diffusion Generator uses both
conditions to complete the hand motion.
Let c = c(B, T , A) denote the encoded text–body con-
text. The generative process is
z ∼pϕ(z | c),
U = Dη(c, z),
H ∼pθ(H | C(B), U).
(1)
Here, pϕ, Dη, and pθ are the conditional prior, feature de-
coder, and diffusion generator. The two paths have separate
body encoders and are trained jointly with the generator.
3.3. Hand Context Encoder
To follow descriptions assigned to different hands and inter-
vals, the Hand Context Encoder maintains a feature for each
hand and frame. A Transformer body encoder [38] produces
framewise features for each hand, while a trainable attention
layer aggregates token features from a frozen DistilBERT
encoder [32]. The assignments A place each description
feature at its corresponding hand and frames, with zero text
features where descriptions are absent. Adding the body and
text features gives context c (Fig. 2).
Body motion and descriptions do not uniquely determine
hand articulation. To represent variation compatible with
these conditions, we adopt a conditional variational formula-
tion [34]. Learned queries pool c across hands and time to
predict means and variances for each hand, defining a Gaus-
sian conditional prior pϕ(z | c) with diagonal covariance.
One sample zh is shared across that hand’s frames. The
feature decoder adds a projection of zh to c and applies at-
tention across hands and time to produce U. The latent thus
represents variation at the sequence level, while c supplies
framewise body and text information.
During training, an approximate posterior qψ(z | c, H)
is additionally conditioned on the target hand motion H to
guide latent learning. Prior and posterior samples, zp and zq,
pass separately through the same feature decoder:
U p = Dη(c, zp),
U q = Dη(c, zq).
(2)
The features U p and U q condition separate passes through
the same generator with the same noisy hand input, diffu-
sion step, and C(B). The generator thus learns from both
posterior features conditioned on the target hand motion and
the prior features available at inference.

Generation losses train both the prior and posterior paths,
while KL regularization couples their latent distributions.
The generator uses the features directly, not the predicted
PCA coefficients, and uses only U p at inference.
3.4. Joint-Query Kinematic Encoder
To provide body cues for individual wrist and finger joints,
JQKE retains joint features alongside anatomical-part and
temporal-scale summaries. Each target joint can thereby
access both individual body joints and broader body structure
and motion.
Spatial attention exchanges information among body
joints within each frame, with learned biases for anatom-
ical relationships. To capture motion over different time
ranges, three temporal branches use short-range convolution,
dilated convolutions, and sequence-wide attention. Each
retains a feature for every joint and frame. Their gated com-
bination is projected and added to the spatial features to form
Ft.
Alongside these joint features, learned pooling of Ft
within anatomical groups gives part summaries Pt. Scale
summaries St are obtained by averaging the output of each
temporal branch over body joints at each frame and project-
ing the result. For each t ∈{1, . . . , T}, the bank is

3.5. Hand Diffusion Generator
The generator combines the two conditions while retaining
a token for each wrist and finger joint. Hand rotations are
standardized with training-set statistics and corrupted by
noise, then projected into tokens with joint, side, and frame
embeddings. Before the first block, a projection of Ut,h is
added to all 16 joint tokens of hand h at frame t, providing
them with the same hand context.
Each denoiser block incorporates Ct,j and the diffusion-
step embedding through a residual MLP, then applies tempo-
ral and within-hand self-attention followed by a feed-forward
update. Joint-specific heads predict the clean rotations ˆH0.
Supervision includes geometric losses computed through
forward kinematics, as in MDM [36].
We use a 100-step cosine noise schedule [22]. At infer-
ence, DDPM [13] starts from Gaussian noise independent
of the HCE latent and generates rotations over 100 reverse
steps, with U and C(B) fixed throughout.
3.6. Training
Following the hybrid training formulation of Sohn et al. [34],
we supervise both prior- and posterior-conditioned predic-
tions with the following objective:
L = αLp
hand + (1 −α)Lq
hand
+ λrecLcoef + βDKL(qψ∥pϕ).
(5)
Superscripts p and q denote prior- and posterior-conditioned
predictions. Both hand losses compare local rotations us-
ing 6D reconstruction and geodesic errors, and supervise
positions obtained through forward kinematics, rotational
increments, and temporal changes in those increments. The
auxiliary loss supervises posterior-pass PCA coefficients.
The KL term, averaged over latent coordinates, regularizes
the posterior toward the conditional prior. The weights λrec
and β control auxiliary supervision and KL regularization.
Only the prior is available at inference, so we use a prior-
conditioned reference loss to adapt the balance α between
the two hand losses. This loss uses sampled diffusion steps
on training recordings excluded from the current batch. We
follow the virtual-update approach of Ren et al. [31], adapt-
ing a global loss weight rather than per-example weights. To
estimate a finite-difference gradient for α, we take one vir-
tual update at each of two perturbed weights, both from the
same saved state. We restore the model and optimizer states
before applying the updated weight to the actual training
step.
4. Experimental Setup
Dataset.
We use OakInk2 [42], which pairs body–hand
motion with descriptions assigned to specific hands and time
intervals. We split the official training partition into 308
training recordings (3,842 clips) and 70 validation record-
ings (728 clips), and use the official test partition of 157
recordings (1,709 clips). Recordings are disjoint across
splits. We process the motion at 30 FPS and divide each
recording into clips of at most 150 frames. All methods
receive the complete body sequence and available text anno-
tations. We retain frames and clips without text annotations;
descriptions cover 51.69% of valid test hand–frame pairs.
Baselines.
We compare with MDM [36], MLD [5], T2M-
GPT [43], BOTH2Hands [48], and the diffusion variant of
HandX [49]. We adapt their released implementations to
generate wrist and finger rotations from the same body mo-
tion, descriptions, and explicit hand–time assignments. Our
adaptations address the task’s inputs, outputs, and validity
masks, retaining each method’s core architecture, pretrained
text encoder, and original training and sampling procedures
wherever compatible. HandX uses T5-base [30], while the
other baselines use CLIP [29]. All motion networks are
trained from scratch on the same OakInk2 training split.
Adapted methods are marked with *.
Prior-First [33] is not included in our OakInk2 compar-
ison because its training code was unavailable when we
conducted the experiments.
Implementation details.
All methods use a 24-hour train-
ing budget on a single RTX 4090. We train ContextHands
using AdamW [19] and retain method-specific training pro-
cedures for the baselines. Following the validation-FID
selection used by T2M-GPT [43], we select each final-stage
generator checkpoint by the lowest validation FHD within
this budget. All test metrics use the same selected checkpoint
and fixed inference settings.
Evaluation metrics.
We generate sixteen sequences per
test input (K = 16) from each selected checkpoint. We
report minimum and average mean per-joint position error
(MinMPJPE and AvgMPJPE) over 42 hand points, includ-
ing the two given wrist positions, in millimeters. MinMPJPE
uses the complete sequence with the lowest error for each
clip (best of K), whereas AvgMPJPE averages errors over all
K generations. Hand positions are obtained by combining
generated rotations with the given body motion through for-
ward kinematics, without rotation or scale alignment. Both

Joint-Query Kinematic Encoder.
The part and temporal-
scale summaries complement the joint features in JQKE
(Table 2). Preventing the hand-joint queries from attending
to these summaries reduces position accuracy and increases
FHD. We also test whether the body conditions need to
remain joint-specific by averaging them within each hand

Training strategy.
We compare joint training with two
staged strategies that introduce text after body-only pretrain-
ing (Table 3). Both start from the same body-only check-
point: one trains only the text input module, while the other
also fine-tunes the motion model. Full fine-tuning improves
upon text-module-only adaptation. For our architecture and
evaluated training schedules, however, joint training pro-
duces more accurate hand motion with lower FHD than
either staged strategy.
Text conditioning at inference.
The descriptions help
guide hand articulation beyond what the body sequence
alone provides. With the trained model and body motion
fixed, both removing and shuffling descriptions reduce agree-
ment with the reference hand motion (see supplementary
material). Shuffling preserves the hand–time assignments,
so the benefit depends on the description content, rather than
only on which hands and intervals are annotated.
5.3. Qualitative comparison
We compare ContextHands with existing methods in Fig. 3.
Our method generates hand poses that closely resemble the
reference and better captures the articulation of individual
fingers. For example, it reproduces the extended index finger
with the neighboring fingers bent in both lid grasping (a) and
cutting (b). MLD also captures the index extension in (a),
but does not maintain it across the displayed frames, while
MDM extends a different finger from the reference in (c).
In the object views, ContextHands more closely matches
the reference finger configuration around the knife handle
in (b) and the rod in (d). The more open configurations
of MLD in (b) and BOTH2Hands in (d) leave larger visible
gaps around the displayed objects. These wrist-aligned views
compare finger configuration; wrist-orientation accuracy is
evaluated separately in Table 1.
6. Discussion and Limitations
Body motion helps form the context of a hand action and
guides individual joint rotations. The HCE body-input abla-
tion supports this role: removing its body input makes the
generated hands less accurate even when JQKE still pro-
vides body conditions to the generator. Under the evaluated
schedules, jointly training both encoders and the generator
from the start also outperforms introducing text after body-
only pretraining. These findings support learning context for
the completion task, while leaving open which body cues
account for the gains across different hand actions.
ContextHands requires a complete body sequence and
supplied hand–time assignments; its use of future frames
limits it to offline completion. Our evaluation focuses on
detailed hand motion during bimanual object manipulation
in OakInk2, using the dataset’s annotated descriptions. Eval-
uating other activities and freely written descriptions would
help assess its broader applicability, while user studies could
complement the quantitative results by assessing motion
naturalness and agreement with the input descriptions.
ContextHands does not explicitly model object geometry
or hand–object contact. Incorporating visual observations
and contact constraints is a promising direction for extending
ContextHands to object-aware hand motion generation.
7. Conclusion
We introduced ContextHands, a jointly trained model for
completing hand motion from body motion and descrip-
tions assigned to hands and time intervals. The model com-
bines hand context learned from body motion and text with
joint-specific kinematic conditions in a single diffusion gen-
erator. Experiments on OakInk2, which captures detailed
hand motion during bimanual object manipulation, show
more accurate hand motion than the evaluated methods. The
FHD results further suggest that the generated motions better
match the distribution of real hand motion, while qualitative
comparisons illustrate finer finger articulation.
