Abstract
Humanoid motion generation must accommodate evolving
task requirements, where localized adjustments should sat-
isfy new geometric constraints without disrupting desired
motion details.
However, language-based control lacks
geometric precision, while joint-level constraints require
explicit spatial targets. Local geometric constraints are
also underdetermined, complicating target satisfaction and
source preservation. We present AGLR (Attribute-Guided
Latent Refinement), a unified framework for fine-grained
humanoid motion generation and editing through source-
anchored refinement. A compact geometric interface sup-
ports composable, time-varying control of limb extents,
torso orientation, and root trajectories. Given a gener-
ated or encoded base latent, an Attribute Residual Adapter
predicts a single-pass correction, with dual-level supervi-
sion enforcing target matching and selective preservation
across attributes and motion coordinates. On HumanML3D-
G1, AGLR achieves the highest controllable-generation and
preservation-constrained editing success rates among eval-
uated methods. Editing take only 9.1 ms for a cached 500-
frame motion, without additional sampling or test-time opti-
mization. Zero-shot evaluation on BONES-SEED-G1 further
demonstrates transfer without fine-tuning.

1. Introduction
Humanoid robots operating in dynamic environments must
adapt their motions to changing task requirements. Recent
methods generate diverse whole-body motions from natural
language instructions [6, 16, 19, 40, 47, 48], providing ref-
erences for motion tracking policies. Many tasks, however,
require only localized modifications to an existing reference.
For example, a humanoid may need to raise one arm while
walking or adjust its torso orientation while maintaining its
natural gait. Such tasks call for fine-grained motion editing
that modifies selected geometric properties while preserving
the rest of the reference motion.
The first challenge lies in specifying geometric edits
with both precision and compositionality. While semantic
control can describe high-level actions for individual body
parts [34, 52], it offers limited direct control over the mag-
nitude and timing of a geometric change, such as precisely
how far an arm should extend forward at each phase of a
step. Conversely, kinematic control specifies spatial targets
through joint positions [17, 35, 49], full-body poses [7, 41],
or task-space trajectories [23, 38, 45]. Depending on the edit,
these inputs often demand burdensome kinematic details be-
yond what the task naturally specifies. This gap motivates a
part-level geometric interface that supports numerical, time-
varying targets and enables seamless composition of edits
across different body parts.
The second challenge is to satisfy these targets efficiently
while faithfully preserving the details of the source motion.
Multiple whole-body trajectories can satisfy the same set
of geometric targets; for instance, prescribing the forward
extent of one arm during walking leaves the gait and the
movement of the opposite arm underdetermined. Existing
source-conditioned editors [2, 11, 29] leverage a reference
trajectory to guide the transformation, where unselected at-
tributes and motion coordinates are preserved. However,
diffusion-based editors [2, 22, 42] often require iterative
sampling, whereas optimization-based methods [24, 30] rely
on repeated gradient updates, significantly increasing in-
ference latency. These limitations highlight the need for
a framework that directly predicts motion corrections in a
single forward pass, while providing robust supervision for
both target satisfaction and source preservation.
In this work, we introduce Attribute-Guided Latent
Refinement (AGLR), a framework for fine-grained geo-
metric adaptation of generated or existing humanoid robot
reference motions. AGLR specifies edits through the Whole-
body Geometric Attribute Representation (WGAR), a 21-
dimensional per-frame descriptor capturing directional limb
extents, torso orientation, and root motion. A channel-wise
mask dictates which attributes to edit, allowing time-varying
targets to be combined across body parts. To preserve source
motion details left underdetermined by the selected targets,
AGLR refines the source trajectory within a robot-native
latent space learned from dynamics-grounded motions. An
Attribute Residual Adapter (ARA) predicts an additive
correction to the source latent in a single forward pass. The
training objective encourages the edited motion to satisfy the
selected targets while preserving unselected attributes and
motion coordinates. Finally, a downstream motion tracking
policy can use the refined trajectory as a reference to execute
the motion on a physical humanoid robot.
We evaluate AGLR on HumanML3D-G1 for controllable
generation and preservation-constrained editing. AGLR
achieves state-of-the-art success rates among compared
methods, reaching 80.34% for controllable generation and
76.81% and 77.28% for editing under attribute and joint
preservation constraints, respectively. Furthermore, latent
refinement and decoding take merely 9.1 ms for a 500-frame
motion. Zero-shot evaluations on BONES-SEED-G1 demon-
strate robust cross-dataset transfer without fine-tuning. Fi-
nally, real-world experiments on a Unitree G1 humanoid val-
idate that AGLR can selectively and reliably modify whole-
body motions during physical execution.
2. Related Work
Human and Humanoid Motion Generation. Existing
human text-to-motion generation typically relies on vari-
ational models [32], diffusion processes in motion or la-
tent spaces [5, 25, 44, 53], and discrete motion tokens [10,
20, 51]. Extending this paradigm to humanoid robots re-
quires representing motions in the robot’s configuration
space. HARMON [21] transfers generated human motions
via kinematic retargeting, whereas these works [19, 40, 48]
directly generate robot-space motion references. Humanoid-
X [31] and SCHUR [47] use video-derived motions to ex-
pand training data, while OMG [16] broadens the condition-
ing interface with multimodal inputs. Building upon robot-
space motion generation, AGLR employs a text-conditioned
latent flow model to generate a base motion for subsequent
geometric refinement.
Fine-Grained Motion Control. Prior work enables body-
part control through part-aware representations [15], simul-
taneous part-level actions [1, 26], spatial and temporal com-
position [34, 52], and part-specific instructions [4]. Lan-
guage describes the intended actions, while spatial control
interfaces provide numerical targets for trajectories [23, 45],
poses or motion regions [7, 41], and selected joint posi-
tions [17, 35, 49]. Some works [38, 54] combine language
with pose and path constraints. These interfaces offer precise
control over specified locations, but controlling a limb’s over-
all directional extent still requires translating that goal into
point-level targets. Our geometric interface instead exposes
directional limb extents, torso orientation, and root motion
as compact, directly controllable attributes. Attributes can
be selected, combined across body parts, and varied over
time without specifying complete joint trajectories.

Motion Editing. Motion editing adapts a source motion
to new spatial or semantic constraints while preserving its
characteristics.
MotionFix [2] and SimMotionEdit [29]
learn instruction-guided edits, MotionLab [11] unifies mo-
tion generation and editing, and SALAD [13] uses attention
for fine-grained semantic control. TRACE [22] preserves
source coordinates through reference projection, while Uni-
MoFlow [14] refines source latents using conditioned ve-
locity differences. Other methods [24, 30, 45] iteratively
optimize motion latents or diffusion noise via repeated gradi-
ent updates, which add inference cost. MotionCLIP [43] and
MoDi [36] support direct latent editing, but their semantic
controls offer limited support for precise geometric targets.
3. Method
Problem Formulation. We investigate fine-grained geomet-
ric adaptation of humanoid whole-body motions. Given a
base motion Xb, target attribute sequence A ∈RT ×K, and
a binary channel mask M ∈{0, 1}K, our goal is to gen-
erate an adapted motion ˆX that satisfies the selected target
attributes while preserving unselected attributes and other
motion details. Here, T counts valid frames and K denotes
the number of attribute channels. Formally, the adaptation
process is expressed as ˆX = H(Xb, A, M), where H de-
notes the geometric adaptation function.
Overview. To achieve this task, we propose AGLR, a uni-
fied framework for fine-grained geometric adaptation of hu-
manoid whole-body motions. As illustrated in Fig. 2, AGLR
operates across three stages.
Stage 1: Robot-native Motion Prior Learning. We first
train a robot-native variational autoencoder (VAE) on physi-
cally plausible motions to learn a compact latent represen-
tation tailored to the robot’s kinematic structure. Building
upon this representation, we further learn a latent flow prior
to capture the underlying distribution of robot motions.
Stage 2: Efficient Base Motion Generation. To reduce
the cost of base motion generation, we distill the pretrained
latent flow model into a few-step generator, enabling efficient
sampling while retaining the learned motion prior.
Stage 3: Attribute-guided Latent Refinement. To facil-
itate fine-grained geometric adaptation, we introduce two
components: Whole-body Geometric Attribute Representa-
tion (WGAR) and Attribute Residual Adapter (ARA). Specifi-
cally, WGAR provides an interpretable and compositional
interface for specifying geometric targets across different
body parts, while ARA performs attribute-conditioned re-
finement in the robot-native latent space. By introducing
target-specific geometric adjustments in the latent space,
ARA aims to satisfy the specified attributes while preserving
non-target motion details.
During inference, AGLR first obtains an initial motion
latent either from the distilled generator or by encoding an
existing motion with the pretrained VAE encoder. Given
(A, M), ARA then refines the initial latent to produce an
attribute-consistent representation, which is subsequently
decoded by the VAE to obtain the adapted motion ˆX.
3.1. Whole-body Geometric Attributes
Robot Motion Representation. We represent humanoid
robot motions using trajectories generated by a privileged
tracking policy in simulation [28]. Similar to ECHO [19], we
adopt RPR39 as per-frame motion representation, denoted as
xt ∈R39. It consists of 29 joint angles, two heading-local
horizontal root displacement increments, one root height,
one yaw increment, and a 6D heading-local root rotation

root trajectory from the displacement and yaw increments,
and subsequently apply forward kinematics to recover the
3D positions pt,j ∈R3 and orientations Rt,j ∈SO(3) of
each body element j at frame t.

the generator or obtained by encoding a source motion. ARA
takes zb, the target attributes A, and the selection mask M

3.4. Efficient Generation and Deployment
Latent Flow Prior and Distillation. We employ a text-
conditioned latent flow model to generate base motion la-
tents. To accelerate inference, we distill the pretrained flow
model into a four-step student using DMD2 [50]. The dis-
tilled generator is frozen and provides base motion proposals
for ARA, ensuring consistency between training and infer-
ence distributions. Details of the VAE, latent flow model,
and distillation procedure are provided in the supplement.
Latent Refinement and Cached Editing. Given a base
latent zb, obtained either from the distilled generator or
by encoding an existing motion, ARA performs attribute-
guided refinement according to the target attributes A and
selection mask M (Eq. 4). This process requires only one
ARA forward pass and one VAE decoding pass, without
additional flow sampling. Moreover, the base latent can be
cached and reused for successive edits.
Robot Deployment and Motion Tracking. For deploy-
ment, given a source motion Xb, we obtain its base latent
through the pretrained VAE encoder, i.e., zb = E(Xb), and
refine it using the same attribute-guided process. The re-
sulting motion ˆX serves as a reference for a downstream
tracking policy, which generates control actions to execute
the edited motion on the robot. This decouples geometric
adaptation from closed-loop motion execution, allowing the
same tracking policy to handle different geometric edits.
4. Experiments
We evaluate AGLR across three core tasks: text-to-motion
generation, fine-grained controllable generation, and source-
conditioned motion editing. Complete training, evaluation,
baseline adaptation details for controllable generation and
motion editing are provided in the supplement, alongside ex-
tended editing comparisons, ablation studies, and additional
hardware demonstrations.

4.1. Experimental Setup
We use HumanML3D-G1 as the primary benchmark. To as-
sess cross-dataset generalization, we evaluate models trained
on HumanML3D-G1 in zero-shot on BONES-SEED-G1.
HumanML3D-G1. We construct HumanML3D-G1 by retar-
geting HumanML3D [9] motions to Unitree G1 and tracking
them in simulation with a pretrained privileged policy [28]
to mitigate retargeting artifacts. After filtering rollouts with
large tracking errors, we preserve the original splits, retain-
ing 19,728 training and 3,766 test samples. Motions use
RPR39 at 50 Hz, with normalization statistics computed
using the training split.
BONES-SEED-G1.
We use the official G1-retargeted
motions from BONES-SEED [3] and track them in sim-
ulation using the same pretrained privileged policy as for
HumanML3D-G1. For zero-shot evaluation, we randomly
sample 1,000 resulting rollouts from each of the content and
repetition test splits, yielding 2,000 evaluation motions.
4.2. Text-to-Motion Generation
Baselines. We compare AGLR with G1-native methods
(ECHO [19] and TextOp [48]) and methods adapted to G1
(MDM [44], MoMask [10], and MotionHiFlow [27]). All
methods use the same motion representation, dataset split,
and evaluation protocol. To evaluate our generator in isola-
tion, we use text as the sole input to both the 50-step teacher
and the four-step student and bypass ARA in both cases.
Metrics. We report R-Precision (R@1, R@2, and R@3)
with 32 retrieval candidates, Fr´echet Inception Distance
(FID), Matching Distance (MD), and Diversity (Div.). We
compute all four metrics using the same frozen G1-specific
TMR [33] evaluator. End-to-end inference latency (Lat.) is
measured in milliseconds and includes text encoding, sam-
pling, decoding, and pose conversion.
Quantitative comparisons. In Table 1, the 50-step teacher
achieves the lowest FID and MD and the highest R-Precision
at all three cutoffs. Distillation reduces end-to-end latency
from 1,206.3 to 55.2 ms, a 21.85× speedup. The four-step
student outperforms all external baselines on FID and R-
Precision and matches the best baseline (MotionHiFlow [27])
on MD metric. We use the four-step student to generate base
motions for subsequent experiments.

4.7. Tracking and Visualization
We use the edited motions as references for a tracking policy.
Fig. 5 compares the original motion with three edited execu-
tions at five time points. The robot raises its left arm in Fig.
5(b), raises and extends it outward in Fig. 5(c), and combines

at the sampled time points. These examples illustrate that
the robot can execute attribute edits while largely preserving
the motion of unedited body parts. Additional real-world
demonstrations are provided in the supplement.

5. Conclusion
We presented AGLR, a unified framework for fine-grained
humanoid motion generation and editing that enables compo-
sitional, time-varying control over limb extents, torso orien-
tation, and root trajectories. The Attribute Residual Adapter
refines a generated or encoded motion latent in a single pass.
Attribute- and motion-level supervision jointly guide the
model to match target attributes while preserving unselected
attributes and untargeted motion coordinates. Experiments
show that AGLR compares favorably with adapted baselines
in control accuracy and motion preservation, supports effi-
cient editing, and transfers across motion sources without
fine-tuning. Limitations are discussed in the supplement.

A. Real-World Motion Editing and Tracking
We demonstrate attribute-guided motion editing on a phys-
ical Unitree G1 humanoid robot. Figures S3–S10 show di-
rectional limb adjustments, simultaneous control of multiple
body parts, editing with time-varying targets, and modifi-
cations to root trajectories and heading. Each figure com-
pares execution of the base reference motion with execu-
tions of its edited variants.
From Geometric Requests to Robot Execution. Given a
base motion Xb, we encode it into a latent representation
zb = E(Xb) and provide ARA with the selected attribute
targets over time. For an offset-based request, we define the
target as At,k = Ab,t,k + δk(t), where Ab = WGAR(Xb)
and δk(t) denotes the requested offset for attribute k at time
t. Each request jointly specifies all active targets. Repeated
requests for the same base motion reuse the cached latent rep-
resentation zb. The resulting edited reference is executed on
the physical robot using the PPO-based tracking policy from
MimicLite [39], conditioned on the robot’s current state.
Results. Figures S3–S10 illustrate editing results using three
base motions: Base 0 for limb, torso, and time-varying at-
tribute editing; Base 1 for root trajectory editing; and Base 2
for turning while marching. Limb offsets are dimensionless,
while torso offsets are expressed in radians. Arm and leg
directions are defined in the torso and pelvis frames, respec-
tively, with positive Out pointing away from the body on
both sides. Root yaw editing activates all three root-rotation
channels, and the 180◦and 360◦turning requests specify
full heading trajectories over time. These qualitative results
demonstrate that the edited references can be executed on the
physical robot to produce the requested geometric changes
while preserving the overall structure of the base motions.

B.2. Editable Motion Variables and Preservation
Table S3 lists the motion variables in raw-space represen-
tation (RPR39) that can be modified for each selected at-
tribute. The same selection applies to all frames in the se-
quence. When multiple attributes are selected, a motion
variable is editable if it belongs to the editable set of any
selected attribute. Selecting root orientation also allows
root displacement to change, since displacement increments
are expressed in a heading-local frame and therefore de-
pend on the root heading.
We encourage preservation at both the motion-coordinate
and attribute levels. The coordinate-preservation objective
encourages all motion variables outside the editable set to
remain close to the base motion, with differences measured
in standardized RPR39 space. The attribute-preservation
objective encourages unselected attributes to retain their
base values, including those that depend on editable mo-
tion variables. For example, selecting an arm’s Up attribute
allows the corresponding arm joints to change while en-
couraging its Front and Out attributes to remain unchanged.
Attribute preservation compares the edited and base motions
in WGAR space, with each attribute computed in its corre-
sponding reference frame as defined above.

C. Motion VAE Architecture and Training
C.1. VAE Architecture
The motion VAE uses a temporal encoder–decoder archi-
tecture with approximately 15.5M parameters. The encoder
and decoder contain three and two Transformer blocks, re-
spectively, operating at the compressed temporal resolution.
Each block uses pre-layer normalization, eight-head self-
attention, and a residual feed-forward network with hidden
dimensions 384 →1536 →384 and GELU activation [12].
Separate residual temporal-convolution blocks accompany
the downsampling and upsampling stages. The encoder
downsamples the motion sequence in two stages, reducing
its length by a factor of four, and maps the resulting fea-
tures to 64-dimensional latent tokens. Thus, a 500-frame
motion sequence is encoded as 125 latent tokens. The de-
coder reconstructs the motion from these tokens through two
temporal upsampling stages, restoring the original sequence
length and motion feature dimension.

C.4. Training Details
We train the VAE on the HumanML3D-G1 training split for
800 epochs, using motion sequences of up to 500 frames.
The global batch size is 2,048. We use the AdamW optimizer
with a target learning rate of 10−4, betas (0.9, 0.99), and
zero weight decay. The learning rate increases to 10−4 over
the first five epochs and then follows a cosine decay schedule
to 10−6. Gradients are clipped at a norm of 1.
D. Generator Architecture and Training
D.1. Teacher Motion Generator
The motion generator is a text-conditioned latent flow model
that operates in the latent space of the frozen motion VAE.
Given a noisy motion latent zτ, flow time τ, text condi-
tion c, and motion duration T, the model predicts a latent
velocity with the same shape as the input, L × dz. Dur-
ing generation, this velocity field progressively transforms
Gaussian noise into a motion latent, which is then decoded
using the frozen VAE decoder.
Generator Architecture. The generator uses a Transformer
backbone with 12 blocks, a hidden dimension of 768, and
12 attention heads. An input MLP projects the noisy latent
tokens into the hidden feature space, while learned positional
embeddings encode their temporal positions. Each Trans-
former block contains bidirectional self-attention, cross-
attention to the conditioning tokens, and a feed-forward
network with an expansion ratio of four and GELU acti-
vation. Flow time modulates the hidden features through
adaptive layer normalization. After the Transformer blocks,
a time-conditioned output head maps the hidden features
to latent velocities.
Conditioning Mechanisms. Text features are extracted us-
ing a frozen CLIP ViT-B/32 [37] encoder and projected to the
Transformer hidden dimension through a trainable two-layer
MLP. Motion duration is encoded through a sinusoidal em-
bedding followed by an MLP to produce a single duration to-
ken. We concatenate the text tokens and duration token along
the token dimension to form the cross-attention context:
Cflow = Concat (Ctext, clen) ,
(S17)
Flow time is embedded separately and used for adaptive
layer normalization. Padding masks exclude invalid motion
and text tokens from attention.

We adopt DMD2 [50] to distill the flow teacher into a four-

Both the student and the auxiliary
fake-distribution network use the teacher’s architecture and
are initialized from its exponential moving average (EMA)
weights. The teacher, CLIP encoder, and text projector re-

D.3. Training Details
Teacher Training. We train the latent flow teacher for
80,000 iterations while keeping the VAE frozen. We use
AdamW with betas (0.9, 0.99) and zero weight decay, and
clip the gradient norm at 1. The learning rate increases to
10−4 over the first 1,000 updates and remains constant there-
after. The global batch size is 128 across four GPUs, with
32 samples per GPU. For evaluation, we use exponential
moving average (EMA) weights with a decay of 0.9999.
Distillation Training. We train the student for 10,000 up-
dates, performing five auxiliary-network updates for each stu-
dent update. The flow times used to evaluate the distribution-
matching objective are sampled uniformly from [0.02, 0.98].
We set the weight of the student’s adversarial loss to 0.03
and that of the auxiliary network’s discriminator loss to 1.
Both the student and auxiliary network use AdamW with
betas (0.9, 0.99) and zero weight decay. Their learning rates
increase to 10−5 over the first 100 updates, and their gra-
dient norms are clipped at 1. The global batch size is 128
across four GPUs, with 32 samples per GPU. We maintain
student EMA weights with a decay of 0.999. For each stu-
dent update, we uniformly select one of the four sampling
stages for backpropagation. The preceding stages generate
its input without gradient tracking, so gradients propagate
only through the selected stage.
E. ARA Implementation and Training
E.1. ARA Architecture and Optimization
ARA Architecture. ARA predicts a latent residual from the
base motion latent and the requested attribute controls. It first
encodes these inputs into motion tokens and control tokens,
respectively. A Transformer then updates the motion tokens
through cross-attention to the control tokens. The updated
motion tokens are mapped to a residual, which is added to
the base latent before decoding with the frozen VAE decoder.
The attribute encoder takes a 44-dimensional vector at
each frame, consisting of 21 masked standardized target
values, 21 selection-mask entries, a normalized frame index
t/(T −1), and a duration feature (T −1)/500. The selection
mask is shared across frames. Three temporal convolutional
layers with channel dimensions 44 →256 →256 →768
and GELU activations [12] produce token embeddings. Two
stride-2 layers reduce the temporal resolution by a factor of
four to match the motion latent sequence. Invalid temporal
positions are masked at each resolution.
The base latent is projected to 768-dimensional motion to-
kens, to which learned positional embeddings and a duration
embedding are added. These motion tokens pass through
eight Transformer blocks, each containing 12-head self-
attention, cross-attention, and a two-layer feed-forward net-
work. Self-attention captures temporal relationships among
motion tokens, while cross-attention uses motion tokens
as queries and control tokens as keys and values to incor-
porate the requested edits.

Training Details. After completing 10,000 student distilla-
tion updates, we freeze the motion generator and train ARA
for 40,000 updates. The VAE remains frozen throughout
ARA training. The training proposal set contains two sam-
pled proposals for each of the 9,974 source motions in the
HumanML3D training split. Selection masks and target per-
turbations are resampled during training. We set the branch
weights to λGT = 0.5, λID = 0.25, and λEdit = 0.25, and
set λq = 0.1. We optimize ARA using AdamW with a batch
size of 1,536 and weight decay of 10−4. The learning rate in-
creases to 2×10−4 over the first 250 updates, then follows a
cosine decay schedule to 4 × 10−5, or 20% of its peak value.

frame to the sampled signed value at the last frame. For

sequence. Each offset is expressed in the corresponding
channel’s native units.

and exclude padded frames from supervision. The result-

hard constraints.

G. Editing Latency Measurement
We measure cached-refinement latency as the time required
for ARA refinement and VAE decoding, starting from a
cached base latent corresponding to a 500-frame motion.
Measurements are performed on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX
4090D GPU with a batch size of one. We use three fixed
validation motions. For each motion, we run ten warmup
requests followed by 30 timed requests, synchronizing the
GPU at the timing boundaries. Timing ends when the de-
coded RPR39 output is available on the GPU. Across the 90
timed requests, the mean latency is 9.103 ms, reported as 9.1
ms in Section 4.4 of the main paper. The end-to-end editing
latency is 13.15 ms and additionally includes source-motion
processing and output conversion.
H. Baseline Adaptation and Training
H.1. Text-to-Motion Baselines
MDM. We adapt MDM [44] to RPR39 by modifying its
input and output layers to process 39-dimensional robot
motion features, while retaining its original diffusion de-
noising objective. We train the model for 80,000 updates
with a global batch size of 128.
ECHO. For ECHO [19], we replace its native motion rep-
resentation with RPR39 while retaining its temporal U-Net
architecture and clean-motion prediction objective. Train-
ing uses a 1,000-step diffusion process with a linear noise
schedule. The reconstruction loss is the mean squared er-
ror over valid frames and motion features, averaged within
each sequence and then across the batch. We train ECHO
for 50,000 updates with a global batch size of 128, using
AdamW with an initial learning rate of 2 × 10−4 and weight
decay of 10−2. Gradient norms are clipped at 1, and EMA
weights are used for evaluation.
MoMask. We adapt MoMask [10] to RPR39 by first training
a residual vector-quantized motion tokenizer, then training
its masked and residual token predictors using the original
cross-entropy objectives. The tokenizer uses a Smooth-ℓ1
motion reconstruction loss, a commitment loss with weight
0.02, and an auxiliary G1 FK position reconstruction loss
with weight 0.5. We train the tokenizer for 80,000 updates
with a global batch size of 256, and each token predictor for
60,000 updates with a global batch size of 64. All models use
AdamW with a learning rate of 2 × 10−4, betas (0.9, 0.99),
and 2,000 warmup updates. Weight decay is zero for the
tokenizer and 10−5 for the token predictors. The resulting
robot-motion tokenizer and token predictors also provide
the initialization for MaskControl.
TextOp. We adapt TextOp [48] to robot motion while re-
taining its history-conditioned motion VAE and autoregres-
sive latent diffusion model. At each rollout step, the model
conditions on two history frames and predicts eight future
frames. For text-only generation, it starts from a fixed G1
neutral pose and uses generated motion as history for sub-
sequent steps. We train the VAE for 40,000 updates using
Adam, followed by 60,000 diffusion-model updates using
AdamW. Both stages use a global batch size of 1,024 and
a learning rate of 10−4.
MotionHiFlow. We adapt MotionHiFlow [27] to robot mo-
tion while retaining its motion VAE and text-conditioned
latent flow model. The VAE compresses the temporal res-
olution by a factor of four, and the flow model uses 20
sampling steps at inference. We train the VAE for 40,000
updates with a global batch size of 256, followed by 50,000
flow-model updates with a global batch size of 64. Both
stages use AdamW with a learning rate of 2×10−4 and zero
weight decay. For evaluation, we use the EMA weights
of the flow model.
H.2. Fine-Grained Controllable Generation
MotionLCM. We adapt MotionLCM [8] using a pretrained
robot-motion VAE and latent consistency prior. Only the at-
tribute encoder and a ControlNet branch copied from the de-
noiser are trained; the pretrained components remain frozen.
The controller encodes masked attribute targets and selec-
tion masks, then injects control features into the denoiser.
Training combines clean-latent MSE with Smooth-ℓ1 error
between decoded WGAR attributes and targets, normalized
by training attribute scales and averaged over selected chan-
nels and valid frames. Angular differences are wrapped
to [−π, π]. Gradients pass through the frozen decoder to
train the controller.
We train for 10,000 updates with a global batch size
of 192, using AdamW with a learning rate of 10−4 and
zero weight decay. The learning rate uses a 1,000-update
warmup followed by cosine decay. Gradient norms are
19


clipped at 1, and text conditioning is dropped with prob-
ability 0.1. Inference uses four consistency steps followed
by one decoding pass.
OmniControl. We initialize OmniControl [49] from the
robot-adapted MDM and jointly fine-tune its backbone and
control branch. Analytic guidance differentiates WGAR
target errors through G1 forward kinematics, using training-
scale normalization and wrapped angular differences on se-
lected channels and valid frames. The denoising loss is
RPR39 MSE against the clean training motion over valid
frames.
During training, guidance uses 20 updates per diffusion
level and 100 at the final level. We fine-tune for 10,000
updates with a global batch size of 192, using AdamW with
a learning rate of 10−5 and zero weight decay. In the main
comparison, inference uses 50 diffusion steps with one guid-
ance update at every step, including the final step.
MaskControl. We adapt MaskControl [35] using the pre-
trained MoMask tokenizer and token model. A control Trans-
former copied from the token model injects control features
through zero-initialized projections. Its 63-dimensional in-
put contains 21 masked attribute targets, 21 target–current
attribute differences, and 21 selection-mask entries.
Training weights token cross-entropy and the attribute-
control loss equally. The cross-entropy term supervises
ground-truth motion tokens at valid positions. The attribute
term averages absolute WGAR target errors over selected
channels and valid frames, after training-scale normalization
and wrapping angular differences to [−π, π]. To backprop-
agate this error, we decode probability-weighted token em-
beddings through the frozen tokenizer. We train for 15,000
updates with a global batch size of 896, using AdamW with
a learning rate of 2 × 10−4 and weight decay of 10−5.
Inference uses 18 token-unmasking steps. At the des-
ignated refinement stage, we perform five updates to the
token logits to reduce attribute error, keeping model weights
fixed. These updates use AdamW with a learning rate of
0.06, betas (0.5, 0.9), and weight decay of 10−6.
SFControl.
SFControl [17] uses two temporal U-Nets
with adaptive group normalization. The first completes 21-
channel attribute trajectories from sparse conditions. The
second takes these completed trajectories as conditions and
predicts a 60-channel state comprising 21 attributes and 39
motion coordinates. Both stages use clean-state MSE. The
second also minimizes squared WGAR target errors com-
puted from the predicted motion, normalized by training at-
tribute scales and averaged over selected channels and valid
frames, with wrapped angular differences. This loss weight
increases to 1 over the first 1,000 updates. We train each
stage for 20,000 updates, with global batch sizes of 1,536
and 320, respectively. Both use AdamW with a learning rate
of 10−4, weight decay of 0.01, gradient-norm clipping at
1, and EMA decay of 0.9999. Inference uses 50 diffusion
steps per stage with a guidance scale of 2.5.
TLControl. TLControl [45] first trains a robot-motion VQ-
VAE with six body-part groups: root, torso, left/right arms,
and left/right legs. A Transformer then predicts motion
tokens from masked attribute trajectories and text. The tok-
enizer minimizes RPR39 reconstruction MSE plus a commit-
ment loss weighted by 10−3. The Transformer minimizes
token cross-entropy plus decoded-motion MSE weighted by
10−3, with the tokenizer frozen. Each stage is trained for
40,000 updates, with global batch sizes of 128 for the tok-
enizer and 64 for the Transformer. Both use AdamW with a
learning rate of 2×10−4, betas (0.9, 0.99), and weight decay
of 0.01. Attribute conditions are dropped with probability
0.1 to include text-only training examples.
At inference, the highest-probability tokens initialize the
latent representation, which is then optimized continuously.
We use at most five L-BFGS iterations with strong-Wolfe
line search, a learning rate of 0.1, and a history size of 200.
The optimization objective is 1,000 times the sum of the
Smooth-ℓ1 error on training-scale-normalized WGAR dif-
ferences, averaged over selected channels and valid frames
with angular differences wrapped to [−π, π] and the G1 foot-
skid loss. Additional evaluations required by line search
are not counted as iterations.
ProjFlow. ProjFlow [46] uses an RPR39 flow prior trained
along the interpolation path Xτ = (1 −τ)ϵ + τXGT. It
predicts the target velocity XGT −ϵ using MSE over valid
entries. We train the prior for 50,000 updates with a global
batch size of 192, using AdamW with a learning rate of
2 × 10−4 and weight decay of 0.01.
Because WGAR depends on nonlinear FK, we numeri-
cally project the predicted clean motion toward the requested
attributes. Each projection computes the selected-attribute
error gradient, rescales it using the G1 chain-based kinematic
metric, and estimates attribute changes along the resulting
direction. A line search then tests four progressively smaller
step sizes. Inference uses 50 flow steps with a guidance
scale of 3, two projection iterations at each non-final step,
and eight at the final step. We retain the method’s endpoint
recomposition and noise-mixing procedure.

DNO. DNO [24] freezes the RPR39 MDM and optimizes

and 500 noise-fitting updates against valid-frame source-

RG-DNO. RG-DNO [30] uses the RPR39 MDM with 50-

frames, and fits and adapts that reference for 100 updates
each. It evaluates 255 eight-segment noise-mixture candi-

rewards. The selected mixture receives 100 soft-mask up-
dates followed by 400 final noise updates. Initial stages
use learning rate 0.05 and final refinement uses 0.02. Re-
trieval uses training motions of sufficient valid length. The
final optimized noise produces the edited motion through
the diffusion sampler.
I. Limitations
AGLR is trained and evaluated on motion sequences of up to
500 frames at 50 Hz, or approximately 10 seconds. Editing
minute-long motions has not yet been evaluated. Because
the adapter processes the entire target sequence at once,
its cached single-pass refinement does not directly support
streaming edits. Extending the method to longer motions
will require longer temporal context or a windowed editing
strategy that preserves continuity across window boundaries.
